The best definition of funk I've come across is from Rickey Vincent’s well-researched 1996 book, *Funk: The Music, The People, and The Rhythm of the One*.

> Funk is that low-down dirty feeling that pops up when a *baad* funk jam gets to the heated part, and you forget about that contrived dance you were trying, and you *get off your ass and jam*.

Vincent’s point is that funk is easier to recognize than to reduce to a strict formula, and I agree. Funk has a strong "you’ll know it when you hear it" quality, and any strict definition will fall short.

Still, I owe you an attempt to describe what you are listening for, and to do so I need to use a few musical concepts you may not be familiar with. Don’t worry if some of the following terms don’t make complete sense at first. Over the next 365 songs, your ears will develop a working definition of funk that no written description could ever provide. In this case, a song really is worth a thousand words.

At its core, funk makes rhythm the main event. The groove is not just an accompaniment for a singer or soloist that fades into the background. Instead the groove *is* the song. Funk shifts the musical emphasis away from lyrics, melody, and harmonic movement, and toward tightly interlocking rhythmic parts. Drums, bass, guitar, keys, horns, and vocals behave like the gears of one running machine. Each part leaves rhythmic space for the others, and the groove emerges from the way they interlock.

In most pop music, your attention is guided by harmony. Chords move away from home and eventually resolve, creating a sense of tension and release. Funk often works differently. Most funk songs are harmonically static, or at least harmonically simple. Instead, your attention is drawn to the rhythm. As we’ll discover throughout the year, many funk songs create tension and release too, but they do it rhythmically rather than harmonically.

With funk, the bass guitar becomes more syncopated and moves forward in the mix. The drums likewise become more complex, built around syncopated patterns, short phrases, and sharp accents. The guitar moves away from chordal strumming and searing rock solos, and becomes a more percussive instrument. The whole band is often arranged around “the One,” the hard landing on the first beat of each measure. Even when funk gets psychedelic, jazzy, or abstract, it tends to keep its core commitment to the rhythmic pocket first and everything else second.

In funk, the human voice is still important, but it plays a different role. Instrumentals are more common than in most popular styles, and even on vocal tracks the singer is often treated as just another instrument. Funk does not demand the kind of vocal performance you hear in soul. Instead, you often get call and response, chants, and short repeated phrases that slowly turn words into sounds. It's not a melody floating above the groove, but a voice that sits inside the pocket and helps drive it.

If funk has a single origin point, it's James Brown in the 1960s. This book aims to do justice to funk's heyday as one of the key commercial genres and cultural forces of the 1970s, while still reminding you that funk is a living genre, spanning the 1960s through today.

Finally I want to mention that funk was a singles-oriented genre. That is why this book is organized around one song per day rather than one album at a time. I will tell you which album a track first appeared on, but I want you to keep the singles mindset in mind. This book follows that tradition with one focused listen each day that opens up a new corner of the funk world.
