More than anything, I hope this is not the end of your funk journey. 

If a specific track grabbed your attention during the year, go listen to the rest of the album it came from. If you discovered an artist, keep going through their catalog. A great trick is to play the album they released right before and right after the one you liked. Those bookends are likely to share the same feel, while showing you where they came from and where they went next.

Music is best heard, not read, so if your time is limited, follow your ear, not your eyes. If you do want more context, here are some books I recommend.

*Funk: The Music, The People, and The Rhythm of the One* by Rickey Vincent was published in 1996, and it is still one of the best histories of funk. A more recent pick is *Funk Is Its Own Reward: From R&B to Hip Hop* by Lloyd Bradley.

If all my doting on Prince caught your attention, here are a few good next reads:

*Got to Be Something Here: The Rise of the Minneapolis Sound* by Andrea Swensson
*Prince: All the Songs: The Story Behind Every Track* by Benoit Clerc
*On Time: A Princely Life in Funk* by Morris Day
*My Life in the Purple Kingdom* by BrownMark

If you want to see how this music looked in record stores, and get a feel for the designers, illustrators, and photographers behind the visual language, F*unk & Soul Covers: 45th Ed.* by Jaaquim Paulo is a treat. The title is a meta-joke, there are not 45 editions. Funk and soul were built around the 45 RPM 7-inch, while the book celebrates the era’s LP cover art.

For a funk-adjacent change of pace, Prince’s longtime recording engineer Susan Rogers has a second career as a neuroscientist. Her book *This Is What It Sounds Like: A Legendary Producer Turned Neuroscientist on Finding Yourself Through Music* is a fascinating look at what is happening in our brains when we really connect with a song, and why others can feel unbearable.

Lastly, please remember that it's not just the artists, labels, and streaming services that keep the music industry alive. Long before suggestion algorithms, there was a physical place to discover music: record stores. Support your local record store. Browse the used bins, where they make most of their money, and grab anything from the R&B section that looks interesting from 1969 to 1977. You almost can't go wrong!
