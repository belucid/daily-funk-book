John Robert Smith was born in Louisville, Kentucky, on December 16, 1933. His "Johnny Hammond" nickname came from his command of the Hammond B-3 organ, and it helped distinguish him from jazz guitarist Johnny Smith. Hammond had already moved from bop into soul jazz, but by 1975 he needed another shift in sound.

Today’s song, "Tell Me What to Do," starts with deceptively polite piano chords and cymbal strikes, then drops into a lean guitar riff and kick drum. The real groove does not arrive until about 40 seconds in, when the stuttering drums and bass lock into place and build the bedrock for five minutes of jazzy solos and chanted lyrics.

*Gears* is an album by jazz-funk royalty, but the royalty is not Johnny Hammond. In this era, when an older jazz artist wanted to update his sound, the Mizell brothers were the first call producers. Larry and Fonce Mizell wrote the songs, hired the band, and stamped their jazz-funk signature across the record. It is hard not to hear the line “tell me what to do” as Hammond asking his new producers for direction. In the process, they made *Gears* one of the most beloved jazz-funk LPs of the decade.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The opening that sneaks up on you."** The track starts with deceptively polite chords, then drops into a laid-back groove anchored by a looped guitar riff. It's a perfect "side one, track one" thesis statement for what the Mizells were doing: front-load the jazz manners, then let the funk take over. This angle gives the author a clean way to teach what Sky High Productions actually sounded like.

2. **"When the producers eclipse the leader."** _Gears_ is named in nearly every breath as "a Mizell Brothers album that happens to feature Johnny Hammond." The brothers wrote five of six tracks, hired the band, and stamped their sound across the record. Hammond, an old-school B-3 bopper from Louisville, became the vehicle for someone else's vision, and made one of the most beloved jazz-funk LPs of the era doing it. A theme about producers as authors.

3. **"The riff that wouldn't stay buried."** "Tell Me What To Do" sat as an album opener until DJs and producers started flipping it: Armand Van Helden, Mr. Scruff, Fam-Lee, and a long tail of hip-hop and electronic acts mined the same loop. A theme about how a jazz-funk groove gets a second life through the sample.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. About 35 seconds in, the band drops out of the opening chord vamp and lands on a single looped guitar riff that anchors the rest of the song. Listen for that pivot, then ask yourself: is this a Johnny Hammond record, or is it a Mizell Brothers record with Hammond on top?

2. Count the lead voices: B-3 organ, electric piano, flute, vibes, violin, even a clarinet from Fonce Mizell. Pick one you didn't expect on a jazz-funk record and follow it for thirty seconds. What's it doing in the arrangement that a horn section couldn't?

3. The looped guitar riff that hooks DJs from Mr. Scruff to Armand Van Helden is hiding in plain sight. Find the four-bar phrase you'd loop if you were a sampler in 1997, and see if it changes how the rest of the track sounds to you.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "Tell Me What To Do" is the album opener and clocks at 5:16; it was composed by the Mizell brothers, who wrote five of the six tracks on _Gears_. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gears_(album)
- The track's structure was described in one reissue note as starting "innocuously enough — with fairly straightforward chords. Moments later, however, the band kicks into a laid-back groove, jamming over a catchy, looped guitar riff." — Source: https://jazzdispensary.com/news/johnny-hammond-gears-returns-to-vinyl/
- The looped guitar riff has been sampled repeatedly in dance and hip-hop. Confirmed flips include Armand Van Helden's "This Is It!" (1997, on _Sampleslaya: Enter the Meatmarket_), Mr. Scruff's "Rocking Chair," Fam-Lee's "Runs in the Fam-Lee," and tracks by Devon Hendryx, Shabaam Sahdeeq, Makoto, and Monstro Robot. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Johnny-Hammond/Tell-Me-What-to-Do/sampled/
- The personnel reads like a jazz-funk dream team: Harvey Mason (drums), Chuck Rainey (bass), Jerry Peters (electric piano), Roger Glenn (flute, vibes), Hadley Caliman (tenor sax), Julian Priester (trombone), Michael White (violin), Kenneth Nash (percussion), with Craig McMullen and John Rowin on guitars and backing vocals from Larry and Fonce Mizell. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gears_(album)

#### Album

- _Gears_ was Hammond's first record for Milestone Records after leaving Kudu/CTI; it was recorded in July 1975 at Fantasy Studios in Berkeley, California, with engineering by Eddie Bill Harris and remixing in September 1975. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gears_(album) ; https://flabbergasted-vibes.org/2024/01/28/johnny-hammond-gears-1975-jazz-dispensary-craft-recordings-2022/
- The album peaked at No. 31 on Billboard's Jazz Albums chart; AllMusic gives it 4 of 5 stars. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gears_(album)
- The single "Los Conquistadores Chocolatés" crossed over to land in the Top 5 of Billboard's Dance Club Songs and Disco Singles charts. — Source: https://johnnyhammondsmith.bandcamp.com/album/gears
- Closer "Can't We Smile" was later sampled by both Erykah Badu and Kendrick Lamar, while "Shifting Gears" turns up in more than 20 tracks including DMX and Stetsasonic. — Source: https://jazzdispensary.com/news/johnny-hammond-gears-returns-to-vinyl/
- The Mizell production method on the album: rehearse the rhythm section, cut a full pass with Hammond, then layer additional instrumentation and vocals on top. — Source: https://acerecords.co.uk/gears
- _Gears_ was reissued on November 4, 2022 as part of the Jazz Dispensary Top Shelf Series, all-analog mastered by Kevin Gray, limited to 2,000 copies on 180-gram vinyl. — Source: https://jazzdispensary.com/news/johnny-hammond-gears-returns-to-vinyl/

#### Artist

- John Robert Smith was born December 16, 1933 in Louisville, Kentucky and died of cancer June 4, 1997 in Victorville, California, aged 63. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_%22Hammond%22_Smith
- He started piano at age five and only switched to organ after hearing Wild Bill Davis; the "Hammond" nickname came from his command of the B-3 and also kept fans from confusing him with jazz guitarist Johnny Smith. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_%22Hammond%22_Smith ; https://wbssmedia.com/artists/detail/3158
- He spent roughly a decade on Prestige Records in the 1960s, then signed with Creed Taylor's Kudu/CTI imprint in 1971; his debut for Taylor, _Breakout_, was the inaugural Kudu LP. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_%22Hammond%22_Smith
- Grover Washington Jr. played on Hammond's CTI sessions before launching his own solo career. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_%22Hammond%22_Smith
- Hammond taught at Cal Poly Pomona starting January 1987 and wrote "Quiet Fire" for Nancy Wilson's 1988/89 album _Nancy Now!_ — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_%22Hammond%22_Smith
- His final live performance was at the DuSable Museum of African American History in Chicago in May 1997, weeks before his death. — Source: https://fromthevaults-boppinbob.blogspot.com/2017/12/johnny-hammond-smith-born-16-december.html

### Genre Suggestions

Current value: **Jazz-Funk**.

1. **Jazz-Funk (keep)** — The strongest fit. Hammond is a career jazz organist; the track is built around extended grooves, instrumental solo space, and a 5:16 runtime. AllMusic lists the album under "Jazz-Funk, Soul Jazz." — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/gears-mw0000090769

2. **Soul Jazz (alternate)** — Defensible. Hammond's lineage runs through 1960s soul jazz, and his B-3 voicings are still the harmonic anchor. The book defines soul jazz as "the early simmer," but Hammond is a soul-jazz player even when the band around him is doing something funkier. — Source: On Genres & Regions.md

3. **Classic Funk (weaker fit)** — Possible only if you read the Mizell production as commercial-pipeline funk rather than jazz fusion. The book's classic-funk window (1969–1977) covers the year, but the track's identity is "funk fused into jazz," which the book explicitly assigns to jazz-funk.

Recommendation: keep **Jazz-Funk**.
