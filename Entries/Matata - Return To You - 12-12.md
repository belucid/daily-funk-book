Kenya gained its independence from Britain on December 12, 1963, at Uhuru Gardens in Nairobi. “Uhuru” is Swahili for “freedom,” and it became a rallying cry of the independence movement. Keep an ear out for it in African funk of the late 1960s and 1970s. You will spot it everywhere: in lyrics, song titles, band names, and even musicians’ first names. Uhuru Kenyatta, born during the independence era, later became Kenya’s fourth president from 2013 to 2022.

Today’s song, “Return to You,” by the Kenyan funk band Matata, was the lead track and single from their 1974 album *Independence*. The album title was no coincidence. Many band members came of age in Nairobi after 1963, and by 1974 they were reinventing themselves, declaring independence from their earlier sound.

Prior Matata recordings were sung in multiple African languages and did not feature a J.B.’s-style horn attack. That changed after the band was exposed to James Brown and American funk during trips to London to perform and record. On *Independence*, they sing only in English, and they bring in South African horn players to help capture the sound they had fallen in love with.

#### Basic background

Kenya formally became independent from Britain at midnight on 12 December 1963, at Uhuru Gardens in Nairobi's Lang'ata neighbourhood, where the Union Jack was lowered and the new Kenyan flag was raised for the first time. The word _uhuru_ is Swahili for "freedom" and had been the rallying cry of the independence movement, particularly of the Kenya African National Union (KANU), whose party banner became the basis for the national flag. Jomo Kenyatta, who had been jailed in 1953 at the Kapenguria trial for alleged leadership of the Mau Mau uprising and only released in 1961, was sworn in as the country's first prime minister. Independence followed a staged handover: internal self-rule had been granted on 1 June 1963 (marked today as Madaraka Day), and exactly one year after Uhuru, on 12 December 1964, Kenya became a republic with Kenyatta sworn in as its first president. That December 12 anniversary is now celebrated as Jamhuri Day, from the Swahili word for "republic." Sources: [Wikipedia: Jamhuri Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamhuri_Day), [Wikipedia: Uhuru Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uhuru_Gardens), [Wikipedia: Jomo Kenyatta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jomo_Kenyatta), [History.com: Kenya declares independence](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/december-12/kenya-declares-independence-from-britain).

#### Four flavor facts

1. **Prince Philip's in-ceremony joke.** The Queen dispatched Prince Philip to Nairobi to hand over power. When the halyard twisted during the flag-raising and the new Kenyan flag briefly refused to unfurl, Philip leaned over to Kenyatta and said, "You've still got time to change your mind if you wish." Seconds later the flag caught the floodlights cleanly. Source: [Belfast Media: Prince Philip's legacy in Kenya](https://belfastmedia.com/prince-philip-s-legacy-in-kenya-leaves-many-questions).
2. **Kenyatta went from convict to head of government in under three years.** He was in British custody from 1953 to 1961 (first at Lokitaung prison, then in internal exile at Lodwar), was freed in August 1961, became KANU party president, swept the 1963 election, and was prime minister at independence on 12 December 1963. A convicted Mau Mau "leader" was now raising the flag that replaced the one his jailers had defended. Source: [Wikipedia: Jomo Kenyatta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jomo_Kenyatta).
3. **The flag itself is a compromise document.** Black stands for the African people, red for the blood spilled fighting for independence, green for the land, and the white fimbriations (added later) for peace. The central Maasai shield and crossed spears were included as a cross-party national emblem, keeping KANU's tricolor while adding a symbol that wasn't the exclusive property of any single political faction. Source: [Wikipedia: Flag of Kenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Kenya).
4. **The word that named a president.** _Uhuru_ was so central to the moment that newborns across Kenya were given the name, including the son of a Kenyan politician named Jomo Kenyatta. That baby, Uhuru Kenyatta (born 26 October 1961, about two years before independence), later became Kenya's fourth president and served from 2013 to 2022, literally embodying the word shouted into the Nairobi night in 1963. Source: [Wikipedia: Uhuru Kenyatta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uhuru_Kenyatta), [Dictionary.com: Uhuru](https://www.dictionary.com/browse/uhuru).
5. _(Bonus, in case a fifth helps with pacing.)_ **Uhuru Gardens was ordinary ground made sacred by one night.** The 68-acre site wasn't a stadium or government complex, it was a park on Lang'ata Road near Wilson Airport. It was declared a National Monument in 1966, and in 2021 Uhuru Kenyatta himself reopened it on Jamhuri Day as a full national museum, 58 years to the day after his namesake moment. Source: [Wikipedia: Uhuru Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uhuru_Gardens).

### Writing Theme Suggestions

The entry's `writing theme` field is empty. Three options that the research opens up:

1. **"Independence, twice over."** The album title wasn't chosen casually. Most of the band came out of Nairobi in the decade after Kenya's 1963 independence from Britain, and by 1974 the band had also pulled free of their debut's multilingual Afro-rock template to stand on a single-language, American-style funk footing. "Return To You" opens the album and sets that second kind of declaration. (Sources: [President Records](https://president-records.co.uk/artist/matata/), [We Are Cult](https://wearecult.rocks/matata-wanna-do-my-thing-the-complete-president-recordings-reviewed), [History.com: Kenya declares independence](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/december-12/kenya-declares-independence-from-britain))
2. **"The James Brown pilgrimage."** By most accounts the band returned from their first London trip to Kenya, encountered James Brown's funk in a new way on home soil, then reconvened in London with a radically tighter, JB-flavored approach. "Return To You" is the opening salvo of that shift: Anwar Richard's organ, the bongo-forward pocket, and the JB-style shouts all point at Brown. Useful if you want the entry to center on how an African band reinterpreted a Black American idiom that was itself rooted in African rhythms. (Sources: [We Are Cult review](https://wearecult.rocks/matata-wanna-do-my-thing-the-complete-president-recordings-reviewed), [Cherry Red overview](https://www.cherryred.co.uk/wanna-do-my-thing-the-complete-president-recordings), [The Recoup](https://therecoup.com/2017/04/25/matata-wanna-do-my-thing-the-complete-president-recordings-rpm-records/))
3. **"South African exiles on a Kenyan groove."** The _Independence_ horn section was stacked with Blue Notes refugees Dudu Pukwana and Mongezi Feza, plus Guyanese tenor Colin Dyall, inside a Kenyan-Congolese rhythm section. A pan-African-plus-diaspora blend on one track opens a bigger story about London as a Black musical crossroads in the early 1970s. (Sources: [Wikipedia: Dudu Pukwana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dudu_Pukwana), [Wikipedia: Mongezi Feza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongezi_Feza), [A Dudu Music blog](https://www.adudumusic.com/2007/11/matata-and-pure-funk-era.html))

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "Return To You" is the album's opening track and was one of only two singles President pulled from _Independence_, the other being "I Feel Funky." — Source: [Cherry Red: Wanna Do My Thing - The Complete President Recordings](https://www.cherryred.co.uk/wanna-do-my-thing-the-complete-president-recordings)
- It was produced by Joe Mogotsi, the South African singer (of the Manhattan Brothers) who was married to the band's manager Pearl Connor. Ram John Holder co-produced the _Independence_ sessions. — Source: [President Records: Matata](https://president-records.co.uk/artist/matata/)
- The UK 7-inch paired "Return To You" as the A-side with "Something In Mind" on the B-side, released on President (solid centre) in 1974. A promo pressing with a push-out centre was also issued. — Source: [Discogs: Matata – Return To You / Something In Mind](https://www.discogs.com/release/5063642-Matata-Return-To-You-Something-In-Mind) and [Discogs: Matata – Return To You (Promo)](https://www.discogs.com/release/9577542-Matata-Return-To-You)
- The Afrofunk Forum blog and the Music Republic magazine coverage attribute the "authentic jungle beat" on "Return To You" specifically to bongo player Patrice Oluma. — Source: [Funk My Soul: Matata - 1974 - Independence](https://www.funkmysoul.gr/matata-1974-independence/), [A Dudu Music](https://www.adudumusic.com/2007/11/matata-and-pure-funk-era.html)
- The track runs 3:30 on the 1994 _Feelin' Funky_ CD reissue (President PCOM 1134). Funk My Soul lists the 1974 LP timing as 3:31; the one-second difference is consistent with CD mastering variation. — Source: [MusicBrainz: Feelin' Funky](https://musicbrainz.org/release/13285fa0-dd57-4522-94d5-21897df6408a), [Deezer: Matata - Feelin' Funky](https://www.deezer.com/en/album/1042493)

#### Album

- _Independence_ is the band's second and final studio LP, released 1974 on President Records. Every track is in English, a deliberate shift from the 1972 debut _Air Fiesta_, which rotated through Mijikenda, Lingala, Swahili and English. — Source: [President Records: Matata](https://president-records.co.uk/artist/matata/), [Cherry Red](https://www.cherryred.co.uk/wanna-do-my-thing-the-complete-president-recordings)
- Anwar Richard, the band's organist and lead vocalist, wrote every song on the album except "Love Is The Only Way." — Source: [Cherry Red](https://www.cherryred.co.uk/wanna-do-my-thing-the-complete-president-recordings) and [A Dudu Music](https://www.adudumusic.com/2007/11/matata-and-pure-funk-era.html)
- The horn section for the album is a pan-African supergroup: South African Blue Notes exiles Dudu Pukwana (alto sax) and Mongezi Feza (trumpet), plus Ghanaian-highlife-trained Sammy Obot (trumpet) and Guyanese Colin Dyall (tenor sax). — Source: [Funk My Soul](https://www.funkmysoul.gr/matata-1974-independence/), [A Dudu Music](https://www.adudumusic.com/2007/11/matata-and-pure-funk-era.html), [Wikipedia: Dudu Pukwana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dudu_Pukwana), [Wikipedia: Mongezi Feza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongezi_Feza)
- Two LP pressings exist with a small tracklist difference. Most sources (the Cherry Red compilation, Tidal Waves reissue, Ear Candy Music) show track A4 as "I Believed Her"; the Funk My Soul post shows A4 as "Wanna Do My Thing." The Cherry Red / MusicBrainz / Discogs lineage is the more authoritative source — "I Believed Her" is correct for _Independence_; "Wanna Do My Thing" is from the 1972 debut. — Source: [Cherry Red](https://www.cherryred.co.uk/wanna-do-my-thing-the-complete-president-recordings), [MusicBrainz](https://musicbrainz.org/release/13285fa0-dd57-4522-94d5-21897df6408a), [Ear Candy Music](https://earcandymusic.biz/matata-independence-lp-vinyl/)
- Reissue history: President Records remastered the catalog as two CDs in 1994 (_Wild River_ and _Feelin' Funky_); RPM / Cherry Red issued the 2-CD _Wanna Do My Thing: The Complete President Recordings_ in 2017 with sleeve notes by John Masouri based on interviews with Anwar Richard; Tidal Waves Music pressed a limited (1,000 copy) 180g vinyl reissue of _Independence_ for RSD Black Friday on 26 November 2021. — Source: [President Records](https://president-records.co.uk/artist/matata/), [Cherry Red](https://www.cherryred.co.uk/wanna-do-my-thing-the-complete-president-recordings), [Plaid Room Records: Matata - Independence](https://www.plaidroomrecords.com/collections/bf2021/products/matata-independence), [Record Store Day listing](https://recordstoreday.com/SpecialRelease/14567)

#### Artist

- "Matata" means "trouble" or "worries" in Swahili. The manager reportedly pushed a spin that it meant "fire." — Source: [President Records: Matata](https://president-records.co.uk/artist/matata/)
- The band's roots trace to Congolese refugees playing alongside Kenyan musicians at Nairobi's Starlight Club, run by Robbie Armstrong; the venue drew around 12,000 patrons a month at its peak. — Source: [Wikipedia: Matata (band)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matata_(band)), [The Afrofunk Music Forum](http://afrofunkforum.blogspot.com/2006/11/where-is-matata.html)
- Founder **John Nzenze** (born 1940, Nairobi; died 30 May 2020) is often credited as the originator of Air Fiesta Matata. He was an early Kenyan twist pioneer and received a Head of State Commendation from President Mwai Kibaki in 2009. — Source: [BBC: John Nzenze - the founder of 'Africa's best band'](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-africa-52978770), [Wikipedia: John Nzenze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Nzenze)
- Matata ranked third at the 1968 All-African Music Festival in Algeria, performed for Emperor Haile Selassie in Ethiopia in 1969, and won the BBC World Service's "Best Band in Africa" contest in 1971 — the prize that brought them to London. — Source: [Wikipedia: Matata (band)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matata_(band)), [President Records](https://president-records.co.uk/artist/matata/)
- Their manager in the UK was **Pearl Connor-Mogotsi** (1924–2005), the Trinidad-born theatrical agent who pioneered Black representation in British entertainment. She was the only African act's advocate on the President roster, and she booked them to open for Miles Davis in Cologne. — Source: [Wikipedia: Pearl Connor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_Connor), [President Records](https://president-records.co.uk/artist/matata/)
- They held club residencies at London's Marquee, Tiles, Gullivers, the Flamingo and the 100 Club, plus stints in Norway, Switzerland, Hong Kong and Germany. — Source: [President Records](https://president-records.co.uk/artist/matata/)
- Lead vocalist / organist **Anwar Richard** came from Mombasa; he's described as the son of a police commissioner who walked into the Starlight Club, volunteered to sing, and was hired on the spot. He wrote most of _Independence_. After Matata broke up he formed Sawar, later joining the Chosen Few. — Source: [Cherry Red](https://www.cherryred.co.uk/wanna-do-my-thing-the-complete-president-recordings), [President Records](https://president-records.co.uk/artist/matata/)
- The group disbanded after 1974; bongo player Patrice Oluma revived the Matata name in Munich in 1983 and recorded "Mbongo" and "Ulimwengu" in 1989 before his death in the late 1990s. — Source: [President Records](https://president-records.co.uk/artist/matata/), [The Afrofunk Music Forum](http://afrofunkforum.blogspot.com/2006/11/where-is-matata.html)

### Genre Suggestions

Per the book's `On Genres & Regions.md` definitions:

1. **Afrobeat** — The book defines Afrobeat broadly as "funk rooted in Africa," explicitly noting the term covers what could also be called Afrofunk. Matata is a Nairobi-founded band and most sources (Cherry Red, AllMusic, The Afrofunk Forum, President Records) shelve them as Afrofunk / Afrobeat. Even though the band was based in London when they cut _Independence_, the rhythmic DNA (bongos, congas, the "authentic jungle beat" attributed to Oluma) sits in the African funk tradition. This is my top recommendation. — Source: [Cherry Red](https://www.cherryred.co.uk/wanna-do-my-thing-the-complete-president-recordings), [President Records](https://president-records.co.uk/artist/matata/), [The Afrofunk Music Forum](http://afrofunkforum.blogspot.com/2006/11/where-is-matata.html)
2. **Classic Funk** — A defensible alternative. Contemporary reviewers (The Recoup, We Are Cult) hear the _Independence_ shift as a straight-ahead James Brown move, to the point where one reviewer calls the album "a second-rate James Brown knock-off." Label-backed, English-language, 1974, inside the book's 1968–1977 classic-funk window. The drawback: the book's Classic Funk definition emphasizes tracks whose "primary identity is funk, not funk fused into another genre," and the bongo pocket here fuses this with Afro rhythms. — Source: [The Recoup](https://therecoup.com/2017/04/25/matata-wanna-do-my-thing-the-complete-president-recordings-rpm-records/), [We Are Cult](https://wearecult.rocks/matata-wanna-do-my-thing-the-complete-president-recordings-reviewed)
3. **Deep Funk** — Also defensible. The book defines deep funk as "a commercial fate … overlooked on release and only found their audience later through crate-digging." _Independence_ underperformed commercially, the band dissolved by 1974, and the record only surfaced in wider consciousness through the 1990s President reissues, Dusty Groove rare-groove culture, and the 2017 and 2021 reissue cycle. This is a strong second choice. — Source: [A Dudu Music](https://www.adudumusic.com/2007/11/matata-and-pure-funk-era.html), [Dusty Groove](https://www.dustygroove.com/item/357300/Matata:Feelin-Funky)

**Recommendation:** Afrobeat (primary), with Deep Funk as a viable backup if the author wants to leverage the crate-digger rediscovery story.
