### Open Research Questions

The author left no `open:` items in this entry. Three questions surfaced during research that the author may want to know are unresolved:

1. **Album chart peak conflict.** The Wikipedia article on *Hutson II* says the album reached **#46** on the US R&B albums chart (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutson_II), while the Wikipedia article on Leroy Hutson lists *Hutson II* at **#26** Black Albums and gives **#46** to the earlier *Hutson* (1975) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_Hutson). One of the two is almost certainly a transcription error. I attempted to settle it against MusicVF (https://www.musicvf.com/Leroy+Hutson.songs) and AllMusic (https://www.allmusic.com/album/hutson-ii-mw0000879492); both return HTTP 403 / Cloudflare interstitials to automated fetches. A fact-checker with a print copy of Joel Whitburn's *Top R&B Albums* should resolve it in one lookup. The single's peak (**#82** R&B) is consistent across sources.

2. **Exact single release date.** Discogs dates the promo 7" (Curtom CMS 0124, mono/stereo, both sides "Blackberry Jam") to **April 1977** and the commercial 7" (b/w "I Think I'm Falling In Love") to **May 1977**. No day-level date was found. 45cat's page for CMS 0124 (https://www.45cat.com/record/cms0124) would likely carry a *Billboard* review date, but it returns HTTP 403 to automated fetching. A *Billboard* "New Singles" or "Top Single Picks" column from April–May 1977 would give the exact week.

3. **Sampling history.** No confirmed hip-hop or house sample of "Blackberry Jam" specifically could be verified. WhoSampled's artist page confirms Hutson has been sampled widely (Nas, Notorious B.I.G., Erykah Badu, Pete Rock, UGK, Bone Thugs-N-Harmony, Too $hort, T.I., Wiz Khalifa, Young Jeezy, Memphis Bleek, Paul Wall), but the per-track page at https://www.whosampled.com/Leroy-Hutson/Blackberry-Jam/ returns HTTP 403 to automated fetching, so I could not confirm whether any of those cites this track. Treat "Blackberry Jam has been sampled" as unverified until someone checks that page in a browser.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"The instrumental hiding on the ballad man's album."** Hutson's public identity was the velvet-voiced Curtom romantic — the man who sang "Lucky Fellow" and "All Because of You." "Blackberry Jam" is the one track on *Hutson II* where he drops the voice almost entirely and lets a clavinet, an ARP Odyssey and a horn section do the talking. The theme is about what a singer says when he stops singing.

- **"Best-kept secret of Seventies soul."** *Rolling Stone*'s phrase for Hutson (quoted at https://www.roughtrade.com/en-gb/artist/leroy-hutson and https://soultracks.com/birthday-leroy-hutson/) is a gift of a hook: a man who replaced Curtis Mayfield in The Impressions, co-wrote "The Ghetto" with his college roommate Donny Hathaway, and still ended up as a name only diggers know. "Blackberry Jam" peaking at #82 R&B is the statistic that proves the thesis.

- **"78 seconds of funk on the cutting-room floor."** The album cut runs 4:50; both the promo and the commercial 45 run 3:32. Something got taken out to make this radio-legal, and it charted at #82 anyway. A short entry on what the singles era did to grooves that were built to stretch.

- **"Two roommates at Howard."** Hutson and Hathaway shared a dorm room and wrote "The Ghetto" together; Hathaway left to become Curtis Mayfield's musical director, which is the thread that eventually pulled Hutson into The Impressions and then Curtom. One track, three careers, one hallway.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- Around the halfway mark a synthesizer — an ARP Odyssey, played by James Hirsen, credited on Discogs specifically to this track — starts answering the horn line. Decide whether it's playing a solo or playing a fourth horn part. (Source for the credit: https://www.discogs.com/release/1216665-Leroy-Hutson-Hutson-II)

- Hutson sings almost nothing here. Count how many words you actually hear, then ask why a man who built his reputation on his voice made this the funkiest thing on his own album.

- The 45 edit is 78 seconds shorter than the album version. Play the album cut and try to guess where the scissors went — is the missing minute a solo, a vamp, or the intro?

- Put "Blackberry Jam" next to "Love the Feeling," the sweeping ballad that opens the same record. Same singer, same week, same studio. What survives across both?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "Blackberry Jam" was issued twice as a 7": first as a Curtom promo (CMS 0124) with a mono side and a stereo side, both the same 3:32 edit, in April 1977; then as a commercial single b/w "I Think I'm Falling In Love" in May 1977 — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/5148451-Leroy-Hutson-Blackberry-Jam and https://www.discogs.com/release/3142242-Leroy-Hutson-Blackberry-Jam-I-Think-Im-Falling-In-Love
- The single edit cuts 78 seconds out of the 4:50 album version (3:32 vs 4:50) — Source: https://www.discogs.com/master/625424-Leroy-Hutson-Blackberry-Jam-I-Think-Im-Falling-In-Love and https://www.discogs.com/master/191858-Leroy-Hutson-Hutson-II
- It peaked at **#82** on *Billboard*'s R&B singles chart, entering in May 1977 — the lowest-charting of the two singles pulled from *Hutson II* ("I Do, I Do (Want to Make Love to You)" reached #55) — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutson_II
- Hutson wrote, arranged and produced the track himself and played clavinet on it — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutson_II and http://funkatropolis.blogspot.com/2018/08/blackberry-jam-by-leroy-hutson.html
- Discogs' track-level credits single out two players for side B track 2 (this song): **Cordell Carter** on drums and **James Hirsen** on ARP Odyssey — the synth is credited to this track and one other only — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/1216665-Leroy-Hutson-Hutson-II
- The publishing is split between Silent Giant Publishing Co. and Aopa Publishing Co. (ASCAP) — the two Curtom-affiliated publishers — per the 45 label copy — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/3142242-Leroy-Hutson-Blackberry-Jam-I-Think-Im-Falling-In-Love
- Montreal's long-running funk radio show WEFUNK played it on Show 400 — a marker of its standing as a DJ record rather than a hit record — Source: http://www.wefunkradio.com/song/play/400_ax/leroy+hutson/blackberry+jam
- A crate-digger's description that captures the arrangement well: "pure uncut funk with all the groove essentials: tight horns, poppin' bass, hot beat, nasty guitar licks and funky clavinet. And some badass synth work further deepens the funk." — Source: http://funkatropolis.blogspot.com/2018/08/blackberry-jam-by-leroy-hutson.html
- AllMusic's *Hutson II* review is cooler on it, calling the track "a formulaic but pleasing funker" — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/hutson-ii-mw0000879492 (quote surfaced via search indexing; the page itself blocks automated fetch)

#### Album

- *Hutson II* was Hutson's fifth solo album, released **November 1976** on Curtom, recorded during 1976 at Curtom Studios in Chicago — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutson_II
- Nine tracks in roughly 31 minutes; "Blackberry Jam" at 4:50 is the second-longest thing on it — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutson_II and https://www.discogs.com/master/191858-Leroy-Hutson-Hutson-II
- The band is a Curtom house-band roll call: Phil Upchurch on guitar (A4), Henry Gibson on congas, Reggie Gillerson on bass, Jerry Wilson on tenor sax, Bill McFarland on trombone, Michael Harris on trumpet, with string and horn arrangements by James Mack and Rich Tufo — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/1216665-Leroy-Hutson-Hutson-II
- First pressings are identifiable by embossed text on the upper front of the glossy sleeve and no barcode — a useful detail for anyone buying an original — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/1216665-Leroy-Hutson-Hutson-II
- The record sits at the end of what fans call Hutson's classic trilogy, between *Feel the Spirit* (Feb 1976) and *Closer to the Source* (Feb 1978) — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutson_II
- Acid Jazz Records reissued *Hutson* and *Hutson II* on vinyl, CD and digital on **23 February 2018**, the first vinyl issue in over twenty years, with alternate versions and edits — Source: https://www.thevinylfactory.com/news/soul-crooner-leroy-hutsons-hutson-hutson-ii-lps-reissued
- **Production credit discrepancy:** Wikipedia and Discogs both credit Hutson himself as producer, with Rich Tufo credited only for string and horn arrangements. The Vinyl Factory's reissue write-up says the albums were "produced by Rich Tufo." Go with the label copy (Hutson) and flag Tufo as arranger — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutson_II, https://www.discogs.com/release/1216665-Leroy-Hutson-Hutson-II vs https://www.thevinylfactory.com/news/soul-crooner-leroy-hutsons-hutson-hutson-ii-lps-reissued

#### Artist

- Leroy Hutson was born **4 June 1945** in Newark, New Jersey, and formed a teenage vocal group called the Nu-Tones — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_Hutson
- He went to Howard University intending to become a **dentist**, and switched his major to music theory and composition after rooming with Donny Hathaway — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_Hutson and https://soultracks.com/birthday-leroy-hutson/
- Hutson and Hathaway co-wrote **"The Ghetto"** while at Howard; it became Hathaway's first hit in early 1970 — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_Hutson
- In 1971, roughly three months after leaving college, he **replaced Curtis Mayfield as lead singer of The Impressions**; his first single with them, "Love Me," came out on Curtom in June 1971. He lasted about two and a half years and two albums — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_Hutson
- *Rolling Stone* called him "the best-kept secret of Seventies soul" — Source: https://www.roughtrade.com/en-gb/artist/leroy-hutson
- As a writer/producer he worked with Roberta Flack, The Natural Four, Linda Clifford and The Voices of East Harlem; his Natural Four production "Can This Be Real" hit #10 R&B in late 1973 — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_Hutson
- Between 1973 and 1992 he recorded eight albums and charted thirteen US singles, staying with Curtom until December 1979 — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_Hutson and https://soultracks.com/birthday-leroy-hutson/
- On **27 August 2013** Hutson filed a copyright complaint against Young Jeezy over unauthorised use of The Impressions' "Getting It On" instrumental — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_Hutson
- He returned to recording in 2008 under the name **Lee Hutson**, and from 2010 toured European festivals backed by The Third Degree. Acid Jazz has licensed his catalogue since 2017, and a documentary series, *Leroy Hutson: The Man!*, appeared in 2018 — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_Hutson
- He is the father of producer JR Hutson — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_Hutson
- No death has been reported; as of this research date he appears to be living — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_Hutson (no death date listed)

### Genre Suggestions

Working from `Matter/On Subgenres.md` and the closed enumeration in `Planning/Field Values.md`.

1. **Classic Funk — recommended.** This is the textbook case. 1976 is inside the book's stated classic era (1969–1977); the record came out on an established label (Curtom) with major-label manufacture and distribution (Warner Bros.); it was pushed as a single with a promo 7" mailed to radio; and it charted, however modestly, at #82 R&B. The track's primary identity is funk — clavinet, horn section, drum-and-bass pocket — not funk fused into jazz, rock or Latin styles. Everything the definition asks for is present. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutson_II, https://www.discogs.com/release/5148451-Leroy-Hutson-Blackberry-Jam

2. **Deep Funk — strong alternative.** The counter-argument is the commercial fate. *Hutson II* sold poorly enough that Hutson spent thirty years as a collectors' name, and "Blackberry Jam" is precisely the kind of album cut that DJs found rather than the public. The evidence is the afterlife: WEFUNK spinning it, Acid Jazz licensing the whole catalogue in 2017 and reissuing the album in 2018 for the first vinyl pressing in twenty years, and *Rolling Stone* calling Hutson "the best-kept secret of Seventies soul." The definition explicitly allows a well-known label if the record sold poorly with limited distribution. The one thing that weakens it: this did chart, and it did get a promo 45, so it was not entirely overlooked on release. Sources: https://www.thevinylfactory.com/news/soul-crooner-leroy-hutsons-hutson-hutson-ii-lps-reissued, https://www.roughtrade.com/en-gb/artist/leroy-hutson

3. **Jazz Funk — weak, listed for completeness.** The track is largely instrumental, gives space to tenor sax and synth, and has a Chicago session-player pedigree including Phil Upchurch on the record. But Hutson is a soul singer, not a jazz musician crossing over, and at 4:50 there is no extended improvisation. It fails the "by the players" test that the book's definition leads with. I would not use it.

### Region Suggestions

The enumeration in `Planning/Field Values.md` offers `US - Illinois`, `US - Northeast`, `US - Midwest` as the plausible candidates.

1. **US - Illinois — recommended, and written to the field.** *Hutson II* was recorded at Curtom Studios in Chicago during 1976, on Curtis Mayfield's Chicago label, with a Chicago session band (Phil Upchurch, Henry Gibson, Bill McFarland, James Mack, Rich Tufo). Whatever Hutson's birthplace, this track is a Chicago soul record made by the Chicago soul establishment. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutson_II, https://www.discogs.com/release/1216665-Leroy-Hutson-Hutson-II
