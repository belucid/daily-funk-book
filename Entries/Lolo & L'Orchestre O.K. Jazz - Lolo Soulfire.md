### Open Research Questions

The author left no `open:` prompts in the file. One research-generated question worth flagging for the author:

- **Title spelling, "Soufire" vs "Soulfire."** The original 1971 Fiesta 51.105 labels the B-side "Lolo Soufire." The Planet Ilunga reissue (2024) preserves that spelling. Analog Africa's 2024 compilation re-titles it "Lolo Soulfire." "Soufire" is not a standard French or Lingala word. The 2013 Les Disques Africains post translates it as "Lolo suffers" (reading it as a phonetic rendering of French "souffre"). Analog Africa's editors appear to have re-read the word as a contraction of "soul fire," a nod to the James Brown template. No primary source confirms which reading the singer intended, and the Wikipedia entry for Lola Checain does not list this track among his notable compositions. This ambiguity is itself a potential writing hook. Sources: http://lesdisquesafricains.blogspot.com/2013/07/franco-lolo-lorchestre-ok-jazz-bon-de.html ; https://planetilunga.bandcamp.com/track/lolo-soufire ; https://analogafrica.bandcamp.com/track/lolo-soulfire ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lola_Checain

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **Kinshasa got James Brown before Kinshasa saw James Brown.** The dominant narrative about Congolese funk dates the funk turn to James Brown's headline set at Zaire 74 in September 1974 (the festival around the Rumble in the Jungle). "Lolo Soulfire" was cut in 1971, three years earlier, and it already sounds more like a JB B-side than a rumba record. This reframes the 1974 concert as the loudest moment in a story that was already underway via radio and imported 45s. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaire_74 ; https://analogafrica.bandcamp.com/album/congo-funk-sound-madness-from-the-shores-of-the-mighty-congo-river-kinshasa-brazzaville-1969-1982-analog-africa-no-38

2. **The B-side that out-James-Browned James Brown.** Bandcamp Daily and Silent Radio both note that Lola Checain "almost out-James Browns the great man himself." Pairing that claim with the story of a ringer vocalist stepping out from behind Franco's orchestra to cut a three-and-a-half-minute funk bomb on a B-side is a great vehicle for a short, punchy entry. Source: https://daily.bandcamp.com/album-of-the-day/various-artists-congo-funk-analog-africa-review ; https://www.silentradio.co.uk/03/27/album-review-various-congo-funk-sound-madness-from-the-shores-of-the-mighty-congo-river-kinshasabrazzaville-1969-1982/

3. **From deuxième to frontman.** Lola Checain's usual role in TPOK Jazz was "deuxième voix," the second voice, harmonising under the lead singer. "Lolo Soulfire" is one of the rare moments where he is out front, singing his own composition with the whole Franco machine in support. Frames the entry around the sideman stepping into the spotlight. Source: https://mawalkingradio.com/lola-checain-icon-of-congolese-soukous-excellence/ ; https://kenyapage.net/commentary/okjazz/remembering-lola-djangi-checain/

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. Franco's orchestra spent the late 1960s mastering a laid-back rumba guitar interlock. Listen for the moment the band stops sounding like OK Jazz and starts sounding like the J.B.'s, and try to decide whether the rhythm section ever fully lets go of the rumba feel.
2. Count how many times Lolo yelps, grunts, or drops a James Brown-style "huh!" in three and a half minutes, then compare it to a 1971 Brown single of your choice. Who commits harder?
3. This was the B-side of a 1971 Kinshasa 7" by a rumba orchestra. If you did not know that, what clues in the recording, if any, would tell you it was cut in Congo and not Cincinnati?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- The track is described in reviews as "a pure James Brown 1971 funk cut" and as "Killer Afro Funk à la James Brown," and Bandcamp Daily writes that it "almost 'out-James Browns' the great man himself." Source: https://daily.bandcamp.com/album-of-the-day/various-artists-congo-funk-analog-africa-review ; https://www.popsike.com/FRANCO-LOLO-OK-JAZZ-45-7-Congo-BON-DE-LIVRAISON-LOLO-SOUFIRE-Afro-Funk/380384818193.html
- "Lolo Soulfire" is a B-side. The A-side was "Bon de Livraison" ("Delivery Note"), a Franco composition in the band's more familiar rumba register. So the funk experiment was literally buried on the flipside of a Franco single. Source: http://lesdisquesafricains.blogspot.com/2013/07/franco-lolo-lorchestre-ok-jazz-bon-de.html
- Of the 14 tracks on Analog Africa's Congo Funk! compilation (1969–1982), "Lolo Soulfire" is one of only two recordings that pre-date the 1974 Zaire 74 festival, which is usually cited as the moment Congolese musicians absorbed funk. Source: https://www.silentradio.co.uk/03/27/album-review-various-congo-funk-sound-madness-from-the-shores-of-the-mighty-congo-river-kinshasabrazzaville-1969-1982/
- The title on the original 1971 Fiesta label reads "Lolo Soufire." Analog Africa re-spelled it "Soulfire" for its 2024 reissue; Planet Ilunga kept the original "Soufire" on its 2024 Editions Populaires set. Source: https://planetilunga.bandcamp.com/track/lolo-soufire ; https://analogafrica.bandcamp.com/track/lolo-soulfire
- The original 7" is rare. A copy sold on French auction in November 2011 for €30, with the listing describing it as "very rare" and "impossible to find on the web." Source: https://www.popsike.com/FRANCO-LOLO-OK-JAZZ-45-7-Congo-BON-DE-LIVRAISON-LOLO-SOUFIRE-Afro-Funk/380384818193.html

#### Album

- There was no original album. The track lived only as a B-side on Fiesta 51.105 until 2024, when two different labels reissued it within six weeks of each other: Planet Ilunga's "Franco Luambo Makiadi Presents Les Editions Populaires (1968–1970)" on 29 February 2024, and Analog Africa's "Congo Funk!" on 5 April 2024. Source: https://planetilunga.bandcamp.com/album/franco-luambo-makiadi-presents-les-editions-populaires-1968-1970 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_Funk!
- Analog Africa's owner Samy Ben Redjeb reportedly sifted through about 2,000 tracks on trips to Kinshasa and Brazzaville to arrive at the final 14. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_Funk!
- Congo Funk! hit a Metacritic score of 89/100 ("universal acclaim") on six reviews, including 9/10 from Uncut and 4.5/5 from AllMusic. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_Funk!

#### Artist

- Lola Djangi Chécain (birth name Camille Lola Djangi, stage name "Lolo" or "Checain") was born 18 August 1942 in Kinshasa and died 10 August 1992 after a long illness, eight days short of his 50th birthday. Source: https://kenyapage.net/commentary/okjazz/remembering-lola-djangi-checain/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lola_Checain
- He came from a musical family. His older brother was the guitarist and singer Fariala, better known as Franck Lassan; his brother Johnny Bokosa was a bassist. His own son, Elvis Lola, is a working rhumba singer in Kenya. Source: https://kenyapage.net/commentary/okjazz/remembering-lola-djangi-checain/ ; https://nation.africa/kenya/life-and-style/weekend/rhumba-singer-elvis-lola-following-in-father-s-footsteps-3821886
- He joined OK Jazz in 1967 after being introduced to Franco by Simaro Lutumba and stayed with the band until Franco's death in October 1989: 22 years. He is best remembered as a "deuxième voix," the harmony singer below the lead, which makes a front-and-center funk showcase like "Lolo Soulfire" a real outlier in his catalog. Source: https://kenyapage.net/commentary/okjazz/remembering-lola-djangi-checain/ ; https://mawalkingradio.com/lola-checain-icon-of-congolese-soukous-excellence/
- OK Jazz itself was co-founded on 6 June 1956 in Léopoldville (now Kinshasa), taking its name from the OK Bar owned by Oscar Kashama. Franco renamed it Tout-Puissant Orchestre Kinois de Jazz (TPOK Jazz) in 1971, the same year "Lolo Soufire" was pressed. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TPOK_Jazz ; https://www.karakosproductions.com/sonodisc/artist/franco-le-t-p-ok-jazz/
- Editions Populaires, the label behind the 7", was Franco's own imprint, founded in 1968 and active until 1982. It was one of several artist-run Kinshasa labels in the post-independence boom. Source: https://planetilunga.com/2024/02/01/new-release-franco-luambo-makiadi-presents-les-editions-populaires/

### Genre Suggestions

1. **Afrobeat (current tag).** Under the book's working definition ("a broad label for funk rooted in Africa"), this fits cleanly: the track is African in origin and unambiguously funk. The definition explicitly anticipates a wider use than Fela-only West African Afrobeat. Rationale for keeping. Source: `Matter/On Genres & Regions.md`
2. **Deep Funk (alternative).** Textbook deep-funk story: buried as a B-side on a regional Kinshasa single, never reissued until crate-diggers surfaced it 53 years later on two different 2024 compilations. The book defines deep funk by "delayed recognition," which is exactly what happened here. Strong case for this tag, especially if the writing theme leans on the rediscovery arc. Source: `Matter/On Genres & Regions.md`
3. **Classic Funk (weak case).** Sits inside the 1968–1977 classic-funk window and the identity is clearly funk, not funk-fused-with-rumba (the rumba guitar interlock is mostly absent). But the book ties classic funk to the US commercial pipeline ("major labels, well-distributed singles and albums, radio play, concert tours, and chart presence"), which a rare Kinshasa B-side does not match. Mentioned for completeness, not recommended.

Recommendation: keep **Afrobeat** as the primary tag. If the author wants a secondary lens, **Deep Funk** is the strongest second choice and would let the entry carry the rediscovery story.

**Recommendation: add `Democratic Republic of the Congo` (or `DRC`) to the region enumeration.** Justification:

- The project already has three entries in research status centered on Congolese music (Petelo Vicka, L'Orchestre O.K. Jazz, Lolo & L'Orchestre O.K. Jazz), all sourced from the same 2024 Analog Africa compilation. That is enough critical mass to warrant its own region tag.
- Congolese rumba and its 1970s funk offshoots are a major node in African funk history. Folding three Congo entries under a generic tag like "Nigeria" or "Kenya" would misrepresent them.

Alternatives if the list stays closed:

- `Kenya` would be wrong. Kenya is listed only because of separate East African coverage; Lola Checain's late-life relocation to Kenya via his son is not the recording's origin.
