Today’s track is a cover of Preston’s “Cool Ade” from yesterday, cut by vibraphonist and bandleader Callen Radcliffe Tjader Jr. He was born on July 16, 1925, in St. Louis to Swedish-American vaudevillians. His father tap-danced and his mother played piano.

After the Navy, Tjader studied at San Francisco State and met a young Dave Brubeck. He played drums in Brubeck’s early groups, and had his path altered when Cuban percussionist Armando Peraza sat in with the band. Tjader dove into the tradition, teaching himself bongos and cencerro (cowbell), and eventually became one of the defining forces in Latin jazz.

A modern listener might pause at an outsider leading a Latin jazz band. But what happens when an outsider is also one of the best? Cal Tjader is often described as the most successful non-Latino musician in Latin jazz, and he influenced later artists, including Carlos Santana.

### Open Research Questions

> **Open: what was Cal Tjader's path to Latin music. How did he start playing Latin music?**

**Resolution:** Tjader's path to Latin music unfolded in three stages:

1. **First exposure with Brubeck (late 1940s, San Francisco):** While drumming in Dave Brubeck's groups in San Francisco, Tjader first encountered Cuban music when Cuban percussionist Armando Peraza sat in with the band. Tjader was deeply inspired and began immersing himself in Latin percussion, teaching himself bongos and cencerro (cowbell). — Sources: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/cal-tjader-best-songs-feature/ ; https://jazzprofiles.blogspot.com/2020/11/cal-tjader-life-recordings-of-man-who.html

2. **The turning point with George Shearing (1953, New York):** In 1953, pianist George Shearing recruited Tjader to replace vibraphonist Joe Roland in his popular quintet. This brought Tjader to New York, where Shearing's bassist **Al McKibbon** became the crucial catalyst. McKibbon took Tjader to see the Afro-Cuban big bands of **Machito** and **Chico O'Farrill**, both at the forefront of the nascent Latin jazz sound. In New York, Tjader also met **Mongo Santamaría** and **Willie Bobo**, who were then members of **Tito Puente's** orchestra. Tjader and McKibbon also encouraged Shearing to add Cuban percussionists to the quintet, further deepening Tjader's engagement with Latin rhythms. As the biography by S. Duncan Reid puts it, Tjader "fully perceived how the rhythms could alter the course of his career." — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cal_Tjader (search results); https://latinjazznet.com/artist-profiles/tribute-to-the-masters/cal-tjader-a-bridge-between-jazz-and-latin-rhythms/

3. **Going solo with the Modern Mambo Quintet (April 1954, San Francisco):** Tjader quit Shearing's group after a gig at the Blackhawk jazz club in San Francisco. In April 1954, he formed the **Cal Tjader Modern Mambo Quintet** and debuted at the **Macumba Club** in San Francisco. He began recording prolifically for Fantasy Records. Crucially, he recruited authentic Cuban players: Mongo Santamaría and Willie Bobo both joined his bands, and the exposure they received as Tjader sidemen enabled both to launch their own solo careers. Unlike the exotica acts of Martin Denny and Les Baxter, Tjader's bands featured seasoned Cuban musicians and top-notch jazz talent conversant in both idioms. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cal_Tjader (search results); https://latinjazznet.com/artist-profiles/tribute-to-the-masters/cal-tjader-a-bridge-between-jazz-and-latin-rhythms/

**Key takeaway for the author:** The Al McKibbon connection is the pivotal moment. A bassist in George Shearing's band physically took this Swedish-American kid from California to see Machito and Tito Puente's orchestras in New York. That's the spark. Everything else followed from those nights.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- "Agua Dulce (Cool-Ade)" is a Latin reimagining of "Cool Ade" from *Preston Love's Omaha Bar-B-Q* (1970, Kent Records), an album produced by Johnny Otis featuring his then-teenage son Shuggie Otis on guitar. The original is a short (2:02), raw instrumental funk number. Tjader doubles the length and completely transforms it into a Latin jazz groove with Moog synthesizer, Fender Rhodes, and full percussion section. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/3159276-Preston-Love-Featuring-Shuggie-Otis-Preston-Loves-Omaha-Bar-B-Q
- Rita Dowling plays Moog synthesizer on the track, making this one of the earliest uses of a Moog in a Latin jazz context. The cosmic synth textures create what one reviewer called a fusion of "Afro-Cuban and Space-Age sounds." — Source: https://jazzdiscography.com/Artists/cal-tjader/cal-tjader-discography-part3.php
- The album also includes a Latin jazz cover of the Rolling Stones' "Gimme Shelter," transforming one of rock's heaviest songs into a breezy Latin arrangement with eerie synth notes. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/cover/231967/Cal-Tjader-Gimme-Shelter-The-Rolling-Stones-Gimme-Shelter/

#### Album
- *Agua Dulce* was the second album Cal Tjader made after returning to Fantasy Records in mid-1970 under label head Saul Zaentz. — Source: https://www.psaudio.com/blogs/copper/cal-tjader-part-four-the-second-fantasy-records-era
- Recorded spring 1971 at Fantasy Studios, Berkeley, with horns and vocals overdubbed separately. Post-production completed September 1971. The session employed 13+ musicians. — Source: https://jazzdiscography.com/Artists/cal-tjader/cal-tjader-discography-part3.php
- "Curacao," a Tjader original on this album, was later sampled by Madlib ("Rapper X Radio"), J. Rawls ("Cao"), and Dr. Who Dat? ("Kelly Drive"). — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Cal-Tjader/Curacao/
- The album includes a version of Tito Puente's "Ran Kan Kan" described as "more scintillating" than the original due to Tjader's vibraphone cascades, and a rendition of Clare Fischer's "Morning." — Source: http://www.ambientexotica.com/exorev421_caltjader_aguadulce/ (review text found via search results)
- The BGP/Ace Records CD reissue (CDBGPM 243) includes liner notes by Dean Rudland calling Tjader "one of the most successful, most recorded and most influential jazz players" and the album "unjustly overlooked." — Source: https://www.acerecords.co.uk/agua-dulce

#### Artist
- Callen Radcliffe Tjader Jr. was born July 16, 1925 in St. Louis to Swedish-American vaudevillians. His father tap-danced and his mother played piano. When Cal was two, the family settled in San Mateo, California and opened a dance studio. His mother taught him classical piano; his father taught him to tap dance. — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/artist/cal-tjader-mn0000643549
- Tjader attended San Francisco State College, where he met Dave Brubeck and Paul Desmond. He graduated in 1950 with a B.A. in education and a minor in music. He played with Brubeck (1949-1951) and then joined George Shearing's Quintet before going solo. — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/artist/cal-tjader-mn0000643549
- He has been called "the greatest non-Latin bandleader in Latin jazz history." — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/artist/cal-tjader-mn0000643549
- Pete and Coke Escovedo, who play on this album, went on to form Azteca in 1972, a Latin-rock-funk supergroup that at times featured 15-25 members on stage. Alumni included Neal Schon (later of Journey), trumpeters Tom Harrell and Eddie Henderson, bassist Paul Jackson (Headhunters), and drummer Lenny White. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azteca_(band)
- Pete Escovedo is the father of Sheila E. (Sheila Escovedo), who first performed on stage with her father at age 5. She plays congas left-handed because she learned by mirroring her father's technique. Her work with Cal Tjader's circle preceded her career with Prince by many years. — Source: https://www.npr.org/2014/09/06/345548838/the-full-escovedo-what-you-may-not-know-about-sheila-e
- Coke Escovedo died on July 13, 1986, of a heroin overdose. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_Escovedo (via search results)
- Cal Tjader died May 5, 1982 in Manila, Philippines. He had arrived for a series of concerts and was admitted to Manila Medical Center after complaining of chest pains. He was scheduled to perform at the Folk Arts Theater May 7-9. He was 57. He is interred at Italian Cemetery in Colma, California, near San Francisco. — Source: https://www.upi.com/Archives/1982/05/05/American-jazz-musician-Cal-Tjader-57-died-today-in/3211389419200/
- Tjader won the Grammy for Best Latin Recording in 1980 for *La Onda Va Bien*. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cal_Tjader (via search results)
- "Soul Sauce" (1964) was his biggest single hit. — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/artist/cal-tjader-mn0000643549

### Genre Suggestions

The current genre is **Samba Funk**. Here are my considerations:

1. **Jazz-Funk** (recommended primary): This track sits squarely at the intersection of jazz and funk. The Fender Rhodes, Moog synthesizer, and funky rhythmic feel place it firmly in jazz-funk territory. AllMusic classifies the album as Afro-Cuban Jazz, Cool, Latin Jazz, and Post-Bop, but the funky electric instrumentation and groove push it toward jazz-funk within our genre enumeration. The album is frequently described in record-selling contexts as "funk latin soul jazz."
2. **Soul Jazz** (strong alternative): The warm, groove-oriented feel, the electric piano, and the accessible melodic approach have strong soul jazz qualities. The track takes a raw funk source (Preston Love/Otis) and smooths it into something groovier and more melodic.
3. **Samba Funk** (current value, weaker fit): While Tjader had significant Brazilian connections and the album has Latin rhythms, the dominant rhythmic feel here is Afro-Cuban rather than samba. The conga/timbales/bongo framework is more mambo/descarga than samba. Samba Funk is a defensible but slightly off-target label.

**Recommendation:** Jazz-Funk is the best fit. The Fender Rhodes, Moog, and funky groove align perfectly. Soul Jazz is a solid second choice.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The Swedish-American king of Latin funk"**: Tjader was born to Swedish vaudevillians in St. Louis, raised in suburban California, and somehow became the most important non-Latino musician in Latin jazz history. This tension between identity and musical mastery is a compelling hook: who gets to play what, and what happens when someone does it better than almost everyone?

2. **"The Moog meets the conga"**: This track is an early collision of analog synthesis and Afro-Cuban percussion, two sounds that rarely shared a studio in 1971. Rita Dowling's Moog lines threading through Pete and Coke Escovedo's percussion opens up a story about technological experimentation in Latin jazz, and how this album was ahead of its time.

3. **"From bar-b-q to agua dulce"**: The source material is a gritty 2-minute funk instrumental from Preston Love's Omaha Bar-B-Q, produced by Johnny Otis with teenage Shuggie Otis on guitar. Tjader takes that raw sketch and transforms it into something entirely different: longer, lusher, Latin, and cosmic. A story about covers as creative reinvention, not imitation.
