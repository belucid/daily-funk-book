If you can hear the connection between today’s song, David Bowie’s "Fame," and a James Brown track, it's not in the singing. It's in the guitar. Carlos Alomar, who plays guitar on “Fame,” was in Brown’s band before joining Bowie. Strip away Bowie’s art-rock vocal and the track reveals itself as pure James Brown: a one-chord vamp driven by an irresistible rhythm. It stays on F7 throughout, with the drums, bass, and guitar locked tight together. Later that same year, Brown borrowed Alomar's guitar riff for "Hot (I Need to Be Loved, Loved, Loved, Loved)."

"Fame" reached No. 1 on the *Billboard* Hot 100 in the week of September 20, 1975. It was Bowie’s first chart-topper, but more familiar territory for his co-writer. Along with Bowie and Alomar, "Fame" was written by John Lennon. Both men were living in New York City at the time, and Mick Jagger introduced them. 

Bowie invited Lennon into the studio, and the song came together quickly in a single session. They began with Alomar's guitar riff, and Lennon started singing the word “fame” over it. Can you hear his backing vocal contributions in the final version?

### Open Research Questions

**1. Verify Bowie had no other #1 songs.**

**Finding: This is incorrect as stated in the entry body.** Bowie had *two* #1 hits on the Billboard Hot 100 during his lifetime:

- **"Fame"** reached #1 on September 20, 1975. Source: [Billboard, "David Bowie 'Fame' No. 1 Hot 100 1975"](https://www.billboard.com/pro/david-bowie-fame-no-1-hot-100-1975-rewinding-charts/)
- **"Let's Dance"** reached #1 on May 21, 1983. Source: [Billboard, "David Bowie Was No. 1 On The Hot 100 In 1983"](https://www.billboard.com/pro/david-bowie-no-1-hot-100-1983-chart-rewind/)

Also corroborated by the [David Bowie Wikipedia discography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bowie_discography), which lists both songs as Bowie's only US #1 singles.

The current body copy reads: *"Bowie's first and only chart-topper during his lifetime."* This should be corrected. Accurate phrasings for the author to consider:
- "Bowie's first US #1, and one of only two he would score in his lifetime, the other being 'Let's Dance' eight years later."
- "Bowie's first US chart-topper. He would hit #1 only once more, with 'Let's Dance' in 1983."

Note: "Fame" *was* Bowie's first and only #1 on the UK Singles Chart if you restrict to UK charts (he had other UK #1s later, e.g., "Ashes to Ashes" 1980, "Under Pressure" 1981, "Let's Dance" 1983). But the body copy specifies "#1 on the Billboard Hot 100," so the US chart is the relevant frame, and "only" is wrong.

**2. Verify Lennon is co-author of Fame.**

**Finding: Confirmed.** "Fame" is credited to three writers: **David Bowie, Carlos Alomar, and John Lennon.**

- [Wikipedia, "Fame (David Bowie song)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fame_(David_Bowie_song)) lists all three as co-writers.
- [SecondHandSongs](https://secondhandsongs.com/work/1540/all) registers the work as "written by Carlos Alomar, John Lennon, David Bowie."
- [JohnLennon.com](https://www.johnlennon.com/music/collaborations/david-bowie-fame/) documents Lennon's role on the session: the "aim / Fame" vocal hook, backwards piano, and backing "oohs."
- Publishing is split across Lenono Music (Lennon), Tintoretto Music / Chrysalis Songs (Bowie), and related publishers, consistent with a three-way co-write. Source: [SecondHandSongs](https://secondhandsongs.com/work/1540/all)

No counter-source found; the three-way credit is universal across every reference checked.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The Godfather gave it back."** James Brown's influence runs through every second of "Fame," from the one-chord vamp to the clipped guitar stabs. Carlos Alomar played in Brown's band before joining Bowie. And then Brown turned around and lifted the riff wholesale for his own single "Hot (I Need to Be Loved, Loved, Loved, Loved)" later that same year. This circular exchange, white British rock star borrowing Black American funk and then having the Godfather of Funk borrow it right back, opens up a vivid conversation about the flow of influence in funk.

2. **"Written in anger, danced to in joy."** Bowie called "Fame" "nasty, angry" and admitted it was written "with a degree of malice" directed at his former manager Tony Defries and the MainMan debacle. Yet the track became a #1 dance hit. The tension between bitter lyrics and an irresistible groove is a compelling frame for the entry.

3. **"One chord, three legends, one evening."** The entire song was written and recorded in a single evening session on January 30, 1975, built almost entirely on one F7 chord. The improbable collision of Bowie, Lennon, and Alomar in one room, plus the spontaneous origin story (Lennon singing "aim" over Alomar's discarded riff), makes for a tight narrative about creative lightning.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- "Fame" was built almost entirely on a single F7 chord, with a brief two-bar movement to B-flat. This minimalist harmonic structure is closer to James Brown than to anything in Bowie's earlier catalog. — Source: https://bowiesongs.wordpress.com/2010/11/22/fame/
- The song's closing section uses varispeed tape manipulation: Bowie's repeated vocal "fame" descends stepwise from a high E-flat through multiple octaves over six bars, the tape literally slowing his voice into a deep growl. — Source: https://bowiesongs.wordpress.com/2010/11/22/fame/
- The reversed piano sound in the intro was supervised by John Lennon. When producer Harry Maslin explained his backwards recording technique, Lennon responded: "The Beatles never did it that way." — Source: https://www.johnlennon.com/music/collaborations/david-bowie-fame/
- Lennon described his contribution modestly: "backwards piano and 'Ooh' and a couple of things." But his most important contribution may have been singing the nonsense syllable "aim" over Alomar's riff, which Bowie then flipped into "Fame." — Source: https://www.johnlennon.com/music/collaborations/david-bowie-fame/
- Lennon later reflected on the track's success with a sense of karmic transfer: "I felt like that was like a karmic thing... I passed it on to Bowie and he got his [Number One]." — Source: https://www.johnlennon.com/music/collaborations/david-bowie-fame/
- The track's two opening bars are in 3/4 time before locking into straight 4/4 for the rest. — Source: https://bowiesongs.wordpress.com/2010/11/22/fame/
- The single edit (3:30) was significantly shorter than the album version (4:12-4:21). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fame_(David_Bowie_song)
- Dennis Davis plays vibraslap on the track in addition to drums. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fame_(David_Bowie_song)

#### Album
- Young Americans was recorded in two stages: August 1974 at Sigma Sound Studios in Philadelphia (the home of the Philly soul sound), and January 1975 at Electric Lady Studios and Record Plant in New York City. "Fame" came from the later NYC sessions. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Americans
- Bowie described the album's sound as "plastic soul," a self-deprecating label acknowledging that a white British rock star was borrowing Black American music. In a 1976 Playboy interview he called it "the squashed remains of ethnic music as it survives in the age of Muzak rock." — Source: https://www.stereogum.com/2053836/the-number-ones-david-bowies-fame/columns/the-number-ones/
- Luther Vandross provided backing vocals on the album and co-wrote "Fascination," years before his own rise to R&B stardom. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Americans
- Local Philadelphia fans known as the "Sigma Kids" waited outside Sigma Sound Studios during the August 1974 sessions. On the final day, Bowie invited them inside to hear rough mixes. — Source: https://www.phillyvoice.com/sigma-kids-david-bowie-philadelphia-young-americans/
- The album's 50th anniversary half-speed mastered LP was released in March 2025. — Source: https://www.davidbowie.com/blog/2025/1/16/young-americans-50th-anniversary-vinyl-due-in-march

#### Artist
- Carlos Alomar, the riff's architect, was born in Ponce, Puerto Rico, taught himself guitar from age ten, and played at the Apollo Theater's Amateur Hour as a teenager before joining the house band. He backed Chuck Berry and leading soul artists before forming the group Listen My Brother with Luther Vandross and Robin Clark. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_Alomar
- Alomar had briefly played in James Brown's band in the late 1960s before joining Bowie, making the Brown-to-Bowie-to-Brown riff cycle even more tangled. — Source: https://www.stereogum.com/2053836/the-number-ones-david-bowies-fame/columns/the-number-ones/
- Dennis Davis (August 28, 1949, April 6, 2016) studied with jazz legends Max Roach and Elvin Jones, played in the Clark Terry Big Band, and served in the U.S. Navy during Vietnam before becoming Bowie's drummer. He went on to play on seven consecutive Bowie albums through Scary Monsters (1980), and later worked with Stevie Wonder. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Davis
- James Brown openly acknowledged lifting the "Fame" riff for his December 1975 single "Hot (I Need to Be Loved, Loved, Loved, Loved)." In a 1979 interview he said: "I did a thing of Fame called 'Hot'." The single only reached #31 on the R&B chart. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/sample/54794/James-Brown-Hot-(I-Need-to-Be-Loved,-Loved,-Loved,-Loved)-David-Bowie-Fame/
- The lyrics were a direct attack on Bowie's former manager Tony Defries and the MainMan management company. Bowie later called the song "nasty, angry" and said it was written "with a degree of malice." Biographer Peter Doggett identified the lyric "bully for you, chilly for me" as a specific jab at Defries. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fame_(David_Bowie_song)
- Defries had also financed a musical theatre production called "Fame" (about Marilyn Monroe) through MainMan, which closed after one night on Broadway, adding another layer to the song's title. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fame_(David_Bowie_song)
- Bowie performed "Fame" on Soul Train on November 4, 1975 (broadcast January 3, 1976), making him one of the few white artists to appear on the show. He was reportedly nervous and had been drinking before the taping. — Source: https://www.openculture.com/2016/11/david-bowie-sings-fame-golden-years-on-soul-train-1975.html
- "Fame" has been sampled in over 30 songs. Notable hip-hop samples include Public Enemy's "Night of the Living Baseheads" (1988), Ice Cube's "Alive on Arrival" (1991), Jay-Z's "The Blueprint" (2001), and Puff Daddy's "Been Around the World" featuring Notorious B.I.G. and Mase (1997). — Source: https://www.billboard.com/music/rb-hip-hop/rap-songs-sampled-david-bowie-6836517/
- "Fame" is included in the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame's "500 Songs that Shaped Rock and Roll." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fame_(David_Bowie_song)
- US Gold certified (1,000,000 units). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fame_(David_Bowie_song)

### Genre Suggestions

The current genre is **Funk Rock**.

1. **Funk Rock** (current value, strong case): AllMusic and Wikipedia both classify the track as "funk rock" and "funk." The track fuses Bowie's rock vocal style and rock-star persona with a pure funk groove, Alomar's guitar riff, and a rhythm section playing straight-up funk. The "rock" element is more about the artist's identity and the vocal approach than the instrumentation, which is nearly pure funk. This is a reasonable fit for the book's definition, especially given that Bowie is fundamentally a rock artist making funk. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fame_(David_Bowie_song)

2. **Classic Funk**: The track was released in 1975, right in the heart of the classic funk era (1968-1977 per the book's definition). It hit #1 on the Billboard Hot 100 and was distributed through a major label (RCA Records). The instrumentation, the one-chord vamp, the clipped guitar stabs, the locked-in rhythm section, are all hallmarks of classic funk. The main argument against this label is that Bowie is not a funk artist by primary identity; he's a rock chameleon who made a funk record. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fame_(David_Bowie_song)

3. **Deep Funk** is NOT appropriate here. "Fame" was a #1 Billboard hit, not a rediscovered obscurity.

**Recommendation:** Funk Rock is the right call. The track is musically almost pure funk, but Bowie's identity as a rock artist, and the way the song fits into his catalog as a genre experiment rather than a home-base recording, makes Funk Rock the better label for this book's purposes.
