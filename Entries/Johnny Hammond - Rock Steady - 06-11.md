“Rock Steady” is such a great song that you get to hear it twice in this book. We already heard Aretha Franklin’s original on February 15. Today’s track is a soul-jazz cover by Johnny “Hammond” Smith.

As is typical of soul jazz, “Rock Steady” is an instrumental cover of a popular song, stretched out to make room for improvisation. Aretha’s version runs about three minutes. Here, it pushes past six, making space for solos by Smith on Hammond B-3 organ, Eric Gale on guitar, Grover Washington Jr. on saxophone, and Bernard “Pretty” Purdie on drums.

Purdie is the glue between the two versions. Born on June 11, 1939, he was Aretha’s touring drummer and musical director in the early 1970s, and he played on her original recording of “Rock Steady.” He was also a busy session musician for Atlantic, CTI, and Kudu Records. Eight months after helping make “Rock Steady” a hit for Aretha, Purdie was behind the kit again, making groove magic with the same song a second time.

### Open Research Questions

Wikipedia hedges by titling the article around the 1971 recording date but listing 1972 as the release. AllMusic lists 1971; most retailers, Discogs, and Apple Music list 1972. Used 1972 as the released-to-public year, consistent with the Kudu KU-907 single also dated 1972. ### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The bassline gets the call."** Aretha's "Rock Steady" (Feb 15, 1971) was a tight 3:15 vocal-and-rhythm-section masterpiece. Eight months later, Creed Taylor handed the song to Johnny Hammond and a room full of CTI session aces, and they doubled its length, opened up the drum break, and turned Eric Gale loose with a wah-wah pedal. The theme: how funk songs grow when the instrumentalists get the keys.

2. **"Bernard Purdie, twice."** Pretty Purdie was Aretha's musical director from 1970, and his drumming on her original is the song's beating heart. Eight months later he sat down at Van Gelder's and played it again for Johnny Hammond, this time with twice the room to stretch. A theme about session musicians as the connective tissue between famous originals and their famous covers, and about Purdie as the rhythmic architecture of Atlantic-and-CTI funk in the early '70s.

3. **"Wild Horses meets Rock Steady."** The album title pairs a Stones ballad with Aretha's funk workout, and the pairing is the joke and the manifesto. Creed Taylor's Kudu was selling crossover jazz to a rock-and-soul audience, and Hammond's B-3 was the bridge. A theme that uses the album title as a Rosetta Stone for what Kudu was trying to do.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. The drummer on this track, Bernard Purdie, also played drums on Aretha's original "Rock Steady" eight months earlier, with a tighter pop-radio runtime of 3:15. Listen for what he does with twice the time at Van Gelder's. Where does the same drummer take the same song when nobody's writing a single?

2. About 90 seconds in, Eric Gale's wah-wah guitar takes a long solo, and Grover Washington Jr. steps up after him. Pick one of the two solos and follow it all the way through. Could either of those have happened on Aretha's three-minute original?

3. Aretha's "Rock Steady" is built around her vocal phrasing. Hammond's cover keeps every chord and bassline but throws away the words. Listen with both versions in mind. What does the song lose without the lyric, and what does it gain?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "Rock Steady" is the album opener, clocking 6:48 — more than double the 3:15 length of Aretha Franklin's original. It was released as a Kudu 7" single (KU-907) in 1972, split into "Rock Steady (Part 1)" (2:55) and "Rock Steady (Part 2)" (3:55). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_Horses_Rock_Steady ; https://991.com/Buy/ProductInformation.aspx?StockNumber=680524
- The personnel reads as a CTI/Kudu session all-star team: Johnny Hammond (organ, electric piano), Eric Gale (guitar, wah-wah), Grover Washington Jr. (saxophone), Ron Carter (bass), Bernard Purdie (drums), Omar Clay (percussion), with strings (eight violins, no violas or cellos) arranged by Bob James. — Source: https://www.dougpayne.com/adsnotes6.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_Horses_Rock_Steady
- Bernard Purdie is the rhythmic link between Aretha's "Rock Steady" original and Hammond's cover: he played drums on both. Purdie was Aretha Franklin's musical director from 1970, a position he held for five years. He's not an incidental crossover; he's the rhythmic author of both versions. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Purdie ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_Steady_(Aretha_Franklin_song)
- One contemporaneous description of the cover: "Pretty Purdie goes at it again with some mean drumming … the break from the original is opened up even further by Purdie then the tune just goes into overdrive," with strings entering before another drum flourish closes the track. — Source: https://fleamarketfunk.com/2012/09/13/johnny-hammond-rock-steady/
- Sampled by 4 Hero on "Parallel Universe" (1994) and by garage producer Todd Edwards on "Saved My Life" (1995), among other acid jazz and electronic flips. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Johnny-Hammond/Rock-Steady/

#### Album

- _Wild Horses Rock Steady_ was Hammond's second Kudu LP after _Breakout_ (KU-01, 1971), and the fourth release on the Kudu label overall (KU-04). — Source: https://www.jazzdisco.org/cti-records/kudu-records-catalog-twelve-inch-series/album-index/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_Horses_Rock_Steady
- Producer Creed Taylor designed the album as a "crossover" project, leaning into pop and rock material (Cat Stevens' "Peace Train," the Stones' "Wild Horses," Andrew Lloyd Webber's "I Don't Know How to Love Him") with strings and horns layered by Bob James over Van Gelder's room sound. — Source: https://www.dougpayne.com/adsnotes6.htm ; https://soundsoftheuniverse.com/sjr/product/johnny-hammond-wild-horses-rock-steady-1972
- Recorded October–November 1971 at Van Gelder Studio, Englewood Cliffs, NJ; engineered by Rudy Van Gelder; arranged and conducted by Bob James. Total album runtime is 38:12 across six tracks. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_Horses_Rock_Steady
- The full-album cast (across all six tracks) includes Hammond, Grover Washington Jr., Eric Gale, George Benson, Melvin Sparks, Bob Mann, Ron Carter, Bernard Purdie, Billy Cobham, Harold Vick, Snooky Young, Pepper Adams, Airto Moreira, and Omar Clay — a Who's Who of early-'70s New York studio jazz-funk. — Source: https://www.dougpayne.com/adsnotes6.htm ; https://soundsoftheuniverse.com/sjr/product/johnny-hammond-wild-horses-rock-steady-1972
- AllMusic awarded 3.5 stars, calling it "another stunning example of great creativity at Creed Taylor's Kudu label through the mid-'70s." Doug Payne calls it the masterpiece of Hammond's four Kudu albums. — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/wild-horses-rock-steady-mw0000462498 ; https://www.dougpayne.com/adsnotes6.htm

#### Artist

- Hammond signed with Creed Taylor's Kudu (a CTI subsidiary) in 1971; his _Breakout_ debut was the inaugural Kudu release, and _Wild Horses Rock Steady_ followed roughly six months later. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_%22Hammond%22_Smith
- John Robert Smith was born December 16, 1933 in Louisville, Kentucky and died June 4, 1997 in Victorville, California (cancer, age 63). The "Hammond" nickname came from his command of the B-3; he dropped "Smith" entirely starting with the Kudu years. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_%22Hammond%22_Smith
- Hammond would later make _Gears_ (1975) on Milestone with the Mizell Brothers, the more commercially celebrated jazz-funk peak of his catalog. The Kudu period that bracketed _Wild Horses Rock Steady_ is sometimes treated as Hammond's transitional move from soul-jazz organ player to full-on jazz-funk session leader. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gears_(album) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_%22Hammond%22_Smith
- Grover Washington Jr. played extensively on Hammond's CTI/Kudu sessions (including this one) before _Inner City Blues_ (1971) and _Mister Magic_ (1974) launched his solo stardom. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grover_Washington_Jr.

### Genre Suggestions

1. **Jazz-Funk (recommended primary).** Career jazz organist plus an all-star jazz session crew (Carter, Purdie, Gale, Grover, Bob James) reframing a 1971 funk hit at extended length, with solo space for organ, wah-wah guitar, and saxophone. Maps directly onto the book's working definition of jazz-funk: jazz musicians playing funk, longer-form, with room for improvisation. AllMusic tags the album "Hard Bop, Jazz-Funk, Soul Jazz." — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/wild-horses-rock-steady-mw0000462498

2. **Soul Jazz (defensible alternate).** Hammond's lineage is soul jazz; his Prestige-era organ-trio voicings still anchor the harmony, even as the band around him pushes into funk. The book describes soul jazz as "the early simmer," and this Kudu cover sits right at the boundary where soul jazz starts boiling over into jazz-funk. — Source: `Matter/On Genres & Regions.md`

3. **Classic Funk (weaker fit).** The original Aretha is classic funk, but Hammond's instrumental, 6:48-long, jazz-musician treatment of it puts the cover in jazz-funk territory by the book's definition.

Recommendation: **Jazz-Funk**, matching the existing Hammond entry (`Tell Me What To Do`) for editorial consistency.
