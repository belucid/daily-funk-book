---
"I'll Take You There" is my favorite funk song. It has no real verses, just call and response, band cues, and raw reactions to the groove. There's no real chord progression either, just a two-chord vamp on C and F. Mavis Staples’ funky vocals and that deep Muscle Shoals pocket make it work.

The song's iconic bassline was lifted from "Liquidator" by the Harry J Allstars, a 1969 Jamaican reggae instrumental. Al Bell brought the record to Muscle Shoals Sound Studio, but the session musicians thought it was a demo due to the rough recording. They used the bassline without crediting the original.

Lead singer Mavis Staples was born on July 10, 1939. She says she co-wrote the lyrics with Al Bell and was furious not to receive a writing credit. The dispute lingered, and has been cited as a factor in the Staple Singers’ eventual break with Stax.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The reggae root nobody heard"** — The song's intro and bassline were lifted directly from "The Liquidator" by Harry J Allstars, a 1969 Jamaican reggae instrumental. Al Bell brought the record to Muscle Shoals, and the session musicians thought it was a demo, not an existing release. This makes "I'll Take You There" arguably the first reggae-influenced single to hit #1 in America, years before reggae broke through to the mainstream. The angle: an invisible Jamaican foundation under a gospel-funk landmark.

2. **"The song Mavis co-wrote but never got credit for"** — Mavis Staples has stated publicly that she and Al Bell co-wrote the lyrics on her Chicago apartment floor, and that the lack of a co-writing credit was a source of lasting fury, contributing to the group's departure from Stax in 1975. The angle: a story about authorship, credit, and power in the music business, told through one of the most joyful songs ever recorded.

3. **"Two chords, no verses, pure conviction"** — The song has no verses, no choruses, just two chords (C and F) and a call-and-response structure. Everything rests on Mavis Staples' voice and the Muscle Shoals pocket. The angle: radical simplicity as a creative choice, where stripping a song to its bones makes it stronger.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- The song's intro and bassline were directly borrowed from "The Liquidator" by Harry J Allstars, a 1969 Jamaican reggae instrumental that was a #9 UK hit. Al Bell brought a copy of the record to the Muscle Shoals sessions and played it for the musicians, who assumed it was a demo Bell had made and not an actual released song. This makes it possibly the first reggae-influenced #1 single in American chart history. — Source: https://stereogum.com/2033579/the-number-ones-the-staple-singers-ill-take-you-there/columns/the-number-ones
- The song uses only two chords (C and F) in the key of C, and has no traditional verse or chorus structure, relying entirely on call-and-response. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%27ll_Take_You_There
- Engineer Terry Manning compiled Mavis Staples' lead vocal from 3-4 takes per song, punching in superior lines without razor-blade splicing, interspersing guide vocal elements with final takes to preserve her spontaneous delivery. — Source: https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/classic-tracks-staple-singers-ill-take-you-there
- Manning added synthesizer textures using a Moog IIIC, creating "tinkly" high-frequency elements placed throughout the track, plus distorted guitar via a Fender Telecaster with humbucking pickup and Gibson Fuzz-Tone. — Source: https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/classic-tracks-staple-singers-ill-take-you-there
- The recording was overdubbed and mixed at Ardent Studios in Memphis. Horns and strings were recorded at Artie Fields Recording Studios in Detroit. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%27ll_Take_You_There
- The song was sampled by Salt-N-Pepa in their 1991 hit "Let's Talk About Sex," and by Big Daddy Kane on multiple tracks. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/The-Staple-Singers/I%27ll-Take-You-There/sampled/
- By December 1972, it had sold 2 million copies, making it the 19th biggest American hit of 1972. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%27ll_Take_You_There
- Rolling Stone editor David Fricke called this song the "epitome of the Muscle Shoals Sound." — Background knowledge, but no source found (attributed in multiple secondary sources but original Fricke piece not located).

#### Album
- *Be Altitude: Respect Yourself* was released February 14, 1972 on Stax Records and contained three Top 10 hits: "Respect Yourself," "I'll Take You There," and "This World." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Be_Altitude:_Respect_Yourself
- The album was reissued in 2022 as a 50th anniversary 180-gram vinyl pressing with all-analog mastering from the original stereo tapes by Jeff Powell at Take Out Vinyl. — Source: https://craftrecordings.com/blogs/news/the-staple-singers-be-altitude-respect-yourself-180-gr-vinyl-reissue
- Producer Al Bell sought "textural" layering beyond traditional R&B approaches. Manning noted that approximately "80 percent" of his experimental overdub ideas received Bell's approval. — Source: https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/classic-tracks-staple-singers-ill-take-you-there

#### Artist
- The Staple Singers were a family group: patriarch Roebuck "Pops" Staples (b. Dec 28, 1914, near Winona, Mississippi) and his children Cleotha (b. Apr 11, 1934), Pervis (b. Nov 18, 1935), Yvonne (b. Oct 23, 1937), and Mavis (b. Jul 10, 1939). They formed in 1948 in Chicago. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Staple_Singers
- Al Bell (born Alvertis Isbell, March 15, 1940, Brinkley, Arkansas) wrote the song after attending the funeral of his little brother, who had been shot to death. — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/the-staple-singers/ill-take-you-there (via search summary; Songfacts was inaccessible for direct fetch)
- Mavis Staples has publicly stated she and Bell co-wrote the lyrics on the living room floor of her Chicago apartment, and she was furious about not receiving a co-writing credit. This dispute contributed to the Staple Singers leaving Stax in 1975. — Source: https://stereogum.com/2033579/the-number-ones-the-staple-singers-ill-take-you-there/columns/the-number-ones
- Bob Dylan cited the Staple Singers' "Uncloudy Day" as a formative influence, saying Mavis' singing "just knocked me out." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Staple_Singers
- The Staple Singers were inducted into the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame in 1999, inducted by Lauryn Hill. — Source: https://rockhall.com/inductees/staple-singers/
- They received the Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award in 2005. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Staple_Singers
- Pops Staples grew up on a cotton plantation near Drew, Mississippi, the youngest of 14 children. He was a neighbor and friend of Charley Patton, the "father of the Delta blues." — Source: https://msbluestrail.org/blues-trail-markers/pops-staples
- Al Bell became the first African American to hold equity in Stax Records when co-founder Estelle Axton sold her shares in 1969. — Source: https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/al-bell-4774/
- After Stax went bankrupt in 1975, the Staple Singers signed to Curtis Mayfield's Curtom Records. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Staple_Singers

### Genre Suggestions

Current genre: **Gospel Funk**

1. **Gospel Funk** — The current assignment is strong. The Staple Singers began as a gospel group and never fully left that identity, even as they moved toward secular themes. "I'll Take You There" invokes a heavenly place ("ain't nobody worried, ain't nobody crying") in a call-and-response style rooted in Black church tradition, delivered over a deep funk groove. This is the textbook intersection of gospel and funk. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Staple_Singers

2. **Classic Funk** — The track hit #1 on the Hot 100 in 1972, was on a major distributed label (Stax), and features the Muscle Shoals Rhythm Section playing a locked-in funk groove. It fits squarely in the 1968-1977 classic era and was a massive commercial hit. The gospel element is undeniable, but the track's primary identity on the radio and charts was funk/soul. — Source: https://stereogum.com/2033579/the-number-ones-the-staple-singers-ill-take-you-there/columns/the-number-ones

3. **Deep Funk** — Not recommended. This was a chart-topping hit, not an overlooked rarity, so Deep Funk doesn't apply.

**Recommendation:** Gospel Funk is the best fit. The song's spiritual intention and call-and-response structure are clearly gospel-derived, while the groove, rhythm section, and production are pure funk. Classic Funk would also be defensible if the book needs fewer Gospel Funk entries.

1. **US - Illinois** — The Staple Singers formed in Chicago in 1948 and were based there throughout their career. Mavis Staples was born in Chicago. The group is widely associated with Chicago's gospel and soul traditions. This is the strongest choice for "where the artist is from." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Staple_Singers

2. **US - Southeast** — The track was recorded at Muscle Shoals Sound Studios in Sheffield, Alabama, with the famous Muscle Shoals Rhythm Section providing the instrumental backing. If the region tag is meant to reflect the sound and recording origin, this is defensible: the track sounds like Muscle Shoals because it is Muscle Shoals. — Source: https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/classic-tracks-staple-singers-ill-take-you-there

3. **US - Southeast (Memphis)** — Stax Records was based in Memphis, Tennessee. The overdubs and mixing happened at Ardent Studios in Memphis. If the label/post-production origin matters, this also points Southeast. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%27ll_Take_You_There

**Recommendation:** US - Illinois. The Staple Singers are a Chicago group, and the artist origin is the most natural fit. However, the Muscle Shoals connection is a notable part of the story.
