Video gamers may already know today's song, “Smokin’ Cheeba Cheeba.” It plays on the rare-groove station Master Sounds 98.3 in *Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas*. It comes from an obscure album on Paul Winley’s small Harlem label. The Harlem Underground Band was Winley’s in-house studio group, with George Benson on guitar, Dave “Baby” Cortez on organ, Willis Jackson on saxophone, Sterling Magee on bass and guitar, and Earl Williams on drums. Of those names, Benson is the one readers are most likely to recognize.

Born on March 22, 1943, in Pittsburgh, Benson was a child prodigy who became a professional jazz guitarist while still young. He built a strong reputation through soul-jazz session work and a successful run of albums for CTI in the early 1970s.

Then came 1976. Benson left CTI for Warner Bros. and released *Breezin’*, the album that transformed him from a respected jazz guitarist into a crossover pop star. It reached #1 on the *Billboard* 200 and introduced a much larger audience to his guitar playing and his voice. Benson's popularity continued to grow through the 1980s, and he went on to record a string of pop hits and win ten Grammy Awards.

That makes “Smokin’ Cheeba Cheeba” a fascinating snapshot. In the same year Benson broke into the mainstream, he was also tearing through a low-budget Harlem funk session on an obscure independent label.

- **Single release:** No 7" single of "Smokin' Cheeba-Cheeba" was located. The track circulated on the 1976 LP and on a later 12" (Discogs release 968149, cat. 12x33-13, ©℗ 1976 but pressed early 1980s). Could not confirm a contemporaneous 1976 45.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"The break that built hip-hop."** The track itself is an obscure 1976 Harlem house-band jam, but its drum/bass break became raw material for a generation of rap producers (Tone Loc, GZA, Eric B & Rakim circles, Smif-N-Wessun). The entry can frame the gap between a record's commercial obscurity and its sampled afterlife.
- **"A house band hiding a star."** A young, pre-fame George Benson plays guitar anonymously inside Paul Winley's studio crew. An angle on session musicians and the records that get retroactively reframed once a sideman becomes famous.
- **"Cheeba cheeba: weed as a hook."** The recurring sung "cheeba cheeba" (slang for marijuana) as a deadpan vocal motif riding a 7+ minute groove — funk as mood and in-joke rather than song.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- Around the midpoint, Buddy Lucas takes a long harmonica solo over the groove — unusual for funk of this era. Track when the harmonica enters and ask whether it reads as blues, jazz, or pure texture.
- Ann Winley's "cheeba cheeba" refrain is the only real vocal hook across nearly eight minutes. Listen for how the band uses her sparingly to mark sections — what is she signaling each time she returns?
- This is a studio house band, not a touring group. Listen for the loop-ready bars producers later sampled: where does the groove feel most "built to be cut up"?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "Smokin' Cheeba Cheeba" was written by Paul and Ann Winley and features a lengthy harmonica solo by Buddy Lucas and the recurring "cheeba cheeba" theme sung by Ann Winley ("cheeba" being slang for marijuana). Source: https://music.fandom.com/wiki/Smokin'_Cheeba_Cheeba
- The track became a heavily sampled hip-hop break, used by Tone Loc on "Cheeba Cheeba" (1989) and referenced by Eric B & Rakim, GZA/Genius, Medina Green and Smif-N-Wessun. Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/harlem-underground-mw0000899565
- It appears on the in-game radio station "Master Sounds 98.3" in Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas, giving the obscure cut a second life with gamers. Source: https://music.fandom.com/wiki/Smokin'_Cheeba_Cheeba
- Runtime is 7:44 on the LP — long even by 1970s funk-jam standards. Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/468942-Harlem-Underground-Band-Harlem-Underground

#### Album

- The LP also yielded "Ain't No Sunshine" (a Bill Withers cover, just 2:21) which itself was sampled by A Tribe Called Quest, The Beatnuts and Ghostface Killah — so a single obscure album seeded multiple hip-hop classics. Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/harlem-underground-mw0000899565
- The album is genre-tagged as Jazz and Funk/Soul with Soul-Jazz and Funk styles on Discogs, reflecting its mix of organ-led soul-jazz and break-heavy funk. Source: https://www.discogs.com/master/139515-Harlem-Underground-Band-Harlem-Underground
- Personnel reads like a soul-jazz dream team for a tiny Harlem label: George Benson (guitar), Dave "Baby" Cortez (organ), Willis Jackson (sax), Buddy Lucas (harmonica), Sterling Magee (bass), Earl Williams (drums). Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/468942-Harlem-Underground-Band-Harlem-Underground

#### Artist

- The Harlem Underground Band was effectively the house band of Paul Winley's Winley Records and later backed the label's pioneering hip-hop releases. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winley_Records
- Winley Records was founded in 1956 on 125th Street in Harlem as a doo-wop label; Paul Winley got his start writing songs for his brother's group The Clovers and working with Atlantic artists like Ruth Brown and Big Joe Turner. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winley_Records
- The label pivoted into early hip-hop in 1979 — Paul Winley's daughters Tanya "Sweet Tee" Winley and Paulette Winley recorded rap for the label — making the same family/band that cut "Cheeba Cheeba" a direct bridge from doo-wop to rap. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winley_Records
