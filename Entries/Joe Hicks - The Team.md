### Open Research Questions

- **Joe Hicks's birth and death dates could not be established.** Wikipedia files him under "Year of birth missing (living people)" and "Possibly living people," and supplies no birth date, birthplace beyond San Francisco, or death date. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Hicks_(musician). Searches were run against obituary aggregators (Legacy.com), 45cat, Discogs artist page, Rate Your Music, the Light in the Attic Stone Flower reissue notes, and collector blogs (Mixtape Riot, Bentleyfunk, Zorba le Break). All confirm the same thing in different words: "information on Joe Hicks is incredibly scant" (https://mixtaperiot.com/wp/2006/04/the-mysterious-joe-hicks/). The name is also badly collided — there is a Mississippi Delta blues drummer named Joe Hicks who played with the Fieldstones, a Los Angeles civil-rights figure named Joe R. Hicks, and a currently active English singer-songwriter named Joe Hicks — which poisons most search results. **Suggested next step for the author:** the BMI repertoire database and the Stax/Concord archive (staxrecords.com) hold writer registrations for "That's the Way I Feel About Cha" and "Ruby Dean" that may carry a full legal name and dates; neither is web-searchable from here.
- **No track-level songwriter credit for "The Team" exists in any accessible database.** Discogs lists album-level credits only (https://www.discogs.com/release/12069998-Joe-Hicks-Mighty-Joe-Hicks); AllMusic's album page returns HTTP 403 to automated fetches. Since Hicks produced and arranged the LP himself and wrote or co-wrote elsewhere on it, self-authorship is the likely answer but is not sourced.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"The two-minute man."** The entire recorded legacy of Joe Hicks is one album and five singles, and the piece of it the world actually kept is the open drum break in the first thirty seconds of side one. Mixtape Riot notes with some irritation that online reviews of the album "focused narrowly on the delectable open drum break in the album's opener, largely dismissing the rest of the material" (https://mixtaperiot.com/wp/2006/04/the-mysterious-joe-hicks/). That is a whole argument about how the sampling era decides which musicians get remembered and for how many bars.
- **"Sly's last stablemate."** Hicks was the final artist on Sly Stone's Stone Flower label, and "Life and Death in G&A" is described by Light in the Attic as "one of the bleakest moments Sly Stone ever created on disc" (https://lightintheattic.net/products/i-m-just-like-you-sly-s-stone-flower-1969-70). Hicks was in the room at Sly's L.A. mansion during the *There's a Riot Goin' On* sessions. Three years later he made his own album — and it sounds nothing like Sly. The angle: what do you do after you've been inside somebody else's masterpiece?
- **"Named after a starship."** Enterprise Records was founded by Al Bell in late 1967 and named for the USS Enterprise from *Star Trek* (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterprise_Records). A track called "The Team," on a label named after a spaceship's crew, on a record that nobody bought. The label folded in 1975 without ever making money on anything but Isaac Hayes.
- **"The strangest band on a Stax record."** The rhythm section is a Burrito Brother, a Muscle Shoals founder, and two unknowns — a country-rock bassist and an Alabama session keyboardist cutting funk in Hollywood for a Memphis label. See Interest and Surprise, below.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- The first eight bars are just drums, and they are the only part of this record that ever became famous — a Bay Area rapper built a whole song on them twenty years later. Count how long the break runs before the band arrives. Would you have flipped it too?
- The bass on this track was played by Chris Ethridge, who co-wrote *The Gilded Palace of Sin* with Gram Parsons, and the keyboards by Barry Beckett of Muscle Shoals. Listen for the moment the groove tips toward Southern rock instead of funk. Can you hear the country-rock player in the pocket, or does he disappear into it completely?
- At 2:36 this is the shortest thing on the album, which closes with a nine-minute blues called "Ruby Dean." Play "The Team" and then play the last minute of "Ruby Dean." Is this the same record?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "The Team" is the opening track (A1) of *Mighty Joe Hicks*, running 2:36 — the shortest cut on an album that ends with a nine-minute closer. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/master/554788-Joe-Hicks-Mighty-Joe-Hicks
- The track opens with an exposed drum break, and that break is essentially the reason the record is remembered. Collectors describe the album as being reduced by reviewers to "the delectable open drum break" in its opener. — Source: https://mixtaperiot.com/wp/2006/04/the-mysterious-joe-hicks/
- Casual sampled "The Team" on "Thoughts of the Thoughtful," the sample landing at 0:01 of the rap track — i.e. the break is the record's first sound and the sample's first sound too. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/sample/478832/Casual-Thoughts-of-the-Thoughtful-Joe-Hicks-The-Team/
- Casual is a member of Hieroglyphics, the Oakland crew, and "Thoughts of the Thoughtful" appears on his debut *Fear Itself* (Jive, 1 February 1994), produced by Domino. So a San Francisco singer's forgotten 1973 break came back through the East Bay twenty-one years later. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear_Itself_(Casual_album), https://www.whosampled.com/sample/478832/Casual-Thoughts-of-the-Thoughtful-Joe-Hicks-The-Team/
- "The Team" was never issued as a 45. A full sweep of the Discogs release database for Joe Hicks turns up twenty 7" pressings across AGC, Scepter, Stone Flower and Enterprise, and none of them carry "The Team"; the only Enterprise single was "Ruby Dean" b/w "Train of Thought" (ENA-9081). — Sources: https://www.discogs.com/release/18553732-Joe-Hicks-Ruby-Dean-Train-Of-Thought, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Hicks_(musician)
- The track's modern streaming life runs entirely through a 1993 budget CD twofer, not the original LP: on Spotify, Apple Music and Deezer, "The Team" is track 1 of *Something Special*, a Stax reissue pairing all nine Hicks cuts with eleven by Jimmy Hughes. — Sources: https://www.discogs.com/release/12824759-Joe-Hicks-Jimmy-Hughes-Something-Special, https://open.spotify.com/track/0YtEqzEpI6VnJjmH5LyYnr

#### Album

- *Mighty Joe Hicks* (Enterprise ENS-1028, 1973) is the only album Joe Hicks ever released, and he produced and arranged it himself. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Hicks_(musician), https://www.discogs.com/release/12069998-Joe-Hicks-Mighty-Joe-Hicks
- It was recorded at Paramount Recording Studios in Hollywood — not at Stax in Memphis — and produced for Hickory Stick Productions, with Stax Records, Inc. holding the copyright. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/12069998-Joe-Hicks-Mighty-Joe-Hicks
- The band is a genuinely odd assembly for a Stax subsidiary: Chris Ethridge (bass), founding Flying Burrito Brother and Gram Parsons co-writer; Barry Beckett (keyboards), a founder of Muscle Shoals Sound Studio; Ken Khristian (guitar); Ed Byers (keyboards); Phil Parker and Ricky Holly splitting drums. — Sources: https://www.discogs.com/release/12069998-Joe-Hicks-Mighty-Joe-Hicks, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Ethridge, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Beckett
- One of the engineers was Kerry McNabb, alongside Reed Stanley; Hicks himself took a remix-engineer credit. Lacquers were cut by Larry Nix, the Stax mastering engineer whose "L. NIX" scrawl appears in the deadwax. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/12069998-Joe-Hicks-Mighty-Joe-Hicks
- A collector's account reports that Hicks credited Spooner Oldham in the album's liner notes, and hears Sly Stone's influence running deep in "Water Water." Treat this as a single-source claim — the liner text itself was not independently verified. — Source: https://mixtaperiot.com/wp/2006/04/the-mysterious-joe-hicks/
- The album is stylistically all over the place by design — one review calls it "wonderful, if a bit disjointed," ranging "from the psychedelic space-funker" to "almost Lynyrd Skynyrd-esque southern blues-rock," with heavy organ and drums throughout. Another describes "a mix of blues with a slight dose of soul" plus heavy rock guitar, Delta blues, and "occasional funkadelic elements." — Sources: https://mixtaperiot.com/wp/2006/04/the-mysterious-joe-hicks/, https://www.funkmysoul.gr/joe-hicks-1973-mighty-joe-hicks/
- Side two closes with "Ruby Dean," a nine-minute version of a song Hicks co-wrote with Bobby Womack for Womack's 1972 *Understanding*. Hicks was covering himself, by way of somebody else's hit album. — Sources: https://www.discogs.com/master/554788-Joe-Hicks-Mighty-Joe-Hicks, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Hicks_(musician)
- Enterprise Records was launched by Al Bell in late 1967 as a Stax subsidiary and named after the USS Enterprise from *Star Trek*, Bell's favourite show at the time. Outside of Isaac Hayes it was never a commercial success, and it folded in 1975. *Mighty Joe Hicks* sat at ENS-1028, between River City's *Anna Divina* and O.B. McClinton's *Obie from Senatobie*. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterprise_Records, https://www.bsnpubs.com/stax/enterprise.html
- The album was reissued in Japan by P-Vine on 10 July 1997 (PCD-4466) as a gatefold mini-LP replica with obi — a marker of how strong the Japanese collector demand for it was. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/4215118-Joe-Hicks-Mighty-Joe-Hicks

#### Artist

- Joe Hicks was an R&B and soul-blues singer and songwriter from San Francisco who, in Wikipedia's phrase, "found limited success in the late 1960s and early 1970s." Almost no biographical detail about him survives. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Hicks_(musician), https://mixtaperiot.com/wp/2006/04/the-mysterious-joe-hicks/
- His 1968 debut "Soul Meetin'" b/w "Don't It Make You Feel Funky" (AGC 0001) was produced by Pat Vegas of Redbone. The B-side later resurfaced on the 1995 compilation *A Treasure Chest of Northern Soul* and was reissued as a Kent Records 45 in 2019 — a second, separate act of rediscovery from the one that saved "The Team." — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Hicks_(musician), https://www.discogs.com/release/13918617-Joe-Hicks-Dont-It-Make-You-Feel-Funky
- Hicks was the last artist signed to Sly Stone's Stone Flower label. His "Home Sweet Home – Part II" (1969) was, per the Light in the Attic reissue notes, "the first released Stone Flower production," and his 1970 single "Life and Death in G&A" is called "one of the bleakest moments Sly Stone ever created on disc." — Sources: https://lightintheattic.net/products/i-m-just-like-you-sly-s-stone-flower-1969-70, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Hicks_(musician)
- He was present at Sly Stone's Los Angeles mansion during the recording of *There's a Riot Goin' On*, described in one account as "a singer everybody liked." — Source: https://www.funkmysoul.gr/joe-hicks-1973-mighty-joe-hicks/
- Hicks's biggest commercial moment was as a writer, not a singer: he co-wrote Bobby Womack's "That's the Way I Feel About Cha," released in spring 1972, which reached No. 2 R&B and No. 27 on the Billboard Hot 100. He also co-wrote "Simple Man" and "Ruby Dean" on Womack's *Understanding*. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/That%27s_The_Way_I_Feel_About_Cha, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Womack, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Hicks_(musician)
- He also wrote with Delaney Bramlett — "Sound of the City" and "I Know Something Good About You" both appear on Delaney & Bonnie & Friends' 1972 *D&B Together*. Between Sly Stone, Bobby Womack and Delaney & Bonnie, Hicks touched three completely different corners of early-70s American music and was famous in none of them. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Hicks_(musician)
- Wikipedia carries an explicit disambiguation warning: this Joe Hicks is not the Mississippi-raised Delta blues drummer who played with the Fieldstones, and not the English singer-songwriter releasing music since 2017. Fact-checkers should be aware that streaming platforms and search engines routinely conflate all three. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Hicks_(musician)

### Genre Suggestions

Current value: `Funk Rock`. Discogs files the album as Funk / Soul + Blues, style Soul (https://www.discogs.com/master/554788-Joe-Hicks-Mighty-Joe-Hicks). Measured against `Matter/On Subgenres.md`:

1. **Deep Funk — strongest fit.** The book defines deep funk as "less a sound than a commercial fate… tracks that were overlooked on release and only found their audience later through crate-digging DJs… If a song came from a well-known label, it came from a poorly selling record with limited distribution. The defining trait of deep funk is delayed recognition." That is this record exactly. Enterprise was a Stax imprint that never made money outside Isaac Hayes (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterprise_Records); the album vanished; its afterlife came entirely through the break, sampled by Casual in 1994 (https://www.whosampled.com/sample/478832/Casual-Thoughts-of-the-Thoughtful-Joe-Hicks-The-Team/) and reissued for Japanese collectors in 1997 (https://www.discogs.com/release/4215118-Joe-Hicks-Mighty-Joe-Hicks). Dealers still describe it as "a rare album of funky blues" (https://www.funkmysoul.gr/joe-hicks-1973-mighty-joe-hicks/).
2. **Funk Rock — defensible, and the author's current pick.** The book's definition covers rock-and-roll rhythm fused with funk, and reviewers hear exactly that on this LP: "heavy rock guitar work," "almost Lynyrd Skynyrd-esque southern blues-rock," heavy organ (https://www.funkmysoul.gr/joe-hicks-1973-mighty-joe-hicks/, https://mixtaperiot.com/wp/2006/04/the-mysterious-joe-hicks/). The band's makeup — a Flying Burrito Brother on bass, Muscle Shoals on keys — supports it structurally, not just sonically. The caveat is that most of that rock character sits elsewhere on the album; "The Team" itself is the tightest, most straightforwardly funky thing on it. If the genre tag is meant to describe the track rather than the record, Deep Funk is the better label.
3. **Classic Funk — worth considering, probably not right.** 1973 sits inside the book's 1969–1977 classic window, and Enterprise was a major-distributed imprint, so on paper it qualifies. But the book's classic-funk definition rests on "radio play, concert tours, and chart presence," and this record had none of the three. The commercial fate argues for Deep Funk over Classic Funk.

**Recommendation:** change to `Deep Funk`, keeping Funk Rock as the album-level descriptor in the prose. Not edited — genre is the author's field.

### Region Suggestions

Current value was empty; set to **`US - California`**, which appears verbatim in `Planning/Field Values.md`.

1. **US - California — recommended, and what was written.** Two independent strands point here. Hicks was from San Francisco (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Hicks_(musician)), and the album was recorded at Paramount Recording Studios in Hollywood (https://www.discogs.com/release/12069998-Joe-Hicks-Mighty-Joe-Hicks). His earlier records were Californian too: AGC produced by Pat Vegas of Redbone in Los Angeles, and Stone Flower, Sly Stone's own imprint. `Matter/On Regions.md` allows the tag to reflect where the artist is from, where the recording happened, or where the scene lived — here all three agree on California, which makes this an unusually clean call.
2. **US - Southeast — the case against.** The only thing pulling east is the label: Enterprise was a Stax subsidiary and the record carries a Stax Records, Inc. copyright out of Memphis (https://www.discogs.com/release/12069998-Joe-Hicks-Mighty-Joe-Hicks). But the Stax house band is nowhere on this record, it wasn't cut in Memphis, and the artist has no Memphis connection. Memphis is the address on the envelope, not the origin of the music. Rejected.
3. **A note, not a request to extend the enumeration.** If the author ever wants to distinguish Bay Area from Los Angeles funk, this entry is a good test case, because Hicks is a San Franciscan who made his one album in Hollywood and whose break was resurrected in Oakland. `US - California` flattens all of that, but the book explicitly avoids city-level tags (`Matter/On Regions.md`), so no new region value is proposed. The enumeration is fine as it stands.
