### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The man who replaced the irreplaceable"** — When Chano Pozo was murdered in a Harlem bar in 1948, Dizzy Gillespie needed a new conguero. Candido got the call and went on to outlast nearly everyone in jazz. This angle frames the track as one moment in an almost impossibly long career arc, from Havana milk-can drums to a 1973 funk record arranged by another Cuban legend.

2. **"One man, three drums"** — Candido invented the modern multi-conga setup out of sheer budget necessity: Carmen and Rolando's dance team could only afford one percussionist, so he played two drums at once. He later expanded to three, tuning them to pitches after watching New York Philharmonic timpanists. "Candido's Funk" is a showcase of what that innovation sounds like filtered through early-'70s Latin funk.

3. **"The funk trilogy's opening statement"** — *Drum Fever* was the final installment of an unofficial trilogy (*Thousand Finger Man*, *Beautiful*, *Drum Fever*) that tracked Candido's move from soul jazz into hard Latin funk. This theme lets you frame the track as the culmination of a three-album journey arranged by Chico O'Farrill, with blaxploitation-era wah-wah guitar meeting Afro-Cuban congas.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- "Candido's Funk" opens *Drum Fever* as Track A1, co-written by Candido and Chico O'Farrill. It features wah-wah guitar, staccato horn blasts, and Candido's signature multi-conga work over a Latin funk groove at 107 BPM. — Source: https://earcandymusic.biz/candido-drum-fever-12-vinyl/ and https://www.shazam.com/song/1443205872/candidos-funk
- The track was released as a 7" single for the first time in 2026 by Dynamite Cuts (backed with "Soulwanco"), limited to 500 copies. The label described it as "a percussive bass driven dancer with great latin horns." — Source: https://dynamitecuts.bandcamp.com/album/candido-candidos-funk-soulwanco-no-digital-download-vinyl-only
- Both "Candido's Funk" and "Soulwanco" from *Drum Fever* are noted as "bboy breaks, battle cut choice" in crate-digging circles. — Source: https://www.groovecollector.com/candido-drum-fever/release/434/

#### Album
- *Drum Fever* (1973, Polydor, catalog PD 5063) is the third and final installment of what collectors call Candido's "Funk Trilogy," following *Thousand Finger Man* (1970, Solid State) and *Beautiful* (1971, Blue Note). — Source: https://behardbop.blogspot.com/2018/03/candido-drum-fever.html
- Chico O'Farrill arranged and conducted the album. O'Farrill was himself born in 1921, the same year as Candido, making *Drum Fever* the product of two Cuban-born 1921 contemporaries collaborating in their early fifties. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chico_O'Farrill
- The album includes funked-up versions of two jazz standards: W.C. Handy's "St. Louis Blues" and Sonny Rollins' "St. Thomas." — Source: https://behardbop.blogspot.com/2018/03/candido-drum-fever.html
- *Drum Fever* was reissued on vinyl (Soul Jazz Records/Polydor reissue) and tracks from the album later appeared on compilations including *The Verve Grooves, Vol. 1* (2010). — Source: https://www.deezer.com/en/track/6603863

#### Artist
- Candido pioneered playing two conga drums simultaneously out of pure economic necessity: when he came to New York in 1946 with dance team Carmen and Rolando, there was only budget for one conguero. He played both a conga and a quinto, keeping the groove on one hand while improvising with the other. This technique, now standard worldwide, was invented on the spot. — Source: https://drummagazine.com/candido-the-father-of-modern-conga-drumming/
- In 1950 at the Apollo Theater, Candido debuted three congas tuned to specific pitches, inspired by watching New York Philharmonic timpanists. This turned the conga from a purely rhythmic instrument into a melodic one. — Source: https://drummagazine.com/candido-the-father-of-modern-conga-drumming/
- After Chano Pozo was murdered in a Harlem bar in 1948, Dizzy Gillespie contacted Candido as a replacement. Though Candido "passed the test," language difficulties delayed him joining the band for over a year. He eventually toured with Gillespie and performed on "Manteca." — Source: https://cubaonrecord.substack.com/p/candido-camero-latin-fire
- Candido recorded the theme for *I Love Lucy* with Marco Rizo and Desi Arnaz. — Source: https://cubaonrecord.substack.com/p/candido-camero-latin-fire
- He performed alongside Chano Pozo in the Afro-Cuban show *Pantera* at Havana's Tropicana before either came to New York. — Source: https://cubaonrecord.substack.com/p/candido-camero-latin-fire
- He also invented a foot-operated cowbell and an attached guiro, allowing one person to replicate three percussionists simultaneously while also singing. — Source: https://www.congachops.com/blog-articles/percussion-profiles-candido-camero
- Candido was named an NEA Jazz Master in 2008 and received the Latin Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award in 2009. — Source: https://www.arts.gov/honors/jazz/candido-camero
- A documentary, *Candido: Hands of Fire* (dir. Ivan Acosta, 68 min), was released in 2006. — Source: https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0899267/
- He died peacefully in his sleep on November 7, 2020, at age 99, at his home in New York City. He had performed into his 90s, including a tribute concert for Cachao in 2019 at age 97. — Source: https://www.wbgo.org/show/latin-jazz-cruise/2020-11-07/remembering-candido-camero-percussionist-and-afro-cuban-pioneer-who-has-died-at-99
- His advice on longevity: "Don't smoke, don't use drugs, don't take bad advice or be in bad company." — Source: https://cubaonrecord.substack.com/p/candido-camero-latin-fire
- Later in the 1970s, Candido pivoted to disco with Salsoul Records. His 1979 album *Dancin' and Prancin'* became hugely influential on the Chicago and Detroit house scenes, and his version of "Jingo" (originally by Olatunji) reached #55 on the UK chart. — Source: http://discodelivery.blogspot.com/2014/02/liner-notes-candido-dancin-prancin-1979.html

### Genre Suggestions

The `genre` field currently contains: Latin Funk.

1. **Latin Funk** (current value, strong fit) — This is the most accurate genre. The track combines Afro-Cuban conga patterns with funk instrumentation (wah-wah guitar, staccato horns, electric keyboards) in a tight groove. The Candido-O'Farrill partnership is quintessentially Latin musicians making funk. Groovecollector categorizes the album under "Salsa/Pachanga/Mambo" and "Funk 70s/Soul Funk Bands/Early Funk/Rare Groove." — Source: https://www.groovecollector.com/candido-drum-fever/release/434/

2. **Jazz-Funk** — AllMusic tags *Drum Fever* under "Bop, Global Jazz, Latin Jazz," and the Be Hard Bop blog categorizes it alongside jazz-funk. Candido is fundamentally a jazz musician, the album includes jazz standard covers ("St. Thomas," "St. Louis Blues"), and the arrangements by Chico O'Farrill come from a jazz tradition. The funk here grows out of jazz, not the other way around. — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/drum-fever-mw0000901640

3. **Soul Jazz** — The earlier albums in the trilogy (*Thousand Finger Man*, *Beautiful*) are closer to soul jazz, and *Drum Fever* retains elements of that tradition: organ-driven grooves, small-group arrangements, and a danceable pocket. But the wah-wah guitar and horn stabs push it further into funk territory than pure soul jazz. — Source: https://behardbop.blogspot.com/2018/03/candido-drum-fever.html

**Recommendation:** Latin Funk remains the best primary label, as the Latin percussion is the defining element that separates this from generic jazz-funk. If dual-tagging is possible, Latin Funk + Jazz-Funk would be most accurate.
