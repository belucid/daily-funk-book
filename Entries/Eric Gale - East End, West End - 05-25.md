You've been hearing Eric Gale your whole life, even if you didn't know it. He was New York’s most recorded session guitarists of the 1970s and 1980s, credited on more than 500 albums, including records by Aretha Franklin, Paul Simon, and Bob Marley.

Gale almost passed up a career in music. He earned a master’s degree in chemistry and taught at Penn State before walking away from his Ph.D. program to play guitar full-time. He was fluent in English, German, Spanish, chemistry, and guitar. 

*Ginseng Woman* was the work of his late-1970s New York session crew: Richard Tee, Gary King, Steve Gadd, Ralph MacDonald, Grover Washington Jr., and the Brecker Brothers. “East End, West End” is the closing track, composed by Gale himself, with Bob James arranging the horns and strings.

Gale died of lung cancer on May 25, 1994, at the age of 55.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The session king steps out"** — Gale was one of the most-recorded session guitarists in New York, credited on an estimated 500 albums with everyone from King Curtis and Aretha Franklin to Paul Simon and Bob Marley. "East End, West End" is the rare moment where the hired hand writes the tune, produces it with Bob James, and gets to solo for six minutes on his own record. The angle: what does a career sideman do when the spotlight finally turns on him? (Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Gale)

2. **"Chemistry major, funk guitarist"** — Gale earned a master's in chemistry from Niagara University and taught at Penn State before choosing music. He was fluent in German and Spanish. A writing hook on the unlikely path from the lab to the Mediasound control room and how that precision maybe shows up in his clean, economical phrasing. (Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Gale)

3. **"The Stuff players, just under a different name"** — Ginseng Woman is effectively a Stuff record with orchestration: Gale, Richard Tee, Gary King, Steve Gadd, Ralph MacDonald, plus Grover Washington Jr. and the Brecker brothers. The angle is the tight New York session family of the late '70s, and how this track documents a room full of players who could read each other's breathing. (Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuff_(band), https://www.sessiondays.com/2023/03/1977-eric-gale-ginseng-woman/)

### Listening Mission Suggestions

Current mission (keep): "Listen to the expert interplay between the bass and guitar during the guitar solo. Notice how the bass fills in the spaces without stepping on the guitar. Does it sound like a guitar solo or a bass solo, or both?"

Alternates:

1. Gale's solo is famously unshowy. Count the notes he doesn't play. Where a rock guitarist would fill every bar, Gale leaves silence for Gary King's bass and Grover Washington's sax to breathe. Does the restraint make the solo more or less funky?

2. Bob James wrote the horn and string arrangements. Notice the moment the strings swell in, then back off. Do they add to the groove, or smooth it into easy-listening territory? Where is the line?

3. Grover Washington Jr. takes a short sax break on this track. Compare how he phrases against Gale's guitar: two session giants on the same cut, trading feel over flash.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "East End, West End" is the closing track on Ginseng Woman, composed by Gale himself, with Bob James arranging horns and strings. Grover Washington Jr. plays saxophone on the cut — one of only two tracks on the album where he appears. Source: https://www.sessiondays.com/2023/03/1977-eric-gale-ginseng-woman/

#### Album

- Ginseng Woman was Gale's fourth solo album and his first for Columbia, produced by Bob James, with associate producer Joe Jorgensen. Source: https://www.sessiondays.com/2023/03/1977-eric-gale-ginseng-woman/
- Recorded at Mediasound and Columbia Recording Studios in NYC, mixed at Mediasound, and mastered at Columbia by Vlado Meller. Source: https://www.sessiondays.com/2023/03/1977-eric-gale-ginseng-woman/
- The personnel is effectively the A-list of mid-'70s New York session players: Gale, Bob James (synth, Rhodes, piano), Richard Tee (piano), Gary King (bass), Anthony Jackson (bass on title track), Steve Gadd (drums on tracks 1, 2, 4), Ralph MacDonald (percussion), Michael and Randy Brecker (saxes/trumpet), Grover Washington Jr. (sax), plus vocalists Patti Austin and Lani Groves. Source: https://www.sessiondays.com/2023/03/1977-eric-gale-ginseng-woman/
- One of the tracks is a cover of Hall & Oates' "Sara Smile," which had been a US #4 hit the previous year — a nod to mid-'70s crossover instincts. Source: https://www.sessiondays.com/2023/03/1977-eric-gale-ginseng-woman/
- Apple Music lists an album release date of October 3, 1977; Sessiondays and soulandfunkmusic.com list the original LP release as March 1977. The October date likely reflects a later pressing or the digital catalog date; catalog number is Columbia PC 34421. Sources: https://music.apple.com/us/album/ginseng-woman/921846500, https://www.soulandfunkmusic.com/album/eric_gale/ginseng_woman

#### Artist

- Eric Gale was born September 20, 1938 in Bedford-Stuyvesant, Brooklyn, to an American mother and a British father. He died May 25, 1994 of lung cancer at age 55 in Baja California, Mexico. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Gale
- Before music, Gale earned a master's degree in chemistry from Niagara University and taught at Penn State; he walked away from a Ph.D. track to play guitar full time. He was fluent in German and Spanish. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Gale
- He was a founding member of the New York studio-musician supergroup Stuff, alongside Richard Tee, Cornell Dupree, Gordon Edwards, and (later) Steve Gadd. Stuff supported Joe Cocker's Stingray tour and appeared on Saturday Night Live in its second season. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuff_(band)
- Gale appeared on an estimated 500 albums as a session guitarist, including Van McCoy's "The Hustle" (1975) and Grover Washington Jr.'s "Just the Two of Us." His client list includes Aretha Franklin, Paul Simon, Quincy Jones, Bob Marley, Peter Tosh, Nina Simone, Lena Horne, Billy Joel, and the Fania All-Stars. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Gale
- He was recommended for studio work early on by King Curtis and Jimmy Smith, who took him under their wing in the early 1960s. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Gale
- His 1975 solo album Negril was recorded at Harry J's Studio in Kingston, Jamaica with reggae heavyweights including Aston "Familyman" Barrett and Peter Tosh. Because Gale was under contract to CTI, his credit on the cover read "courtesy of C.T.I." and later reissues misleadingly sold the album as "Peter Tosh & Friends." Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negril_(album)

### Genre Suggestions

Current: Jazz-Funk. This is a strong fit and should stand.

1. **Jazz-Funk (recommended, keep)** — Per the book's working definition, jazz-funk is "funk played by jazz musicians...longer track lengths, room for extended improvisation, and a rhythm section built to support soloists...less on vocal choruses." "East End, West End" is a 6:08 instrumental with a full horn/string arrangement, a session band from the jazz tradition, and a full-length guitar solo. Textbook fit. Source: Matter/On Genres & Regions.md; Ginseng Woman tracklist.
2. **Soul Jazz (alternate)** — Possible but weaker fit. The book's definition places soul jazz in the "earlier, smaller-ensemble" bucket, often organ-trio based. "East End, West End" is a big-band production with strings and multiple horns — it's past the soul jazz boil and into jazz-funk territory. Not recommended.
3. **Deep Funk (alternate)** — Ginseng Woman was a major-label Columbia release with full distribution and a big producer (Bob James). It's not deep funk in the "delayed recognition" sense, though "East End, West End" is certainly an overlooked album cut from a respected session artist. Also not the right genre per the book's definition.

**Recommendation:** Keep Jazz-Funk.
