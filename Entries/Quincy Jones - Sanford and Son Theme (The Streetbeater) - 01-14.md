Friday, January 14, 1972, marked the premiere of *Sanford and Son*. Critics saw Redd Foxx’s Fred Sanford character as NBC’s answer to Archie Bunker from *All in the Family*, which aired directly before it. *Sanford and Son* finished the 1971-1972 season as sixth best in the ratings. It ran for six seasons, ending in 1977, and it's been in syndication ever since.

It is hard to overstate how segregated American music still was in 1972. Black radio and white radio operated in separate spheres, as did the record stores and concert circuits. Today’s song, “Sanford and Son Theme (The Streetbeater)” by Quincy Jones, may have been the first funk millions of white Americans heard every week, not by choosing a station or buying a record, but because it aired before a sitcom.

Jones wrote the theme before seeing the show. He based it on his friendship with Foxx and his familiarity with Foxx's humor.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

Current writing theme: "Funk in TV (mention Seinfeld)"

1. **"I just wrote what he looked like"** — Jones didn't watch the Sanford and Son pilot. He knew Redd Foxx from 30 years on the Chitlin' Circuit, thought Foxx was too blue for TV, and instead of studying the show, he just wrote a portrait of his friend in 20 minutes. "It sounds just like him, doesn't it?" The angle: can music be a portrait? What does it mean that Jones captured a junkyard, a neighborhood, and a personality in a three-minute instrumental written in the time it takes to eat lunch?

2. **"Funk in your living room"** — This was one of the first pieces of funk that tens of millions of Americans heard every week, not by choosing to buy a record but because it played before a sitcom. The Streetbeater smuggled funk into Middle America's Friday nights on NBC. The Seinfeld connection still works here: both shows used funky instrumentals (one real, one synthesized) as the sonic identity of a sitcom, decades apart.

3. **"The session that played everything"** — The musicians on this track and album read like a who's who of 1970s LA session royalty: Chuck Rainey (Steely Dan, Aretha Franklin), Carol Kaye (Beach Boys, Motown), James Gadson (Bill Withers), Dave Grusin, Phil Woods, Ernie Watts, and Tommy Morgan, whose harmonica also opens The Rockford Files and graces Pet Sounds. The angle: the invisible army of session players who defined the sound of an era.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. "The main melody is played on multiple harmonicas layered together by Tommy Morgan, the same session player behind Pet Sounds and The Rockford Files. Can you hear where one harmonica ends and another begins, or does it just sound like one impossibly full instrument?"

2. "Jones said he wrote this in 20 minutes without watching the pilot, just picturing his old friend Redd Foxx. Listen to the looseness, the swagger, the slight wobble in the groove. Does it sound composed, or does it sound like someone walking down a street?"

3. "Pay attention to the drums and the tube shaker holding down the rhythm underneath the harmonica. Strip the melody away in your mind. Is that pocket funky enough to stand on its own?"

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- Jones composed "The Streetbeater" in about 20 minutes and recorded it in about the same amount of time, with just four musicians. He didn't watch the pilot first, saying "I just wrote what he looked like...raggedy, just like Foxx." — Source: https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/quincy-jones-genius-behind-themes-210706099.html
- The main melody is a composite performed on multiple harmonicas layered together by Tommy Morgan, not a single harmonica take. Jones specified using a tube shaker and a bass harmonica as key textures. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanford_and_Son_Theme_(The_Streetbeater)
- The full track runs 3:06, but the TV theme version used only 0:51 of it. Most people who know the theme have never heard the full recording. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanford_and_Son_Theme_(The_Streetbeater)
- Ranked #9 on Rolling Stone's reader poll of best television theme songs. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanford_and_Son_Theme_(The_Streetbeater)
- The track has been sampled by DJ Jazzy Jeff & the Fresh Prince ("Rock the House"), Nice & Smooth ("Step by Step"), MC Shy D ("I've Got to Be Tough"), M.I.A. ("U.R.A.Q.T."), and RZA featuring Ol' Dirty Bastard ("Old Man"). — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Quincy-Jones/The-Streetbeater-(Theme-From-Sanford-and-Son)/sampled/
- Jones created an updated version for his 2010 album Q Soul Bossa Nostra featuring T.I., B.o.B, Prince Charlez, and Mohombi. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/sample/95150/Quincy-Jones-T.I.-B.o.B-Prince-Charlez-Mohombi-Sanford-and-Son-Quincy-Jones-The-Streetbeater-(Theme-From-Sanford-and-Son)/
- The single was released on A&M Records (catalog number 1455) with "Summer in the City" as the B-side. — Source: https://www.45cat.com/record/1455us
- The track failed to chart on the Billboard Hot 100 despite becoming one of the most recognized instrumentals in American television. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanford_and_Son_Theme_(The_Streetbeater)

#### Album
- You've Got It Bad Girl (October 1973, A&M Records) hit #1 on the Jazz Albums chart, #14 on the R&B Albums chart, and #94 on the Billboard 200. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/You've_Got_It_Bad_Girl
- The album's opening track, a cover of The Lovin' Spoonful's "Summer in the City," won the Grammy Award for Best Instrumental Arrangement at the 16th Annual Grammy Awards, beating out Chick Corea's "Spain." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/You've_Got_It_Bad_Girl
- "Superstition" on the album features vocals by Bill Withers, Billy Preston, and Stevie Wonder together, billed as "Three Beautiful Brothers." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/You've_Got_It_Bad_Girl
- Another track, "Chump Change," served as the theme for The New Bill Cosby Show on CBS, meaning the album contains two TV themes. — Source: https://www.sessiondays.com/2020/08/1973-quincy-jones-youve-got-it-bad-girl/
- Recorded across five studios: A&M Studios, Sun West Studios, and The Record Plant in Hollywood; The Burbank Studios in Burbank; and A&R Studios in New York. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/You've_Got_It_Bad_Girl
- The session personnel is staggering: Chuck Rainey and Carol Kaye on bass, Dave Grusin and George Duke and Bob James on keyboards, Phil Woods and Ernie Watts on saxophone, Hubert Laws on flute, Toots Thielemans and Tommy Morgan on harmonica, Cat Anderson on trumpet, Bobbye Hall on percussion, and Grady Tate on drums. — Source: https://www.sessiondays.com/2020/08/1973-quincy-jones-youve-got-it-bad-girl/

#### Artist
- Quincy Delight Jones Jr. (March 14, 1933, Chicago, Illinois, to November 3, 2024, Bel Air, Los Angeles). Cause of death: pancreatic cancer, age 91. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quincy_Jones
- Born on the South Side of Chicago, moved to Bremerton, Washington at age 10, then to Seattle where he attended Garfield High School and found music. He said, "Moving to Seattle forever changed me for the better. Finding music here showed me that I could be more than a statistic." — Source: https://komonews.com/news/local/quincy-jones-music-grammy-ray-charles-garfield-high-school-bremerton-pnw-pacific-northwest
- Won 28 Grammy Awards during a seven-decade career, plus a Primetime Emmy and a Tony Award, with nominations for seven Academy Awards. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quincy_Jones
- Jones knew Redd Foxx from the Chitlin' Circuit roughly 30 years before Sanford and Son, including venues like the Apollo Theater. He initially doubted the show could work because Foxx was too blue for television: "There's not one word he said that could go on national television." — Source: https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/quincy-jones-genius-behind-themes-210706099.html
- Jones was a prolific TV and film composer: Ironside (one of the first synthesizer-based TV themes), The Bill Cosby Show ("Hikky Burr"), In the Heat of the Night (1967 film score), The Italian Job, In Cold Blood, Roots, and later The Fresh Prince of Bel-Air. — Source: https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/quincy-jones-genius-behind-themes-210706099.html
- Tommy Morgan (December 4, 1932 to June 23, 2022), the harmonica player on this track, played on over 7,000 recording sessions and over 500 feature films. He also performed on The Beach Boys' Pet Sounds and "Good Vibrations," and played the harmonica on The Rockford Files theme. He received a Special Lifetime Achievement Award from The Society for the Preservation and Advancement of the Harmonica in 2001. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tommy_Morgan
- James Gadson, credited as drummer on this track, was also the drummer on Bill Withers' Still Bill, Marvin Gaye's "I Want You," and later D'Angelo's "Sugah Daddy." He played in the first lineup of Charles Wright's Watts 103rd Street Rhythm Band. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Gadson
- Chuck Rainey, the bassist, played on over 1,000 albums, including every track on Steely Dan's Aja (except "Deacon Blues") and Aretha Franklin's "Rock Steady." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Rainey

### Genre Suggestions

1. **Classic Funk** (current value) — Strong fit. The track is a groove-driven instrumental built on a funky bass line, drums, and harmonica, released during the core classic funk era (1972-1973). It was commercially released on a major label (A&M) and reached a massive audience through television. It sits comfortably in the classic funk vocabulary. — Source: genre definition in On Genres & Regions.md

2. **Jazz-Funk** — Also defensible. The album You've Got It Bad Girl is classified as jazz on AllMusic, and the session players (Phil Woods, Dave Grusin, Ernie Watts, Hubert Laws) are jazz musicians. The track has jazz-adjacent instrumentation and was produced by a musician (Jones) whose primary identity at this point in his career was as a jazz arranger and bandleader. However, the track itself leans more funk than jazz: it's short (3:06), groove-first, and built on a repeating riff rather than improvisation. — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/youve-got-it-bad-girl-mw0000809156

3. **Deep Funk** — A stretch, but interesting to consider. The single failed to chart, and the full 3:06 track is relatively unknown compared to the 51-second TV edit. Most listeners have never heard the full version. That said, the track was released on a major label by a major artist, which doesn't fit the typical deep funk profile of regional obscurities. Not recommended. — Source: genre definition in On Genres & Regions.md

**Recommendation:** Classic Funk is the right call. The track's identity is funk-first, commercially released, and from the core era.
