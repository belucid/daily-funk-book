1972’s *The World Is a Ghetto* was the best-selling album of 1973 according to *Billboard*, making War one of the biggest acts in American music. Their follow-up, *Why Can’t We Be Friends?*, did not arrive until 1975 due to a dispute with the label. Even with the delay, it reached #8 on the *Billboard* 200 and #1 on the R&B chart.

The album’s hit single “Low Rider” was issued as one of the first promotional 12-inch records for club DJs. That 12-inch also included today’s track, “Heartbeat.” Although “Heartbeat” was not itself a single from the album, the extra space on the single and the dance-floor context helped it reach the Top 10 on the dance chart.

War demonstrates how the groove works by building it up over the first 30 seconds: just guitar, then bass, then drums, then percussion, before the vocals enter and the horns respond. You're probably familiar with Sly Stone's version of the same trick from 1967's *Dance to the Music*.

Five years after the single's release, on June 4, 1980, War’s saxophonist Charles Miller was stabbed to death during a botched robbery in Los Angeles. His iconic deep voice was a big part of "Low Rider." No one was ever arrested for his murder.

### Open Research Questions

**Q: Was *The World Is a Ghetto* really the best-selling album of 1973?**

**A: Yes.** *The World Is a Ghetto* sat at #1 on Billboard's Year-End Top Pop Albums chart for 1973, with Seals & Crofts' *Summer Breeze* at #2, Stevie Wonder's *Talking Book* at #3, Carly Simon's *No Secrets* at #4, and the *Lady Sings the Blues* soundtrack at #5. — Source: https://bestsellingalbums.org/year-end/Billboard_Top_Albums_1973

Billboard confirmed the claim in its own coverage: per the December 29, 1973 issue, the album was "the top pop album of the year," beating out Seals & Crofts, Stevie Wonder, and Carly Simon. — Source: https://www.billboard.com/music/rb-hip-hop/war-the-world-is-a-ghetto-billboard-chart-1235551303/

Worth noting for accuracy: the album only spent two weeks at #1 on the weekly Billboard 200 (February 10 and 17, 1973), but it was the highest-grossing album across the whole calendar year. Some online sources erroneously give year-end 1973's top slot to Elton John's *Goodbye Yellow Brick Road*, but that album was released in October 1973 and actually topped the year-end 1974 chart, not 1973. The "best-selling album of 1973" claim in the entry body is accurate. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_Is_a_Ghetto

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The album track that out-funked the hits"** — "Heartbeat" is a seven-minute jam buried on Side A of an album famous for "Low Rider" and the title track, yet it was the one that crossed over to the dance charts. This frames the entry around War's deep catalog being stronger than their singles, a thesis supported by critics. (See the war.com article arguing "War Is Much Better Than Its Hits.")

2. **"Seven people, one pulse"** — War's signature was that all seven members played percussion alongside their primary instrument, creating an unusually thick rhythmic bed. "Heartbeat" is the track where that philosophy is most literal: the title is the pulse, and every player locks into it. This frames the entry around War's collective identity and the idea that funk is a group conversation, not a solo act.

3. **"The promo that launched a format"** — "Heartbeat" was paired with "Low Rider" on one of the earliest promotional 12-inch singles, released before the 12-inch format became standard in clubs. This frames the entry around the physical format history of dance music and War's role at the intersection of funk and early DJ culture.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- "Heartbeat" was released as a promo-only 12-inch single paired with "Low Rider" (United Artists, catalog IDJ 9), one of the earliest promotional 12-inch singles in funk. The promo version runs 6:58, slightly shorter than the album cut at 7:25. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/2026507-War-Low-Rider-Heartbeat
- The track achieved a Top 10 position on the Billboard dance charts despite never being released as a commercial single. — Source: https://ultimateclassicrock.com/war-why-cant-we-be-friends/
- "Heartbeat" has been sampled in over 20 tracks, including by Ice-T ("Heartbeat"), Nice & Smooth ("Funky for You"), and Twista ("Hokus Pokus"). — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/War/Heartbeat/
- The 2025 50th anniversary reissue of *Why Can't We Be Friends?* includes a previously unreleased "Original Jam" version of "Heartbeat," suggesting the studio recording was edited down from an even longer session. — Source: https://ultimateclassicrock.com/war-why-cant-we-be-friends-expanded-reissue/
- Bass educator Paul Thompson (No Treble) has called the "Heartbeat" bass line one of War's greatest, analyzing B.B. Dickerson's playing in a dedicated video breakdown. — Source: https://www.notreble.com/buzz/2024/03/08/heartbeat-breaking-down-wars-greatest-bass-line/

#### Album
- *Why Can't We Be Friends?* was delayed nearly two years by a legal dispute between War's production company (Far Out Productions) and United Artists Records. — Source: https://ultimateclassicrock.com/war-why-cant-we-be-friends/
- The album reached #8 on the Billboard 200, #1 on the R&B chart, and broke into the Jazz Top 20. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Why_Can%27t_We_Be_Friends%3F
- Both singles from the album ("Why Can't We Be Friends?" and "Low Rider") received Grammy nominations in 1976. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Why_Can%27t_We_Be_Friends%3F
- "Low Rider" was famously recorded in a single take. Lonnie Jordan recalled that Harold Brown started the beat, the others joined in, and "that was the only take there ever was." — Source: https://ultimateclassicrock.com/war-why-cant-we-be-friends/
- NASA played the title track "Why Can't We Be Friends?" to the astronauts and cosmonauts during the Apollo-Soyuz docking in July 1975, the first joint US-Soviet space mission. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo%E2%80%93Soyuz
- The album was recorded at multiple studios: Wally Heider Recording (Hollywood and San Francisco), Crystal Studios (Hollywood), and Far Out Studios (Hollywood), then mixed at Sound City in Van Nuys. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Why_Can%27t_We_Be_Friends%3F

#### Artist
- War formed in Long Beach, California in 1969 out of a band called the Creators (formed 1962), which became Nightshift. Producer Jerry Goldstein discovered them backing football player/singer Deacon Jones at the Rag Doll club in North Hollywood. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)
- War's lineup was notably multi-ethnic: Black, Mexican-American, and Danish (Lee Oskar), reflecting the diverse neighborhoods of South LA where the members grew up. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)
- *The World Is a Ghetto* (1972) was the best-selling album of 1973 according to Billboard, making War one of the biggest acts in American music at their peak. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)
- Every member of War played percussion in addition to their primary instrument, creating their signature layered rhythmic density. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Why_Can%27t_We_Be_Friends%3F (personnel credits)
- Saxophonist Charles Miller was stabbed to death in a botched robbery in Hollywood on June 4, 1980. The case remains unsolved. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Miller_(musician)
- Percussionist Papa Dee Allen died of a brain aneurysm onstage on August 30, 1988. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)
- Lee Oskar, born in Copenhagen, Denmark, arrived in the US as a young man and later founded the Lee Oskar Harmonicas company, now a major manufacturer. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Oskar
- War's music has been sampled so extensively in hip-hop that a 1992 compilation called *Rap Declares War* was released to document it. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)
- On June 5, 2025, War received a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)
- Four original members (Howard Scott, Lee Oskar, Harold Brown, B.B. Dickerson) broke away from manager Jerry Goldstein in 1996 and eventually formed the Lowrider Band. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)

### Genre Suggestions

1. **Classic Funk (current value)** — Strong fit. War is one of the defining classic funk bands of the 1970s, with major-label distribution, chart success, and radio play. "Heartbeat" appeared on a gold-certified album that hit #1 on the R&B chart. The track is a seven-member ensemble jam rooted squarely in the funk vocabulary of the era. This is the most natural and defensible choice. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)

2. **Latin Funk** — Reasonable alternative. War's sound consistently blended funk with Latin percussion, and the personnel credits for this album show congas, bongos, and timbales prominently. The album as a whole leans into Latin flavors (tracks like "Leroy's Latin Lament" and "In Mazatlan"). Whether "Heartbeat" itself foregrounds the Latin elements enough to warrant this tag over Classic Funk is a judgment call for the author. — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/why-cant-we-be-friends--mw0000172112 (styles listed include "Latin Rock")

3. **Psychedelic Funk** — Weaker fit but worth considering. War's early work with Eric Burdon had psychedelic overtones, and the extended jam format of "Heartbeat" has a loose, exploratory quality. However, the track lacks the surreal imagery, alter egos, and hallucinatory storytelling that define psychedelic funk in this book's framework. Classic Funk is a better call.
