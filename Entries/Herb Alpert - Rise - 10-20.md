I can safely say that my introduction to funk was Prince, but it's also safe to say my gateway to jazz-funk was today's track, "Rise," by Herb Alpert. "Rise" reached No. 1 on the *Billboard* Hot 100 on this day in 1979, and it was my father’s favorite song. I must have heard it 500 times or more growing up. Though my siblings grew to hate the whole *Rise* album, I never tired of it. I've always understood my father's obsession, and apparently I'm in very good company. Notorious B.I.G. sampled "Rise" for his 1997 hit "Hypnotize" because he remembered hearing it everywhere in New York City as a ten-year-old.

Herb Alpert's discography can seem almost absurdly large for a trumpet player, but the explanation is simple: he was the A in A&M Records. In September 1962, Alpert co-founded the label with Jerry Moss, the M in the name. A&M went on to enormous success with artists including the Police, Janet Jackson, Cat Stevens, Joe Cocker, Supertramp, and Bryan Adams. In 1989, Alpert and Moss sold the label to PolyGram for $500 million, then later sued the new owners for breach of contract and settled for another $200 million. So make sure to get your kids trumpet lessons.

It is hard to pin down the exact number of Herb Alpert singles and albums on A&M, but between his solo records and those credited to Herb Alpert & the Tijuana Brass, there were hundreds. Twenty-eight of his albums reached the *Billboard* 200, and most of his catalog is on the label he helped build.

**A&M Records: Key Details**

Herb Alpert co-founded A&M Records with Jerry Moss in September 1962. The label was originally called Carnival Records but was renamed after they discovered the name was taken, using the initials of their last names (Alpert & Moss). The label started in Alpert's garage. In 1966 they moved into the historic Charlie Chaplin Studios lot on La Brea in Hollywood. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%26M_Records

Their first release was "The Lonely Bull" by Herb Alpert & the Tijuana Brass (August 1962), which reached #6 on the Billboard chart and sold ~700,000 copies. Within a decade, A&M became the world's largest independent record label. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%26M_Records

The label's roster eventually included The Police, Sting, Janet Jackson, the Carpenters, Cat Stevens, Joe Cocker, Supertramp, Peter Frampton, and Bryan Adams, among many others.

**The Sale:** In October 1989, PolyGram (a subsidiary of Philips) purchased A&M for $500 million in cash (excluding real estate and the Almo-Irving Music publishing arm, which was valued at an additional ~$200 million). Alpert and Moss continued to manage the label until 1993. — Source: https://www.upi.com/Archives/1989/09/15/PolyGram-buys-AM-Records-for-500-million/9559621835200/

**The Lawsuit:** The sale included an integrity clause requiring A&M's identity to be maintained for 20 years. After Universal Music Group acquired PolyGram in 1998 and folded A&M into Interscope Records, shuttering the La Brea offices, Alpert and Moss sued for breach of contract and settled for an additional $200 million. — Source: https://variety.com/1999/biz/news/a-m-duo-can-sue-u-music-1117750529/

**How Many Albums on A&M?** An exact count is difficult to pin down. The On A&M Records discography database lists 1,073 recordings for "Herb Alpert" (solo) and 2,006 recordings for "Herb Alpert & the Tijuana Brass," but these include singles, compilations, and international pressings across all formats. Wikipedia states 28 of his albums charted on the Billboard 200, with five reaching #1. Various sources cite "over forty albums" across his career. He recorded on A&M from 1962 through the late 1980s, then again on other labels after the sale. The vast majority of his catalog was released on A&M. — Source: https://www.onamrecords.com/artists/herb-alpert/discography and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herb_Alpert

### Writing Theme Suggestions

Current writing theme: "Pays to Own the Record Label"

This is a strong theme. Here are alternatives to consider:

1. **"The drummer who slowed it down"** — The story of Steve Schaeffer suggesting they drop the tempo from 120 to 100 BPM is a perfect narrative hook. One musician's instinct in the studio transformed an uptempo dance track into a slow-burning funk landmark. It speaks to the power of groove over speed.

2. **"From garage to $500 million"** — A variation on the current theme, but with sharper framing. Alpert started A&M in his garage, built the world's largest independent label, released "Rise" on it, scored a #1 hit on his own company, then sold the whole operation for half a billion dollars. It's a story about artistic control and its rewards.

3. **"Soap opera funk"** — The General Hospital connection is wild and dark. "Rise" became a #1 hit partly because it was played repeatedly during a rape storyline on the most popular soap opera in America. The song took on an unintended meaning that boosted its chart performance. It's a complicated, surprising angle that most listeners don't know.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- "Rise" was originally an uptempo dance track at 120 BPM. Drummer Steve Schaeffer insisted they try slowing it to 100 BPM during the session. The slower tempo, in Alpert's words, made it "really funky." — Source: https://herbalpert.com/classic-track-rise-herb-alpert/
- It was one of the first pop recordings to use 3M's 32-track digital recording machine. Three engineers from 3M stood in the control room during the session because the technology was so new and unreliable. They kept an MCI 24-track analog machine running as backup because digital editing didn't exist yet. — Source: https://herbalpert.com/classic-track-rise-herb-alpert/
- The layered handclap sound was created by overdubbing 6-8 times with multiple musicians standing in different positions, then processing through an Eventide Harmonizer during mixing. — Source: https://herbalpert.com/classic-track-rise-herb-alpert/
- British club DJs accidentally played imported 33 RPM pressings at 45 RPM, creating a faster version that became popular in UK clubs, without anyone realizing the error. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rise_(instrumental)
- Herb Alpert became the only artist to reach #1 on the Billboard Hot 100 as both a vocalist ("This Guy's in Love with You," 1968) and an instrumentalist ("Rise," 1979). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rise_(instrumental)
- The song knocked Michael Jackson off the #1 spot on the Hot 100. — Source: https://stereogum.com/2074284/the-number-ones-herb-alperts-rise/columns/the-number-ones
- Randy "Badazz" Alpert rejected a Pfizer offer to use "Rise" in a Viagra ad campaign ("Get a Rise out of Viagra..."), saying it was an easy decision for the song's longevity. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rise_(instrumental)
- The General Hospital connection: actor Anthony Geary (who played Luke Spencer) suggested using "Rise" as the soundtrack for the controversial Luke-and-Laura rape scene. The song was replayed multiple times per week as the storyline continued, giving it massive repeated television exposure and boosting it to #1. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rise_(instrumental) and https://stereogum.com/2074284/the-number-ones-herb-alperts-rise/columns/the-number-ones
- The song had zero top-10 singles in the eleven years between "This Guy's in Love with You" (1968) and "Rise" (1979). — Source: https://stereogum.com/2074284/the-number-ones-herb-alperts-rise/columns/the-number-ones

#### Album
- The *Rise* album sold over three million copies. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rise_(Herb_Alpert_album)
- It reached #1 on the Billboard Jazz Albums chart, #6 on both the Billboard 200 and R&B album charts. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rise_(Herb_Alpert_album)
- It was one of the first pop albums to use digital recording technology (3M 32-track recorder), with all tracks except "1980" recorded digitally. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rise_(Herb_Alpert_album)
- The album includes a cover of the Crusaders' "Street Life" and Joaquín Rodrigo's classical piece "Aranjuez (Mon Amour)." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rise_(Herb_Alpert_album)

#### Artist
- Herb Alpert was born March 31, 1935 in Boyle Heights, Los Angeles, into a family of musicians: his father played mandolin, his mother taught violin, his brother was a drummer, and his sister played piano. He started trumpet at age 8. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herb_Alpert
- In 1966, Herb Alpert & the Tijuana Brass outsold the Beatles in album sales. — Source: https://stereogum.com/2074284/the-number-ones-herb-alperts-rise/columns/the-number-ones
- Alpert co-founded A&M Records with Jerry Moss in his garage in 1962. It grew into the world's largest independent record label, was sold to PolyGram for $500 million in 1989, and Alpert/Moss later won an additional $200 million in a breach-of-contract lawsuit. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%26M_Records
- Alpert has won 8 Grammy Awards plus a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award, a Tony Award, was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame (2006), received the National Medal of Arts (2012), and has a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herb_Alpert
- He has donated over $64 million to music education: $30 million to UCLA's Herb Alpert School of Music, $24 million to CalArts, and $10.1 million to Los Angeles City College (the largest gift ever to a California community college). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herb_Alpert

Producer Sean "Puffy" Combs chose the groove because he remembered hearing it everywhere in New York in the summer of 1979 when he was about 10 years old. ### Genre Suggestions

Current genre: Jazz-Funk

1. **Jazz-Funk (keep)** — The strongest fit. "Rise" is an instrumental led by a jazz trumpet player (Alpert) over a funk groove with Fender Rhodes, bass, and drums locked in a pocket. It has the hallmarks described in the book's genre definition: jazz musician playing funk, longer track length (7:37), room for improvisation, rhythm section built to support a soloist. AllMusic classifies it under Jazz and Easy Listening; Wikipedia calls it "jazz-funk and disco." The jazz-funk label is the most accurate for this book's purposes.

2. **Classic Funk** — A secondary case could be made. "Rise" was a massive mainstream hit (#1 pop, #4 R&B) with heavy radio play and cultural penetration, fitting the "commercial pipeline" emphasis of classic funk. However, the track is an instrumental led by a jazz trumpeter, which places it more naturally in jazz-funk territory.

3. **Soul Jazz** — A weaker case. The album has organ/Rhodes textures and a smaller-ensemble vibe on some tracks, but "Rise" itself is firmly a late-70s production with layers of studio polish, not the early-60s simmer described in the book's soul jazz definition.

**Recommendation:** Keep Jazz-Funk. It's the right call.
