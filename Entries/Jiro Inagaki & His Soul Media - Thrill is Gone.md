### Writing Theme Suggestions

- "A blues standard goes to Tokyo": Trace how a 1951 West Coast blues lament (Roy Hawkins/Rick Darnell), made world-famous by B.B. King in 1969, gets reimagined as Japanese jazz-funk just four years later. The angle is funk as a global translation language.
- "Japan answers The Crusaders": The album is explicitly a response to the Crusaders, whom Inagaki was studying at the time, and mixes Crusaders covers ("Put It Where You Want It," "That's How I Feel") with originals. A theme about influence, homage, and the trans-Pacific jazz-funk conversation.
- "The session man becomes the bandleader": Inagaki spent the 1950s-60s as a sideman (Crazy Cats, George Kawaguchi, Hideo Shiraki) before forming Soul Media in 1969. A theme about a journeyman finding his own funk voice.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- This is a cover of B.B. King's signature blues. Listen for where the band keeps the mournful minor-key "thrill is gone" melody and where they trade King's slow-burn ache for a tighter funk pocket. What survives the translation, and what gets funkier?
- The album was made in answer to the Crusaders. Listen to the horn-and-electric-piano interplay here and ask whether you would guess this was recorded in Tokyo rather than Los Angeles. What gives away its origin, if anything?
- Focus on the rhythm section's restraint on a "slow" blues. Where do the drums and bass push the tempo forward versus letting the tune breathe like the original?

### Interest and Surprise

### Track

- "Thrill Is Gone" is a cover of "The Thrill Is Gone," a slow minor-key blues written by Roy Hawkins and Rick Darnell in 1951; Hawkins's original reached No. 6 on the Billboard R&B chart. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Thrill_Is_Gone
- The song became a worldwide standard via B.B. King's 1969 recording (album *Completely Well*), released as a single in December 1969, reaching No. 3 on Billboard's Best Selling Soul Singles and No. 15 on the Hot 100 — making Inagaki's 1973 version a remarkably quick funk response to a then-current hit. — Source: https://blues.org/blues_hof_inductee/the-thrill-is-gone-b-b-king-abc-bluesway-1969/
- On early B.B. King releases the song was sometimes miscredited to "Arthur Benson" and "Dale Pettite." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Thrill_Is_Gone

### Album

- *In The Groove* was conceived as a direct response to The Crusaders, whom Inagaki was closely following; the LP mixes Crusaders covers (e.g. "Put It Where You Want It," "That's How I Feel" by Wilton Felder/Joe Sample/Stix Hooper) with originals by arranger Norio Maeda. — Source: https://www.hhv-mag.com/review/jiro-inagaki-soul-media-in-the-groove/?lang=en
- The album was recorded across two sessions, February 26 and 28, 1973, at Columbia Akasaka Studio 2, with arrangements by Norio Maeda and Jiro Inagaki. — Source: https://www.hhv-mag.com/review/jiro-inagaki-soul-media-in-the-groove/?lang=en
- It has become a sought-after crate-digger / rare-groove title, reissued by labels such as Mr Bongo and Light in the Attic; Discogs shows roughly 2,600+ "want" vs ~1,200 "have," signaling collector demand. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/master/1739127-Jiro-Inagaki-His-Soul-Media-In-The-Groove

### Artist

- Jiro Inagaki (born October 3, 1933) is a Japanese jazz saxophonist (tenor, soprano, flute) who played as a sideman through the 1950s-60s — including with Frankie Sakai, Hajime Hana and the Crazy Cats, George Kawaguchi, and Hideo Shiraki — before forming Soul Media in 1969. — Source: https://wikitia.com/wiki/Jiro_Inagaki
- Soul Media's rotating lineup included notable players such as guitarist Ryo Kawasaki and pianist/arranger Masahiko Satoh, both of whom went on to significant international jazz careers. — Source: https://www.last.fm/music/Jiro+Inagaki+&+Soul+Media/+wiki
- Note: one source lists an alternate birth date of July 11, 1935, conflicting with the more common October 3, 1933. — Source: https://en.everybodywiki.com/Jiro_Inagaki

### Genre Suggestions

Per *On Subgenres.md* definitions:

- **Jazz-Funk** (strongest): The book defines jazz-funk as "funk played by jazz musicians," with longer track lengths, room for improvisation, a rhythm section built for soloists, and a 1970s home. Inagaki is a career jazz saxophonist leading a band that injects funk into jazz; reviewers and Discogs file this LP under Jazz / Jazz-Funk / Jazz-Rock. Recorded 1973 — squarely the jazz-funk era. — Source: https://www.hhv-mag.com/review/jiro-inagaki-soul-media-in-the-groove/?lang=en
- **Soul Jazz** (secondary): If the track leans more toward the early-simmer, groove-over-fusion side, soul jazz could apply, though the book reserves soul jazz for the "earlier, smaller-ensemble" side — this is a fuller-band 1973 recording, making jazz-funk the better fit.
