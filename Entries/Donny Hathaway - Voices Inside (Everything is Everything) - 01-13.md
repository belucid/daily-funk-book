The studio version of today’s “Voices Inside (Everything Is Everything)” appears on Donny Hathaway’s 1970 debut album *Everything Is Everything*, and it clocks in at a radio-friendly 3m 28s. In this live performance, Hathaway and the band stretch it to nearly fourteen minutes. That extra space is where the jazz in jazz-funk lives.

Hathaway takes the first solo on a Wurlitzer electric piano. By the early 1970s, electric piano and clavinet were pushing aside the Hammond organ that had defined so much 1960s soul jazz.

Mike Howard steps in with a short, jazz-inflected guitar solo that's a little dissonant and heady, but still sits in the groove. Next up, Cornell Dupree takes over on guitar with a different voice. To my ear, it leans more blues, more grit, more grease.

Finally, Willie Weeks delivers the moment the crowd didn't know they were waiting for: a bass solo so funky that you hear the room react. Hathaway’s solo has spark, but Weeks lights the fuse.

My only complaint is that Fred White, the funky drummer here, didn't take a solo. Born on January 13, 1955, he was only 16 at the time, but already playing like a veteran. He earned his first gold record on this album before going on to join Earth, Wind & Fire.

## Research Notes

### Open Research Questions

Open: What kind of electric piano is Hathaway playing on Voices Inside (Everything is Everything)

**Resolution (2026-04-23):** Almost certainly a **Wurlitzer 200** electric piano.

The short version for the author: Hathaway is the most famous Wurlitzer player in soul. The *Live* album is widely identified as a Wurlitzer record, and by 1971 the specific model in production was the Wurlitzer 200 (the 200A did not ship until 1974). No primary source (album liner notes, an interview with Hathaway, or a photograph of the Bitter End rig) nails the instrument for this specific track, so this is a confident deduction from converging secondary sources rather than a documented fact.

What the evidence shows:

1. **Wikipedia's personnel list for *Live* is non-specific.** Hathaway is credited with "vocals, electric piano, piano, organ, arrangements." The model of electric piano is not stated. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_(Donny_Hathaway_album)
2. **Every substantive review or keyboard-focused write-up of the album names a Wurlitzer.** The longform Songs From So Deep piece on *Live* states: "The live performance is dominated by Hathaway's Wurlitzer electric piano." Search-indexed summaries of the same article add, "Hathaway used the Wurlitzer, technically believed to be a Wurli 200." Source: https://songsfromsodeep.wordpress.com/2019/02/14/double-live-gonzos-part-5-live-donny-hathaway/
3. **Hathaway's career-long association is with the Wurlitzer, not the Rhodes.** The Abbey Road Institute's history of the Wurlitzer 200/200A names him alongside Supertramp, Elton John, Queen, Joni Mitchell and Steely Dan as signature users; it does not make the same claim for him on the Rhodes. Source: https://abbeyroadinstitute.com/miami/blog/classic-keys-the-wurlitzer-electronic-piano/
4. **Equipboard lists both a Wurlitzer 200A and a Fender Rhodes Mark I Stage 73 (1969–1975) as Hathaway's gear.** This corroborates a Wurlitzer as his primary instrument while noting a Rhodes was also in the rig at times. It does not specify which instrument was used on *Live*. Source: https://equipboard.com/pros/donny-hathaway
5. **A 1971 recording predates the 200A.** The Wurlitzer 200 was in production from roughly 1968 and was replaced by the 200A in 1974. So a 1971 live set would have been a **200**, not a 200A. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wurlitzer_electronic_piano ; https://docwurly.com/wurlitzer-ep-history/wurlitzer-electric-piano-models-a-list/
6. **A single conflicting data point.** One search excerpt referenced "elastic Fender Rhodes stabs" on "The Ghetto." This is outvoted by multiple sources pointing to Wurlitzer on that same track, so the reference appears to be mistaken. It is worth mentioning only because it is the one contrary note in an otherwise clean consensus.
7. **Aural tell for the reader.** The Wurlitzer has a reedy, barking, slightly overdriven sound when pushed; the Rhodes has a glassy, bell-like tone. Hathaway's piano on "Voices Inside" sits in that bark-and-bite range, which matches the Wurlitzer signature.

What would have tipped this from "confident deduction" to "documented fact," and why it does not exist in the public record:

- The original Atlantic/Atco 1972 gatefold liner notes (checked via Discogs release pages) credit musicians by name and instrument family only, not by gear make or model, which was typical for the era. Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/5613647-Donny-Hathaway-Live ; https://www.discogs.com/release/6955426-Donny-Hathaway-Live
- Hathaway gave few interviews in his lifetime, and the surviving interview record (Disc and Music Echo, August 1971, referenced in the Wax Poetics piece) does not address gear. Producer Arif Mardin's own interviews focus on arrangement and engineering rather than the piano. Source: https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/rediscovery/donny-hathaway-live/ ; https://www.soundonsound.com/people/arif-mardin-producer
- None of the band members who did give later interviews (Cornell Dupree, Willie Weeks) are on record specifying which electric piano Hathaway was playing at the Bitter End on 7 October 1971.

If the author wants to hedge, the safe wording is: "a Wurlitzer electric piano, almost certainly a Wurlitzer 200." If the author wants to commit, "a Wurlitzer 200" is fully defensible on the weight of secondary evidence, the era's production window, and the sonic fingerprint.

This is a strong theme that plays off the track's transformation from a 3:28 studio cut into a 13:47 live meditation. Here are some alternatives:

1. **"The teenager and the bass player walk into a bar"** — The band on this track includes Fred White, who was 16 years old and already earning a gold record, and Willie Weeks, who delivers what many bassists consider the greatest bass solo ever recorded. The generational collision of youth and mastery in a single performance is a vivid hook.

2. **"A 3-minute song becomes a 14-minute sermon"** — The studio version of "Voices Inside" is a compact 3:28. Live at the Bitter End, Hathaway and the band stretch it to nearly four times the length, and the expansion isn't padding: it's four solos, a crowd in a trance, and a bass solo that changed what bassists thought was possible. This theme frames the entry around the alchemy of live performance.

3. **"The room where nobody's drinking"** — The Bitter End was a "dry venue" (no alcohol). Cornell Dupree noted the New York audience was more restrained than the Hollywood Troubadour crowd. Yet the performance builds to one of the most incandescent finales in live soul music. What does it say about funk's power that the groove didn't need any liquid courage?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- The studio version of "Voices Inside (Everything Is Everything)" on the 1970 debut album *Everything Is Everything* runs just 3:28. The live version stretches it to 13:47, nearly four times the length, transforming a compact album opener into an extended soul-jazz jam. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everything_Is_Everything_(Donny_Hathaway_album)
- The song was written by Richard Evans, Ric Powell, and Phil Upchurch. Co-writer Ric Powell recalled that the bass line intro was inspired by Percy Mayfield's "River's Invitation" (1963), while Powell added the "everything is everything" hook and the opening lyric "I hear voices, I see people." — Source: https://secondhandsongs.com/work/287969/all
- The live version features four sequential solos: Hathaway on electric piano, Mike Howard on guitar (described as "tense and rather dissonant, full of bent notes"), Cornell Dupree on guitar ("fluid and lyrical"), and Willie Weeks on bass. — Source: https://songsfromsodeep.wordpress.com/2019/02/14/double-live-gonzos-part-5-live-donny-hathaway/
- Willie Weeks' 3.5-minute bass solo on this track is widely considered one of the greatest bass solos ever recorded. Victor Wooten included the *Live* album in his list of 10 essential bass albums in 2005 specifically because of it. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_(Donny_Hathaway_album)
- Weeks himself said of the solo's impact: "Everywhere I go someone knows my name because of that bass solo. It's not like with Michael Jackson, I don't get mobbed, but I am famous." — Source: https://www.guitarworld.com/features/willie-weeks-bass-solo-on-donny-hathaway-live
- The studio version has been sampled by: Delinquent Habits ("Juvy"), Geto Boys feat. Ganksta N-I-P, Big Mello and Seagram ("Bring It On"), Dooley-O ("I Gotcha"), and Del the Funky Homosapien feat. Hieroglyphics ("Burnt"). — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Donny-Hathaway/Voices-Inside-(Everything-Is-Everything)/sampled/
- Atlantic Records asked Hathaway not to mention that the shows were being recorded. During "You've Got a Friend," he remarks to the audience anyway: "this might be a record here." — Source: https://songsfromsodeep.wordpress.com/2019/02/14/double-live-gonzos-part-5-live-donny-hathaway/

#### Album
- *Live* was recorded at two venues on different coasts, months apart: Side A at the Troubadour in Hollywood (August 24-30, 1971) and Side B at the Bitter End in Greenwich Village, New York (October 7, 1971). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_(Donny_Hathaway_album)
- The Bitter End was a "dry venue" with no alcohol. Guitarist Cornell Dupree observed that the New York crowd was "rather more restrained than the Troubadour crowd." — Source: https://songsfromsodeep.wordpress.com/2019/02/14/double-live-gonzos-part-5-live-donny-hathaway/
- The album peaked at #18 on the Billboard 200 and #4 on Billboard Top Soul LPs. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_(Donny_Hathaway_album)
- Rolling Stone ranked it #48 on their 2015 list of the 50 Greatest Live Albums of All Time. — Source: https://albumism.com/lists1/50-greatest-live-albums-of-all-time-donny-hathaway-live
- Hathaway was so impressed by the recordings that he told producer Arif Mardin: "Oh, it sounds like I'm in the room." — Source: https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/rediscovery/donny-hathaway-live/
- Side A was produced by Arif Mardin; Side B by Mardin and Jerry Wexler. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_(Donny_Hathaway_album)
- Bassist Willie Weeks described the audience during Hathaway's performances as "swaying like a wheat field in the wind." — Source: https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/rediscovery/donny-hathaway-live/

#### Artist
- Donny Hathaway (October 1, 1945 - January 13, 1979) was born in Chicago and raised by his grandmother Martha Pitts, a professional gospel singer, in the Carr Square housing project in St. Louis. He began singing in the church choir at age three. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donny_Hathaway
- Hathaway attended Howard University on a fine arts scholarship, where he met his roommate and drummer Ric Powell (co-writer of "Voices Inside") and fellow student Roberta Flack. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donny_Hathaway
- Before his solo career, Hathaway was a songwriter, session musician, and arranger for Curtis Mayfield's Curtom Records in Chicago. He worked with Aretha Franklin and The Staple Singers. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donny_Hathaway
- King Curtis discovered Hathaway at a trade convention and signed him to Atco Records. Cornell Dupree said: "It was through Curtis that I met Hathaway, and I started working with him. I think I did most of his recordings." — Source: https://timhigginsguitar.com/2024/10/04/great-guitarists-15-cornell-dupree/
- Hathaway was diagnosed with paranoid schizophrenia and between 1973-1974 was hospitalized multiple times. At one point he was taking fourteen different medications twice daily. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donny_Hathaway
- On January 13, 1979, Hathaway was found dead below the 15th floor of the Essex House hotel in New York City. Earlier that day during a recording session, he had exhibited paranoid and delusional behavior. His funeral was conducted by Reverend Jesse Jackson. He was 33 years old. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donny_Hathaway
- The drummer on the *Live* album, Fred White, was just 16 years old at the time of the recording and earned his first gold record for this album. He went on to become the drummer for Earth, Wind & Fire during their entire run of six consecutive top-10 albums. — Source: https://www.npr.org/2023/01/02/1146587565/fred-white-drummer-earth-wind-fire-dead
- George Harrison said at a 1974 press conference that he would "rather have Willie Weeks on bass than Paul McCartney," citing Weeks as a reason a Beatles reunion was unnecessary. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willie_Weeks
- Cornell Dupree, who plays guitar on the Bitter End side of the album, recorded on approximately 2,500 sessions in his career, earning the nickname "Mr. 2500." He listed his work on Hathaway's *Live* among the five recordings he considered his best. — Source: https://www.allaboutjazz.com/musicians/cornell-dupree/
- Hathaway was posthumously awarded a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award in 2019. Rolling Stone named him the 49th greatest singer of all time in 2010. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donny_Hathaway
- His daughter Lalah Hathaway became a successful R&B and jazz singer in her own right. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donny_Hathaway

2. **Jazz-Funk** — This track has strong jazz-funk credentials: it's 13+ minutes of extended improvisation, features four solos from jazz-trained musicians, and the rhythm section supports soloists in the jazz-funk tradition.
