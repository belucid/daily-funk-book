*Emotional Rescue* topped charts around the world and spent seven weeks at #1 in the US. The title track was its biggest hit, and yet the Rolling Stones did not play "Emotional Rescue" live until 2013. Perhaps the song never quite fit the band’s self-image.

Mick Jagger sings the entire thing in a high falsetto that saxophonist Bobby Keys reportedly called “Minnie Mouse vocals.” Jagger defended his choice and name-checked Prince as a fellow falsettoist. 

The high voice and the spoken-word seduction sound like Jagger winking at the listener, inviting them in on the joke. The result is both legitimately funky and also a bit of a gag, which might be why it stayed off the set list for so long.

### Open Research Questions

**Q: Was "Emotional Rescue" the biggest hit off the album?**

**Yes, by a wide margin.** The album produced three singles:

1. **"Emotional Rescue"** — No. 3 US (Billboard Hot 100), No. 1 Canada, No. 9 UK
2. **"She's So Cold"** — No. 26 US (Billboard Hot 100), No. 33 UK
3. **"Dance Pt. 1"** — No. 9 on Billboard's Dance chart (not a pop chart entry)

"Emotional Rescue" was comfortably the album's biggest hit, peaking 23 positions higher than the next single on the Hot 100.

Sources: [Wikipedia: Emotional Rescue (song)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_Rescue_(song)), [Wikipedia: She's So Cold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/She%27s_So_Cold), [Wikipedia: Emotional Rescue (album)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_Rescue)

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"When Jagger stole from himself stealing from Don Covay"** — Jagger got his falsetto idea from Don Covay's "Mercy, Mercy," which the Stones had already covered in 1965. Fifteen years later, he circled back to that trick and turned it into the Stones' biggest funk moment. This creates a through-line about how artists metabolize their influences over decades, not months.

2. **"The guitarist who played bass on a funk hit"** — Ronnie Wood played bass on this track, not Bill Wyman. Wyman moved to synthesizer while Wood locked in the central groove. The entry could explore what happens when a rock guitarist steps into the bass chair on a funk track, and how the unusual personnel swap shaped the song's feel.

3. **"33 years without a stage"** — Despite being a Top 3 hit, the Stones never performed "Emotional Rescue" live until May 3, 2013, a 33-year gap. This opens a thread about the tension between commercial success and artistic identity, and what it means when a band doesn't claim one of its biggest hits as part of its live self.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- Jagger composed the song on a Wurlitzer electric piano and sang the initial take in falsetto from the start. He later said he got the falsetto idea from Don Covay's "Mercy, Mercy," which the Stones had covered in 1965. He also referenced Prince's use of falsetto to defend the vocal approach: "I'm not the only person to have sung in falsetto, Prince did three albums singing like that around this time." — Source: https://ultimateclassicrock.com/rolling-stones-emotional-rescue-song/
- Bobby Keys, the saxophonist on the track, reportedly said he was shocked when he first heard the "Minnie Mouse vocals." — Source: https://rollingstonesdata.com/songs/emotional-rescue/
- Ronnie Wood played bass, not Bill Wyman. Wyman moved to string synthesizer. This was unusual for Stones sessions where Wyman typically held the bass chair. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_Rescue_(song)
- The song includes a spoken-word section where Jagger delivers whimsical romantic declarations, which he later described as intentional humor, or "piss-taking." — Source: https://ultimateclassicrock.com/rolling-stones-emotional-rescue-song/
- Despite being a Top 3 US hit, the Stones never performed "Emotional Rescue" live until May 3, 2013, during the 50 & Counting tour in Los Angeles, a gap of 33 years. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_Rescue_(song)
- Engineer Chris Kimsey shaped the track's dance character using delay, phasing, and reverb on the hi-hat to create a shimmering, airy texture. — Source: https://rollingstonesdata.com/songs/emotional-rescue/
- A longer unreleased version from 1979 exists, described as more raw and spontaneous. — Source: https://rollingstonesdata.com/unreleased/emotional-rescue-long-version-1979/
- Three different edits exist: the 5:39 LP version, a 4:18 promo single, and a 3:42 compilation edit (on Forty Licks/GRRR!). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_Rescue_(song)
- Two music videos were produced: a conventional performance video directed by David Mallet, and an experimental one using thermographic imaging directed by Adam Friedman. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_Rescue_(song)

#### Album
- The Emotional Rescue album topped charts in at least six countries, including spending seven weeks at No. 1 in the US, the Stones' longest run at the top at that time. — Source: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/rolling-stones-emotional-rescue-album/
- The album cover used thermographic photography by French-based British artist Roy Adzak. The original release included a large color poster with thermal images of the band. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_Rescue
- The recording sessions (Jan-Oct 1979) were so productive that leftover tracks formed the core of the follow-up album, 1981's Tattoo You. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_Rescue
- Michael Shrieve, the drummer from Santana (famous for his Woodstock solo at age 20), played percussion on the album. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_Rescue
- Critical reception was initially muted, with reviewers calling the album "formulaic," but retrospective assessments have been kinder. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_Rescue
- Peter Corriston designed the album artwork, the same designer behind the covers for Some Girls and Tattoo You. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_Rescue

#### Artist
- During the Emotional Rescue sessions, Mick Jagger assumed near-total creative control while Keith Richards focused on getting sober. Richards contributed only minimal guitar overdubs to the title track. — Source: https://rollingstonesdata.com/unreleased/emotional-rescue-long-version-1979/
- Jagger characterized Ronnie Wood's presence in the band as "a breath of deodorant for everyone." — Source: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/rolling-stones-emotional-rescue-album/
- Ron Wood had been jailed for five days in February 1980 on St. Martin for drug possession during the album's production period. — Source: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/rolling-stones-emotional-rescue-album/
- Bill Wyman was already becoming vocal about eventually leaving the band during these sessions, though he wouldn't officially depart until 1993. — Source: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/rolling-stones-emotional-rescue-album/
- This was the first Rolling Stones album following Keith Richards' exoneration from his Toronto drug charges. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_Rescue

### Genre Suggestions

The `genre` field is currently set to `Funk Rock`.

1. **Funk Rock** (current value) — The strongest case. The Stones are fundamentally a rock band, and this track layers funk grooves under that identity. Ronnie Wood's bass line, Watts' locked-in drumming, and the overall groove are funk, but the context (a Rolling Stones record), the guitar textures, and the song's DNA are rock. AllMusic classifies the track's styles as including "Funk" and "Dance-Rock." Rate Your Music tags the single as "Funk Rock." The book's definition of Funk Rock ("mixing rock and roll with funk rhythms") fits well. **Recommended.**

2. **Classic Funk** — A case could be made that the track's groove, falsetto vocal, and dancefloor intent place it closer to classic funk than funk rock. The production borrows heavily from late-70s funk and disco, and the rhythm section operates in a funk pocket more than a rock pocket. However, the Stones' rock identity and the 1980 release date push slightly against the "classic era" definition (1968-1977).

3. **Deep Funk** — While the track was a hit (not obscure), the 33-year gap before live performance and the band's apparent ambivalence toward it give it a "rediscovery" quality. This is a stretch, however, since the song charted at No. 3 and was widely known on release.
