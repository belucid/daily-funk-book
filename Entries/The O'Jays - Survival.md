### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"Survival of the fittest, Philly style"** — The lyric pits Darwin against the working class. Eddie Levert sings as someone "busted, walkin' around broke," and Gamble and Huff's chorus reframes the natural-selection cliche as economic violence. The hook lands in 1975, with stagflation ripping through Black communities, gas lines still fresh in memory, and unemployment at a postwar high. The angle: this is funk as field report on the recession, dressed up in MFSB strings.

2. **"The B-side that became a message"** — "Survival" was tucked behind "Let Me Make Love to You" on the 45 (Philadelphia International ZS8 3573). The A-side was the radio play; the B-side was the social commentary. Christgau gave the album a C+ and called it "the drabbest" of the Gamble-Huff O'Jays records, but the title track and "Rich Get Richer" were the politics he wished had been singles. The angle: the message track that didn't quite get its shot.

3. **"What happens when the hit factory points at hard times"** — Sigma Sound, Bobby Martin arrangements, the Baker-Harris-Young rhythm section, Vince Montana on vibes: the same machinery that polished "Me and Mrs. Jones" gets turned toward survival anxiety. The Philly Sound's signature lushness becomes a kind of ironic frame, dressing despair in cascading horns. The angle: how the most commercial Black pop production team of the 70s used their sweetest tools on bitter material.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "Survival" was the title track of the album but was issued only as the B-side of "Let Me Make Love to You" (Philadelphia International ZS8 3573). Source: https://www.bionicdisco.com/2022/06/27/music-for-monday-the-ojays-survival-1975/
- Eddie Levert sings the lead, delivering what one critic described as a vocal "filled with desperation, sadness and resignation" as he addresses being broke and out of options. Source: https://dereksmusicblog.com/2012/12/07/the-ojays-survival/
- Music critic Robert Christgau singled the album out as "the drabbest studio album" Gamble and Huff had made with the O'Jays, but praised the social-commentary tracks "Survival" and "Rich Get Richer," lamenting that "Rich Get Richer" was not released as the single. Source: https://www.robertchristgau.com/get_album.php?id=7554
- The arrangement leans on the Baker-Harris-Young rhythm section (Ronnie Baker on bass, Norman Harris on guitar, Earl Young on drums), with Vince Montana Jr. on vibes and a horn chart that "blazes" and "cascades" beneath punchy O'Jays harmonies. Bobby Martin handled the arrangement. Source: https://dereksmusicblog.com/2012/12/07/the-ojays-survival/
- The opening lyric states the thesis bluntly: "Survival! Survival of the fittest!" Source: https://www.bionicdisco.com/2022/06/27/music-for-monday-the-ojays-survival-1975/
- The track runs 3:42, sitting as A3 on side one of the LP, between "Let Me Make Love to You" and "Where Did We Go Wrong." Source: https://www.soulandfunkmusic.com/album/the_o_jays/survival

#### Album

- Survival is the eighth studio album by The O'Jays, their fourth on Philadelphia International Records, released April 1, 1975 (catalog KZ 33150). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survival_(The_O'Jays_album); https://music.apple.com/us/album/survival/158513207
- The album hit #1 on the Billboard Top R&B Albums chart and #11 on the Billboard 200, was certified Gold by the RIAA, and spent 23 weeks on the chart. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survival_(The_O'Jays_album)
- Recorded at Sigma Sound Studios, Philadelphia; produced by Kenny Gamble and Leon Huff; arranged by Bobby Martin with MFSB as the house orchestra; engineered by Joe Tarsia; cover photo by Don Hunstein, art by Jose Gerson. Source: https://www.soulandfunkmusic.com/album/the_o_jays/survival
- The album produced two charting singles: "Give the People What They Want" (#45 Pop, #1 R&B) and "Let Me Make Love to You" (#75 Pop, #10 R&B). "Survival" itself never got an A-side. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survival_(The_O'Jays_album)
- The album sits in what one writer called the post-Watergate "wicked ennui" mode of mid-70s Philly soul, with social-commentary tracks ("Survival," "Rich Get Richer," "Give the People What They Want") interleaved with romantic ballads. Source: https://www.funkmysoul.gr/the-ojays-1975-survival/
- It is often described as overlooked within the O'Jays' 1970s canon, sandwiched between Ship Ahoy (1973) and Family Reunion (1975), but is widely regarded as a "true Philly soul classic." Source: https://www.funkmysoul.gr/the-ojays-1975-survival/

#### Artist

- The O'Jays formed in 1958 in Canton, Ohio, while members attended Canton McKinley High School. They were originally called The Triumphs, then The Mascots, before renaming themselves in 1963 in tribute to Cleveland radio DJ Eddie O'Jay, who championed and helped manage the group. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_O%27Jays
- By 1972, original members Bobby Massey and Bill Isles had left, leaving the trio of Eddie Levert, Walter Williams, and William Powell when the group signed with Gamble and Huff's new Philadelphia International Records label. This is the lineup that recorded Survival. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_O%27Jays
- Eddie Levert was born June 16, 1942, in Bessemer, Alabama. Source: https://www.alamhof.org/eddielevert
- Walter Lee Williams was born August 25, 1943, in Canton, Ohio. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_O%27Jays
- William Powell was born January 20, 1942, in Canton, Ohio. He was diagnosed with cancer in 1975 (the same year Survival was released), stopped touring in 1976, and died May 26, 1977, at age 35. Powell sang on Survival, making this one of the last albums he recorded. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_O%27Jays
- The O'Jays were inducted into the Vocal Group Hall of Fame in 2004 and the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 2005. Source: https://vocalgroup.org/inductees/the-ojays/

### Genre Suggestions

1. **Classic Funk** (current) — Solid case. The track was released into the commercial pipeline of a major label in the heart of the classic era (1975), got airplay as a B-side that charted, and the groove is driven by a bona fide funk pocket: Ronnie Baker's bass and Earl Young's drums working a syncopated 4/4 with horn punches. The book defines classic funk as "music that defined the core vocabulary and was released into the commercial pipeline of major labels," which fits cleanly. It also keeps "Survival" consistent with its O'Jays siblings already in the book ("For the Love of Money," "Ship Ahoy"). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survival_(The_O'Jays_album); book definition in `Matter/On Genres & Regions.md`

2. **Soul Jazz** — Weak. The track has horn and string charts, but no jazz improvisation, no organ trio, and the rhythm section is firmly in funk territory rather than the swung pocket of soul jazz. The book's definition of soul jazz centers on smaller ensembles and the early-1960s simmer rather than mid-70s big-band Philly productions, so this is a stretch.

3. **Jazz-Funk** — Weak. The album leans on extended-form orchestrations in places, and MFSB had jazz players in its ranks, but "Survival" itself is a 3:42 vocal track with no extended improvisation. It does not fit the book's "longer track lengths, room for extended improvisation, and a rhythm section built to support soloists" criterion.

**Recommendation**: Keep Classic Funk. It is the cleanest fit and keeps the O'Jays family in the book genre-consistent.

### Region Suggestions

1. **US - Northeast** (current) — Strong case. Recorded at Sigma Sound Studios in Philadelphia; released on Philadelphia International Records; arrangements by Bobby Martin and the MFSB house band, all Philly-based. The Philly Sound is the defining Northeast funk-soul phenomenon of the 1970s, and "Survival" is squarely a product of that scene. Also matches sibling entries "Ship Ahoy" (11-10) and "For the Love of Money" (10-19). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survival_(The_O'Jays_album)

2. **US - Ohio** — Reasonable alternative. The O'Jays were a Canton, Ohio group from formation in 1958. The book's regions guidance says "each choice is meant to represent the track's origins and be defensible," and one could argue the group's identity should anchor the tag. The "For the Love of Money" research notes considered US - Ohio before settling on US - Northeast. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_O%27Jays

**Recommendation**: Keep US - Northeast for production-site consistency with the rest of the O'Jays' 70s catalog as filed in this book.
