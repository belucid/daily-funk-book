At twelve minutes, the album version of “Papa Was a Rollin’ Stone” is built on patience. The vocals don't arrive until nearly four minutes in, and the long instrumental stretches return throughout. People have suggested those gaps mirror the father’s absence. At 6m 54s, the single edit was one of the longest #1 hits in *Billboard* Hot 100 history up to that point.

The Temptations were not the first to record it. The Undisputed Truth recorded a shorter radio-friendly version earlier the same year but it failed to move up the charts. Producer Norman Whitfield turned the three-minute flop into a twelve-minute hit. The Temptations resisted the direction and nearly refused to record it, but Whitfield’s patience paid off.

The Temptations’ version went to #1 and won three Grammys in 1973. It also sits at #169 on *Rolling Stone’s* list of the 500 Greatest Songs of All Time.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

The `writing theme` field is currently empty.

**Suggestion 1: "The producer's coup"**
Norman Whitfield turned a three-minute Undisputed Truth flop into a twelve-minute #1 hit by essentially the same method: same song, same studio, same year, different arrangement. The entry could explore what happens when a producer's vision overrides the performers, and how the Temptations nearly refused to record the song that became their final pop #1. Whitfield made the vocals wait almost four minutes to enter, and the group resented him for it, but the result won three Grammys.

**Suggestion 2: "The day the song names"**
"It was the third of September, that day I'll always remember." Dennis Edwards almost refused to sing the opening line because his own father had recently died (on October 3, not September 3). He thought Whitfield was mocking him. Whitfield insisted the coincidence was accidental. The entry could explore how a fictional date in a song collided with a singer's real grief, and how Edwards' restrained delivery (which Whitfield demanded over Edwards' objections) made the line land harder precisely because it was personal.

**Suggestion 3: "Twelve minutes of absence"**
The track is about a man who was never there, and its structure mirrors that absence: nearly four minutes of instrumental before anyone sings, long stretches of groove between verses, the story told in fragments by different voices. The entry could explore how the song's epic length becomes a formal expression of its theme, with the music filling the space the father left empty.

#### Track
- The album version runs nearly 12 minutes, with an instrumental introduction lasting 3 minutes and 53 seconds before any vocals enter. The single edit cut the track to 6:54. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papa_Was_a_Rollin%27_Stone
- The B-side of the single was the instrumental version alone, and it won its own Grammy Award for Best R&B Instrumental, one of three Grammys the song won in 1973. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papa_Was_a_Rollin%27_Stone
- Dennis Edwards nearly refused to sing the opening line ("It was the third of September...") because his own father had recently died on October 3. He believed Norman Whitfield was deliberately mocking him. Whitfield insisted the date was coincidental. — Source: https://ultimateclassicrock.com/temptations-papa-was-a-rollin-stone/
- Whitfield forced Edwards to adopt a restrained, muted vocal delivery against Edwards' wishes. Edwards wanted to "put more on it," but Whitfield insisted on understatement. Edwards later acknowledged the approach was correct. — Source: https://ultimateclassicrock.com/temptations-papa-was-a-rollin-stone/
- The recording sessions took place on May 15, June 14, 22, and 28, 1972, at Hitsville USA (Studio A), Detroit. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papa_Was_a_Rollin%27_Stone
- The song's rhythm has been described as a "merciless four-four stomp-clap" that presaged both disco and house music. — Source: https://stereogum.com/2035527/the-number-ones-the-temptations-papa-was-a-rollin-stone/columns/the-number-ones/
- At nearly seven minutes, the single edit was one of the longest #1 hits in Billboard Hot 100 history at the time. — Source: https://stereogum.com/2035527/the-number-ones-the-temptations-papa-was-a-rollin-stone/columns/the-number-ones/
- The song was the Temptations' fourth and final #1 pop hit. — Source: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/temptations-papa-was-a-rollin-stone-song/

### Genre Suggestions

**Suggestion 1: Classic Funk**
The strongest fit. The Temptations were a major-label act on Gordy/Motown with massive chart presence. The track hit #1 on the Billboard Hot 100 in 1972, squarely within the 1968-1977 classic funk era. The instrumental bed is driven by a hard funk groove from the Funk Brothers: chicken-scratch guitar from Wah-Wah Watson, a deep pocket bass line, and a relentless four-on-the-floor rhythm. Despite Motown and psychedelic soul associations, the track's primary rhythmic identity is funk. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papa_Was_a_Rollin%27_Stone, https://stereogum.com/2035527/the-number-ones-the-temptations-papa-was-a-rollin-stone/columns/the-number-ones/

**Suggestion 2: Psychedelic Funk**
A strong secondary case. AllMusic and multiple sources categorize the track as "psychedelic soul." Norman Whitfield's production approach, inspired by Sly Stone and Funkadelic, pushed the Temptations into hallucinatory, extended-form arrangements with heavy effects and unconventional structures. The nearly four-minute wordless intro, the 12-minute runtime, and the brooding atmospheric tone all align with the psychedelic funk definition in the book: "surreal... alter egos, invented worlds, and hallucinatory storytelling." The track doesn't have surreal lyrics, but its structure and mood are deeply psychedelic. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Whitfield, https://www.allmusic.com/album/all-directions-mw0000198718

**Suggestion 3: Jazz-Funk**
A weaker but arguable case. The extended runtime (11:45), the prominence of instrumental passages over vocals, and the orchestral arrangement by Paul Riser using Detroit Symphony Orchestra players give the track some jazz-funk DNA. The solo trumpet line in the intro, the electric piano work from Earl Van Dyke, and the improvisational feel of the extended instrumental sections overlap with jazz-funk territory. However, the track's rhythm section and production are more funk and psychedelic soul than jazz.
