“Shiftless Shuffle” had been part of Hancock’s stage repertoire in the early 1970s, and his Head Hunters band recorded a version that was not released. Today's take comes from a new Tokyo session with a different lineup, different instrumentation, and an unusual recording process.

The *Directstep* album, released on January 21, 1979, was a Japanese audiophile experiment. It used direct-to-disc recording, a technique that predates magnetic audio tape. The band performed live, and the performance was cut directly to a master acetate in real time, with no edits or overdubs possible. This leaves the fewest possible steps between the sound captured in the room and the resulting vinyl record. Hancock and a six-piece electric band had to deliver “Shiftless Shuffle” in one continuous take.

Reissued in 1983 as one of the first commercial compact discs, the album serves as a bridge across the history of recording technology. This 1978 electric jazz-funk performance, captured with a pre-tape method, was reintroduced through the digital format of the 1980s and is likely reaching you today through streaming.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"No second takes."** Direct-to-disc recording cut directly to a master acetate, in real time, with no edits, no overdubs, no punch-ins, no mercy. Hancock and a seven-piece electric band had to deliver "Shiftless Shuffle" in one continuous performance, with the limit of about 16 minutes per side governing pacing for the whole side. This is funk performed under tightrope-walker conditions, and you can hear the focus.

2. **"The composition that took five years to find a home."** "Shiftless Shuffle" was written and tracked during the 1973 Head Hunters sessions but stayed in the vault. Hancock pulled it out in Tokyo five years later for a brand-new recording with a different drummer (Mouzon, not Harvey Mason) and different texture (two synth players, electric guitar). It is a single tune existing in two distinct, both-essential versions, which lets you hear how Hancock's groove brain evolved between the Headhunters peak and the Feets Don't Fail Me Now/Mr. Hands era.

3. **"The Japan-only album that became a CD pioneer."** Directstep was a Japanese audiophile experiment: cut at CBS/Sony Studios Tokyo in October 1978, pressed direct-to-disc, never sold in the US. Then in 1983 it became one of the earliest commercial compact discs, leapfrogging from a 1900s pre-electric format to a 1980s digital format with no analog tape generation in between. A single track that bridges three different recording eras.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. The whole record was cut in a single live take to a master acetate, with no edits possible. Listen for a moment where you can hear the band breathing as one organism, then ask yourself: where would they have started over if they could have?

2. This is the same composition as the "Shiftless Shuffle" on Mr. Hands, but a different recording with a different drummer (Alphonse Mouzon here, Harvey Mason there). Pull up both versions and decide which pocket you prefer, and why.

3. There are two keyboard players on this track: Hancock and Webster Lewis. Try to follow which synth lines are coming from where. When the textures get dense, notice how they leave each other space.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "Shiftless Shuffle" had been part of Hancock's 1973-1974 working repertoire and was originally tracked during the Head Hunters sessions, but went unreleased for years. The Directstep version is a brand-new 1978 Tokyo re-recording, not the vault take. — Source: https://www.herbiehancock.com/music/discography/album/directstep/
- The Directstep cut features Alphonse Mouzon on drums instead of Harvey Mason, plus two added musicians not on the original Head Hunters lineup: guitarist Ray Obiedo and second keyboardist Webster Lewis. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directstep
- Bennie Maupin plays the Lyricon on the track, an electronic wind controller that turns sax-style breath and embouchure into synthesizer voltage. It first showed up in Hancock's records on Man-Child (1975) and is one of the defining sound colors of late-70s Hancock. — Source: https://www.gratefulweb.com/articles/herbie-hancock-directstep-lp
- The composition is co-credited to Hancock, Paul Jackson, Bennie Maupin, Harvey Mason, and Bill Summers, the entire original Headhunters quintet — even though Mason did not play on the Directstep take. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Hands_(album)

#### Album

- Directstep was released only in Japan, on January 21, 1979, on CBS/Sony, catalog number 30AP-1032. It had no US release at all in the original LP era. — Source: https://www.herbiehancock.com/music/discography/album/directstep/
- The album was recorded direct-to-disc on October 17 and 18, 1978, at CBS/Sony Studios, Tokyo. Direct-to-disc means the band played live straight to the master acetate disc; there is no master tape in the chain at all, and no overdubbing or edits are possible. Each side has a hard real-time limit of roughly 16 minutes including the gap between songs. — Source: https://www.gratefulweb.com/articles/herbie-hancock-directstep-lp
- Directstep was one of the earliest commercial compact disc releases. A CD edition appeared in 1983 (catalog 38DP 39), making the album a small piece of audio-format history twice over. — Source: https://recordstoreday.com/SpecialRelease/11910
- It was the first Hancock album to feature a second dedicated keyboardist (Webster Lewis) on the date itself, and the first to make extensive use of the Sennheiser Vocoder. The Vocoder vocabulary on Directstep is a stepping-stone toward Hancock's full electro turn on Future Shock (1983). — Source: https://www.gratefulweb.com/articles/herbie-hancock-directstep-lp
- Get On Down reissued Directstep on vinyl for Record Store Day Black Friday 2019 in a limited run of 2,200 copies, the first widely available vinyl pressing in the West in roughly thirty years, packaged with a replica obi strip. — Source: https://recordstoreday.com/SpecialRelease/11910

#### Artist

- Herbert Jeffrey Hancock, born April 12, 1940 in Chicago, Illinois, performed Mozart's Coronation Concerto with the Chicago Symphony at age 11. He later took degrees in both electrical engineering and music at Grinnell College, which feeds straight into his later embrace of synths and direct-to-disc and digital recording. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbie_Hancock
- The recording band was tightly associated with the San Francisco Bay Area scene. Producer David Rubinson ran his San Francisco-based operation, Paul Jackson was from Oakland, and Ray Obiedo grew up in Richmond, California. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Jackson_(bassist) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Obiedo
- Drummer Alphonse Mouzon was a Weather Report alumnus (he played the band's 1971 self-titled debut) and a fixture of Larry Coryell's Eleventh House. Slotting him in for Harvey Mason gave the Directstep version a more aggressive fusion drummer's attack than the original Head Hunters grouping. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphonse_Mouzon

### Genre Suggestions

The entry's genre field is currently empty. My recommendations:

1. **Jazz-Funk** (strongest fit). Rate Your Music tags Directstep as Jazz-Funk. The Directstep band is a jazz musician's band: Hancock, Maupin, Mouzon all carry serious jazz pedigree, the track is over seven minutes long, and the form leaves wide room for Fender Rhodes and Lyricon improvisation over a funk pocket. Per the book's working definition (jazz-funk = funk played by jazz musicians, with longer track lengths, room for extended improvisation, and a rhythm section built to support soloists), this is a textbook example. — Source: https://rateyourmusic.com/release/album/herbie-hancock/directstep/

2. **Classic Funk** (weaker fit). 1978 sits inside the book's classic funk window (1969-1977 by definition, and Directstep is on the back edge). However, the track's primary identity is funk played by jazz musicians, not funk as primary identity, so jazz-funk is the better label per the book's own rule.

I would recommend Jazz-Funk.

### Region Suggestions

1. **US - California** (recommended, populated). Hancock had been based in the San Francisco Bay Area since the early 1970s, working closely with David Rubinson's San Francisco-based production operation. Paul Jackson was from Oakland, Ray Obiedo from Richmond, CA, and Bill Summers had Bay Area Latin-jazz roots. The Directstep band is essentially a Bay Area jazz-funk unit. The book's own region note says "regions reflect a single defensible call about the track's origins," and the Bay Area band identity is the most defensible call. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Jackson_(bassist) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Obiedo
