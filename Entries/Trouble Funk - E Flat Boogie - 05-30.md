It is not often you can point to the exact recorded track that helped birth a genre. With go-go, you can come pretty close with “E Flat Boogie,” which was released as a single in 1980. Trouble Funk did not invent go-go on its own. The group began as Trouble Band and Show, a Top 40 cover band, and learned the sound opening for go-go pioneer Chuck Brown at Club LeBaron in Palmer Park, Maryland. When “E Flat Boogie” came out, the band’s name was still settling. The label credited the record simply to "Trouble".

Go-go was a local scene built around a handful of bands working D.C.-area clubs. One force holding that scene together was producer Maxx Kidd, the Kidd in Al & The Kidd Records. After leaving the Army, Kidd built a music career in the D.C. area, then worked with the key go-go acts: Chuck Brown & The Soul Searchers, Trouble Funk, and E.U.

Thirty-eight years later, on May 30, 2018, “E Flat Boogie” was the closing song on Trouble Funk’s Tiny Desk concert, with twelve current members squeezed behind NPR’s famous desk. It's a fun watch on YouTube.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"Before go-go had a name."** In 1980 the genre that would soon be called go-go was still consolidating its identity. E Flat Boogie is one of the foundational records of that moment, released so early in Trouble Funk's life that the label literally credits "Trouble." The hook is the music documenting a scene in the act of becoming itself.
- **"The key is in the title."** The track is named for its key signature (E♭), and the title functions almost as a producer's chalkboard label that survived to release. This opens a small but vivid angle on the band's working method, where the musicians' shop talk became a hook.
- **"Maxx Kidd's first pitch."** The story of how the band's own pressing reached Maxx Kidd's Al & The Kidd Records imprint and his decades-long crusade to get go-go on national radio. E Flat Boogie is the entry point into a longer biography of go-go's "Berry Gordy of Washington." Source: https://www.billboard.com/music/rb-hip-hop/go-go-pioneer-maxx-kidd-obit-7727710/

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- At nearly nine minutes long, this track is a marathon. Pay attention to how the band uses the percussion break to hold tension. When the horns finally re-enter, what changes — and what stays exactly the same? This is the heartbeat that defined a city's sound.
- Most early go-go gets its energy from a live crowd. E Flat Boogie was cut in a studio. Listen for the moments where the band tries to fill the absence of an audience. Where does the call-and-response live when there's no one to answer back?
- The title isn't a metaphor. Try to hear the song's home key (E♭) — then notice how rarely the band leaves it. What keeps it from feeling static?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- The 1980 single label credits the band simply as "Trouble," not "Trouble Funk" — they hadn't fully settled on the longer name yet. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/374533-Trouble-E-Flat-Boogie and https://www.dustygroove.com/item/39410/Trouble-aka-Trouble-Funk-:E-Flat-Boogie-voc-inst
- The track is named for its musical key, E♭. The title is essentially a working name from the studio that stuck. — Background knowledge, no specific source found confirming this as the explicit origin of the title. (Multiple Discogs listings confirm the key reference but do not narrate the title's origin.)
- E Flat Boogie was a Tiny Desk closer when Trouble Funk squeezed twelve members behind NPR's desk in 2018; NPR's writeup calls it their "first hit in 1979," with Big Tony's vocals up front. — Source: https://www.npr.org/2018/05/30/612403223/trouble-funk-tiny-desk-concert
- The track was reissued in 2000 on Funky Delicacies as the title cut of a Trouble Funk early-singles compilation, then reissued again in 2016 by Tuff City Records on vinyl and digital. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E_Flat_Boogie and https://tuffcity.com/album/e-flat-boogie
- A second 1980 pressing released the vocal version on side A and the instrumental on side B of the 7", a deliberate move toward DJ-friendly formats just as hip-hop's 12" culture was taking shape. — Source: https://www.dustygroove.com/item/39410/Trouble-aka-Trouble-Funk-:E-Flat-Boogie-voc-inst

#### Album

- E Flat Boogie was a non-album single in 1980; the "album" of the same name is the 2000 Funky Delicacies compilation that gathers the band's pre–Sugar Hill singles (E Flat Boogie, Let's Get Small, Pump Me Up, So Early In The Morning, Hey Fellas, Trouble Funk Express). It received 4.5/5 from AllMusic. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E_Flat_Boogie and https://www.allmusic.com/album/e-flat-boogie-mw0000675708
- The 2000 compilation is dedicated to band members who have since died — Robert Reed, Mac Cary, Lonnie Duckett, and Herbert Hicks. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Complete_Collection_of_Trouble_Funk (parallel dedication on the related TF Records compilation)
- The 2016 Tuff City vinyl reissue sold out, then continued as a digital-only release on Bandcamp. — Source: https://tuffcity.com/album/e-flat-boogie

#### Artist

- Trouble Funk grew out of "Trouble Band and Show," a Top 40 cover group put together in 1975 by manager Reo Edwards, who recruited Big Tony Fisher at age 17. The band changed its name after a stage sign reading "Trouble will funk you" got chopped by sightlines so only "Trouble" and "Funk" were legible. — Source: https://www.jay-quan.com/post/drop-the-bomb-the-story-of-trouble-funk-d-c-go-go-with-trouble-funk-leader-big-tony-by-jayqua
- They learned go-go by sharing a residency with Chuck Brown at Club LeBaron in Palmer Park, Maryland — Trouble Funk took the early set, Brown took the late shift. The first original they wrote, "Roll With It," began as a botched attempt to play Kraftwerk's "Trans-Europe Express." — Source: https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2017/04/trouble-funk-feature/
- Co-founder and trombonist/keyboardist Robert "Syke Dyke" Reed (born August 4, 1957, Newport News, Virginia) died April 13, 2008 of pancreatic cancer at age 50. — Source: https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-dyn/content/article/2008/04/30/AR2008043003547.html (via search excerpt; full article behind paywall)
- The band's later D.E.T.T. label was an acronym standing for the founding members and an acknowledgment of "debt" after the Sugar Hill collapse: Dyke, Edwards, Taylor, Tony. — Source: https://www.warr.org/trouble.html
- Trouble Funk frequently shared bills with DC hardcore punk acts like Minor Threat and Big Boys during the early 1980s. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trouble_Funk
- Their later track "Pump Me Up" became one of the most-sampled records in hip-hop, used in Public Enemy's "Fight the Power," MARRS's "Pump Up The Volume," and Kurtis Blow's "If I Ruled the World." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trouble_Funk

### Genre Suggestions

- **Go-Go (current)** — Strongly defensible. The track is one of the canonical early documents of the genre, made by a band that helped define it in tandem with Chuck Brown's Soul Searchers. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trouble_Funk
- **Classic Funk** — Weaker case. The book defines Classic Funk as the central commercial form 1969–1977; E Flat Boogie sits just outside that window in 1980 and its identity is unmistakably go-go, not crossover funk. Not recommended as the primary tag.
- **Deep Funk** — A reasonable secondary case. E Flat Boogie went unrecognized nationally on its 1980 release, only finding a wider audience through the 2000 compilation, 2016 Tuff City reissue, and crate-digger appreciation. The book defines deep funk as "delayed recognition," which fits — but go-go remains the truer primary genre. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E_Flat_Boogie

### Region Suggestions

- **US - DC (current)** — Trouble Funk formed in Washington, D.C. in 1978 and is universally identified with DC as the home city of go-go. No change recommended. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trouble_Funk
