We end our journey where it began, with James Brown. About four and a half minutes into "Funky Drummer," Brown tells the band exactly what he wants, and just past the five-minute mark he barks: “1, 2, 3, 4, hit it!” The result is a twenty-second drum break that became a core rhythm of hip-hop.

The funky drummer is Clyde Stubblefield. His improvised creation has been sampled on over two thousand tracks, yet he never received royalties for it. He died of cancer in 2017, leaving unpaid medical bills behind.

Prince and Stubblefield never met, but Prince quietly covered much of his medical care during chemotherapy. Stubblefield’s wife revealed the financial support only after Prince’s death.

“Funky Drummer” was released as a two-part single on King Records in March 1970. It didn't appear on an album until the 1986 Polydor compilation *In the Jungle Groove*. The original 7-inch single split the performance into Part 1 (2m 36s) and Part 2 (2m 55s). The unedited version runs 9m 14s.

This is the power of funk: one groove, one funky drummer, one perfect break, and a whole new world built on top of it.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- The track was recorded on November 20, 1969, at King Studios in Cincinnati. The band had just arrived at sunrise from a gig the night before and were exhausted. There was no written music or plan; Brown simply turned on the red light and directed a jam. — Source: https://medium.com/orbital-music-park/the-legend-of-the-funky-drummer-4ffda9c7414f
- Brown appears to name the song spontaneously during the recording itself, repeating: "The name of this tune is 'The Funky Drummer', 'The Funky Drummer'." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funky_Drummer
- The original single version contains Brown's vocal percussion ("kooncha"), which differs from the album version. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funky_Drummer
- The iconic drum break occurs at approximately the 5:20 mark of the full version: an eight-bar, roughly 20-second unaccompanied drum solo that became the most sampled drum break in music history. — Source: https://theconversation.com/funky-drummer-how-a-james-brown-jam-session-gave-us-the-greatest-drum-break-of-them-all-134493
- Despite its later significance, the single only reached #51 on the Billboard Hot 100 and #20 on the R&B chart. It was not a major hit on release. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funky_Drummer
- The track did not appear on any album for 16 years, until the 1986 compilation *In the Jungle Groove*. Its rediscovery was driven by hip-hop producers, not by the original audience. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funky_Drummer
- The drum break has been sampled on over 1,000 recordings (some sources cite 1,366+), making it one of the most sampled pieces of music ever. Notable uses include Public Enemy's "Fight the Power" (1989), LL Cool J's "Mama Said Knock You Out" (1990), Run-DMC's "Run's House" (1988), George Michael's "Freedom '90" (1990), Sinéad O'Connor, Beastie Boys' "Shadrach" (1989), and Ed Sheeran's "Shirtsleeves" (2014). — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funky_Drummer, https://theconversation.com/funky-drummer-how-a-james-brown-jam-session-gave-us-the-greatest-drum-break-of-them-all-134493
- The drum break was used as the theme music for the animated series *The Powerpuff Girls*. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funky_Drummer

#### Album
- *In the Jungle Groove* was released in August 1986 on Polydor Records. It compiled James Brown's late-1960s/early-1970s funk instrumentals and became a key source record for hip-hop producers. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_the_Jungle_Groove
- The album version of "Funky Drummer" includes a "Bonus Beat Reprise," a drum loop layered only with vocal percussion, guitar, and tambourine. This section became the primary source for the iconic two-bar breakbeat that producers sampled. — Source: https://articles.roland.com/behind-the-beat-funky-drummer-by-james-brown/

#### Artist
- Clyde Stubblefield (April 18, 1943, Chattanooga, Tennessee, to February 18, 2017, Madison, Wisconsin) was self-taught and inspired by industrial sounds: factories, trains, and washing machines shaped his rhythmic sensibility. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clyde_Stubblefield
- Stubblefield joined James Brown's band in 1965 after performing in Macon, Georgia, where he had also worked with Otis Redding. He served as one of two primary drummers alongside John "Jabo" Starks. — Source: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/clyde-stubblefield-james-browns-funky-drummer/
- Stubblefield played on several other landmark James Brown recordings: "Cold Sweat," "Say It Loud, I'm Black and I'm Proud," "There Was a Time," and "Get Up (I Feel Like Being a) Sex Machine." — Source: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/clyde-stubblefield-james-browns-funky-drummer/
- Brown employed musicians on a work-for-hire basis and claimed sole authorship. Stubblefield received no songwriter credit and no royalties from any of the sampling. He said: "He didn't tell me what to play... I played what felt but he owned it." — Source: https://theconversation.com/the-story-of-the-funky-drummer-the-most-exploited-man-in-modern-music-73473
- Stubblefield died with approximately $90,000 in unpaid medical bills after battling bladder cancer and kidney disease. — Source: https://theconversation.com/the-story-of-the-funky-drummer-the-most-exploited-man-in-modern-music-73473
- Prince secretly paid approximately $80,000 toward Stubblefield's medical bills while Stubblefield was undergoing chemotherapy. The two had never met. Prince requested the donation be kept quiet; Stubblefield's wife Jody Hannon went public only after Prince's death on April 21, 2016. — Source: https://www.billboard.com/music/music-news/prince-funky-drummer-clyde-stubblefield-medical-bills-7348492/
- Bootsy Collins eulogized Stubblefield: "We lost another Pillar Stone that held up the Foundation of Funk." — Source: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/clyde-stubblefield-james-browns-funky-drummer/
- LA Weekly named Stubblefield the second-best drummer of all time in 2014. He received an honorary doctorate from the University of Wisconsin-Madison posthumously in 2017, and his drumsticks are displayed in the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clyde_Stubblefield
- James Brown was born May 3, 1933, in Barnwell, South Carolina, and died December 25, 2006, in Atlanta, Georgia. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Brown
