### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **The song that states the doctrine out loud.** Almost no funk record names its own organizing principle in the title. This one does. Bootsy Collins learned "the One" from James Brown as a bass instruction — "He wanted me to play on the one, to emphasize that first beat of every measure" — and then, in Bootsy's words, "George took it to another degree… Everything was on the one — everybody was emphasizing the one — even the vocals" (https://relix.com/articles/detail/bootsy-collins-on-james-brown-george-clinton-and-the-power-of-the-one/). The track is Clinton turning a rhythm-section note into scripture, and the entry can use it as the book's plainest available definition of what funk actually is.
- **A one-song manifesto in a nine-song comic book.** *The Clones of Dr. Funkenstein* is an elaborate sci-fi narrative — Starchild cloning funkateers to seed the Earth — and in the middle of Side Two, the mythology drops away and the band simply announces its method. Ace Records' sleeve notes call it "a one song summation of the refreshed Parliament groove philosophy" (https://acerecords.co.uk/the-clones-of-dr-funkenstein). Good angle for showing that the costumes were always a delivery system for a rhythmic idea.
- **The One is a claim, not a rule.** Worth a mildly heretical entry: musicians who talk about the One rarely play it literally. Bootsy himself says he plays *before* the One when the feel calls for it — "I can purposely play on the One when I'm feeling that, but I don't wanna be locked" (https://www.edgeoftheline.co/articles/theone). A track titled "Everything Is On The One" is an invitation to test whether everything actually is.
- **A jazz horn section on a funk sermon.** The horns here were arranged by Bernie Worrell, not Fred Wesley (Wesley took tracks 3–6; Worrell took 2, 7, 8, 9 — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Clones_of_Dr._Funkenstein), and the section contained Michael and Randy Brecker alongside Wesley and Maceo Parker. Two of the most in-demand New York jazz-session players in the world, on a P-Funk chant.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- The song is named after the downbeat. Count the bars for a minute and decide honestly: is the bass actually landing on the one, or is it arriving a hair early and letting the drums claim the beat? Bootsy Collins says he plays it both ways on purpose.
- The horn charts on this track are Bernie Worrell's, not Fred Wesley's, even though Wesley and Maceo Parker are both playing them. Compare the horn writing here with "Dr. Funkenstein" — which is Wesley's chart — and see whether you can hear the difference between a keyboard player's horn lines and a trombonist's.
- This is the shortest song on Side Two and the only one with no story attached. After eight tracks of clones, Starchild and intergalactic pharmacy, what changes when Parliament stops explaining the mythology and just tells you what the rule is?
- Michael and Randy Brecker — jazz session players who worked with Horace Silver and Frank Zappa — are somewhere in this horn section. Can you pick out a phrase that sounds like it wandered in from a different genre?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- The track was never issued as a single anywhere in the world. A Discogs search restricted to 7" releases containing "Everything Is On The One" returns only Argentine pressings of a *different* song, "Gamin' On Ya." The two singles pulled from the album were "Do That Stuff" (Casablanca NB 871, b/w "Handcuffs") and "Dr. Funkenstein" (Casablanca NB 875). — Source: https://www.discogs.com/master/15923-Parliament-The-Clones-Of-Dr-Funkenstein and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Clones_of_Dr._Funkenstein
- Written by George Clinton, Bootsy Collins and Bernie Worrell; horn arrangement by Worrell. Worrell arranged tracks 2, 7, 8 and 9; Fred Wesley arranged 3, 4, 5 and 6. So the ex–James Brown trombonist did *not* write the charts on the song about James Brown's rhythmic invention. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Clones_of_Dr._Funkenstein
- At 3:47 it is the second-shortest full song on the album (only the 1:40 "Prelude" is shorter), on a record whose other Side Two tracks run 4:47, 6:01 and 5:56. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/master/15923-Parliament-The-Clones-Of-Dr-Funkenstein
- Ace Records' reissue notes describe it as "a one song summation of the refreshed Parliament groove philosophy," pointing to the album's move away from long free-form structures toward tighter compositions. — Source: https://acerecords.co.uk/the-clones-of-dr-funkenstein
- The phrase in the title has an identifiable teacher. Bootsy Collins: "'On the one' is a musical term I learned from James Brown. He wanted me to play on the one, to emphasize that first beat of every measure… When I got with George, he made it a whole concept. Everything was on the one — everybody was emphasizing the one — even the vocals. So George took it to another degree." — Source: https://relix.com/articles/detail/bootsy-collins-on-james-brown-george-clinton-and-the-power-of-the-one/
- The phrase outlived the record. Rickey Vincent's 1996 history of the genre is titled *Funk: The Music, the People, and the Rhythm of The One*; it won the ASCAP Deems Taylor Award for music writing in 1997. Bootsy Collins named his 2021 album *The Power of the One*. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rickey_Vincent and https://relix.com/articles/detail/bootsy-collins-on-james-brown-george-clinton-and-the-power-of-the-one/
- Sampling status unverified — see Open Research Questions. WhoSampled's page for the track exists but could not be retrieved: https://www.whosampled.com/Parliament/Everything-Is-on-the-One/

#### Album

- *The Clones of Dr. Funkenstein* was Parliament's fifth studio album and their second gold record, reaching No. 3 on the *Billboard* R&B Albums chart and No. 20 on the pop chart. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Clones_of_Dr._Funkenstein
- Recorded at United Sound Systems in Detroit and Hollywood Sound Recorders in Hollywood; mastered by Allen Zentz; sleeve art directed and designed by Chris Whorf at Gribbitt!, with photography by Ron Slenzak. Casablanca cat. no. NBLP 7034, pressed at the Columbia plant in Pitman, New Jersey. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/70285-Parliament-The-Clones-Of-Dr-Funkenstein
- The horn section is a jazz-and-JB summit: Fred Wesley and Maceo Parker (both ex–James Brown), Rick Gardner, and the Brecker Brothers, Michael and Randy. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Clones_of_Dr._Funkenstein
- One of the backing vocalists, credited on the sleeve as "Taka Kahn," is Taka Boom — Chaka Khan's younger sister, who sang on several Parliament albums in the 1970s and who fronted the Undisputed Truth on their 1976 disco hit "You + Me = Love." — Sources: https://www.discogs.com/release/70285-Parliament-The-Clones-Of-Dr-Funkenstein and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taka_Boom
- Robert Christgau gave the album a B+, writing that "the message seems to be that clones are cool, and the proof seems to be the predictable yet effective funktoons that dominate the album." *Rolling Stone* gave it five stars; AllMusic 4.5. — Sources: https://www.robertchristgau.com/get_chap.php?k=P&bk=70 and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Clones_of_Dr._Funkenstein
- Casablanca's cross-promotion tells you a lot about the label's 1976: some copies shipped with an order sheet and envelope for Parliament *and* Donna Summer merchandise — T-shirts, a life-size stand-up, a moon mobile, a satin pillowcase — plus a free bonus disco LP for returning the questionnaire. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/70285-Parliament-The-Clones-Of-Dr-Funkenstein

#### Artist

- The Mothership — the stage prop that defined the Parliament live show — made its first landing at the Municipal Auditorium in New Orleans in late October 1976, months after this album, on the P-Funk Earth Tour. Sources differ between October 26 and October 27; at the first show the crew landed it at the *opening* of the concert and immediately realised nothing could follow it, so from the next night on it came near the end. — Source: https://calendar.songfacts.com/october/26/21027 and https://andscape.com/features/forty-years-later-george-clintons-mothership-is-still-landing/

### Genre Suggestions

The book's subgenre definitions are in `Matter/On Subgenres.md`. The `genre` field is currently empty.

1. **Psychedelic Funk — recommended.** The book explicitly defines this label as its home for the George Clinton universe: "At the center of the sound is George Clinton and his Parliament-Funkadelic universe… I use 'psychedelic funk' as the broader label." The sibling entry from this same album, `Parliament - Dr. Funkenstein - 07-20.md`, is tagged Psychedelic Funk, and consistency across two tracks from one record argues for the same tag. Discogs styles the release as "P.Funk," which is the same claim in Discogs' vocabulary. — Sources: `Matter/On Subgenres.md`; https://www.discogs.com/master/15923-Parliament-The-Clones-Of-Dr-Funkenstein

2. **Classic Funk — a real alternative.** The book draws classic funk as "roughly 1969 through 1977," defined by major-label release, wide distribution, radio play and chart presence. A 1976 Casablanca album that went gold and hit No. 3 R&B / No. 20 pop is squarely inside that. And unlike most of the album, this track carries no sci-fi narrative at all — no clones, no Starchild, no mythology. It is a plain, un-costumed funk chant. If the author wants one entry that demonstrates P-Funk without the psychedelia, this is the track and Classic Funk is the honest tag. The counter-argument is that the surrounding album is unambiguously psychedelic and the tag is meant to point at the family tree, not the individual cut.

3. **Not recommended: Electro Funk or Funk Rock.** Worrell's synthesizers are present but the book restricts Electro Funk to records "defined by [their] electronic texture," which this is not — it is horns, bass and drums. There is no rock guitar posture here either, despite Michael Hampton being in the room.

### Region Suggestions

The book's regions are enumerated in `Planning/Field Values.md`; `Matter/On Regions.md` notes that region can reasonably mean where the artist is from, where the band formed, where the label operated, or where the recording happened, and that a decision is made per entry.

1. **US - Michigan — recommended, and written to the field.** The album was recorded at United Sound Systems in Detroit (with overdub/mix work at Hollywood Sound in Hollywood). Detroit is also where Clinton's operation was based through the P-Funk years, and United Sound is the studio most identified with the Parliament-Funkadelic catalogue. Decisively, the sibling entry from the same album already uses `US - Michigan`, so this keeps the book internally consistent. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Clones_of_Dr._Funkenstein; https://www.discogs.com/release/70285-Parliament-The-Clones-Of-Dr-Funkenstein

2. **US - Northeast — the origin-story case.** The Parliaments formed in the back of a Plainfield, New Jersey barbershop, and the New Jersey thread runs straight through this track's personnel: Bernie Worrell was born in Long Branch, New Jersey, and Garry Shider and Glenn Goins were both born in Plainfield. If the author wants region to mean "where these people come from" rather than "where the tape rolled," `US - Northeast` is defensible. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernie_Worrell; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garry_Shider; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenn_Goins

3. **US - California — the label case, and the weakest.** Casablanca Records operated out of Los Angeles, part of the album was cut at Hollywood Sound Recorders, and it was mastered at Allen Zentz Mastering in Hollywood. This is where the record was finished and sold from, but not where its sound came from. Mentioned for completeness only. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/70285-Parliament-The-Clones-Of-Dr-Funkenstein

No case for extending the region enumeration arises here; `US - Michigan` is a valid enumerated value and fits.
