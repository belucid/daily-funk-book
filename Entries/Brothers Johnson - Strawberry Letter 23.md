### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The cover that ate the original."** Shuggie Otis wrote the song at 17 and let it sit unnoticed on a 1971 album. The Brothers Johnson, Quincy Jones, and Lee Ritenour together made it a #1 R&B hit and a Tarantino touchstone, yet most listeners have no idea it's a cover. The entry could frame the track as a master class in what a producer-and-band team can do with a quiet song.

2. **"Strawberry-scented vinyl and other Quincy theater."** A meditation on Quincy Jones's instinct to package funk as a full sensory event: the strawberry-scented red 12-inch, the gleaming arrangement, Lee Ritenour's spacey solo cut a half-tone above the original. A hook into how 70s funk's biggest hits often arrived as luxury objects, not just records.

3. **"How a love letter becomes a groove."** The song's lyric is a cryptic, surreal letter the listener reads over the band's shoulder. The entry could lean into the strangeness of a #1 R&B hit whose title phrase never appears in the song, and whose words feel less like communication than like a dream.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. "Listen for Lee Ritenour's guitar solo, especially the spacey outro that floats out past four minutes. Does it feel like a tribute to Shuggie Otis's original, or has Ritenour quietly redrawn the song?"

2. "Count how many times the phrase 'Strawberry Letter 23' is actually sung. (Spoiler: zero.) Where would you have placed it, and why do you think Otis left it out?"

3. "Tune in to Louis Johnson's bass under the second verse. He's a 22-year-old future Thriller session player here. What does he do that a standard funk bass line wouldn't?"

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- The 12-inch single was pressed on red, strawberry-scented vinyl, with a strawberry-scented jacket. Reportedly the scent has faded from most surviving copies. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strawberry_Letter_23
- The 12" version was recorded a half-tone higher than the album cut, with a different intro, and B-sided with an extended "Get The Funk Out Ma Face." It was cut at 45 RPM, unusual for early A&M 12-inches. — Source: https://forum.amcorner.com/threads/brothers-johnson-strawberry-letter-23-sp-12003.11448/
- The title phrase "Strawberry Letter 23" never appears in the song's lyrics. Otis pictured a girl handing a guy a pink envelope, with the song's words being the contents of letter number 23, the previous 22 implied. — Source: https://classicsongoftheday.com/strawberry-letter-23-the-brothers-johnson/
- Shuggie Otis has denied the persistent myth that the lyric was inspired by scented letters from a girlfriend. — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/the-brothers-johnson/strawberry-letter-23
- The single entered the Billboard Hot 100 at #71 on June 26, 1977, climbed sharply after the brothers performed it on American Bandstand, and peaked at #5 on September 24, 1977, spending 19 weeks on the chart. — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/the-brothers-johnson/strawberry-letter-23
- It topped the Billboard R&B singles chart on July 31, 1977. — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/the-brothers-johnson/strawberry-letter-23
- Quincy Jones hired studio guitarist Lee Ritenour to recreate (and arguably surpass) Shuggie Otis's original guitar solo. The "spacey, futuristic feel" of the outro is Ritenour's. — Source: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/brothers-johnson-strawberry-letter-23-song/
- George Johnson discovered the song through one of Shuggie Otis's cousins, whom he was dating. — Source: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/brothers-johnson-strawberry-letter-23-song/
- Louis Johnson loved the original so much he used the Shuggie Otis recording as his wedding soundtrack. — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/the-brothers-johnson/strawberry-letter-23
- Quentin Tarantino used the track briefly in Pulp Fiction (1994) as Jules and Vincent walk down a hallway, then prominently in Jackie Brown (1997). It has also appeared in Six Feet Under's finale and an Entourage episode. — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/the-brothers-johnson/strawberry-letter-23
- A 2002 Kellogg's Special K Red Berries commercial used a re-recorded version (by local artists) to avoid performance rights payments. — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/the-brothers-johnson/strawberry-letter-23
- Pitchfork ranked the Brothers Johnson version the 134th best song of the 1970s. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strawberry_Letter_23
- Tevin Campbell, then 13, recorded a third Quincy Jones-produced version for his 1991 debut T.E.V.I.N., later included on the 1992 soundtrack to Boomerang. — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/the-brothers-johnson/strawberry-letter-23

#### Album
- Right on Time was recorded February 1 to March 21, 1977 at A&M Studios in Hollywood, and released May 10, 1977. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_on_Time_(Brothers_Johnson_album)
- The album peaked at #13 on the Billboard 200 and #2 on the Top Soul Albums chart, and was certified Platinum by the RIAA. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_on_Time_(Brothers_Johnson_album)
- The instrumental track "Q," a tribute to Quincy Jones, won the Grammy for Best R&B Instrumental Performance at the 1978 (20th Annual) Grammy Awards. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_on_Time_(Brothers_Johnson_album)
- "Strawberry Letter 23" is the only cover on the album, and Jones picked it as the first single. — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/the-brothers-johnson/strawberry-letter-23
- Core personnel beyond the brothers: Harvey Mason Sr. on drums, Ralph MacDonald on percussion, Dave Grusin and Ian Underwood on keyboards. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_on_Time_(Brothers_Johnson_album)

#### Artist
- George Johnson (lead guitar/vocals, nickname "Lightnin' Licks") was born May 17, 1953; Louis Johnson (bass, nickname "Thunder Thumbs") was born April 13, 1955. Both were born in Los Angeles. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Johnson_(bassist)
- Before going by The Brothers Johnson, George and Louis played in Johnson Three Plus One with their older brother Tommy and cousin Alex Weir, then backed Bobby Womack and the Supremes on tour. — Source: https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/brothers-johnson-1973/
- George and Louis joined Billy Preston's band and contributed songs to his albums Music Is My Life and The Kids & Me before going on their own in 1973. — Source: https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/brothers-johnson-1973/
- Quincy Jones first hired them for his 1975 album Mellow Madness, which led directly to him producing their debut Look Out for #1 (1976, also Platinum) and Right on Time. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Brothers_Johnson
- Louis Johnson played bass on Michael Jackson's Thriller, including "Billie Jean" and "Wanna Be Startin' Somethin'." He was ranked #38 on Bass Player magazine's "100 Greatest Bass Players of All Time." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Johnson_(bassist)
- Louis Johnson died May 21, 2015 in Las Vegas at age 60. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Johnson_(bassist)
- The duo had three #1 R&B singles: "I'll Be Good to You" (1976), "Strawberry Letter 23" (1977), and "Stomp!" (1980). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Brothers_Johnson

### Genre Suggestions

1. **Classic Funk (recommended).** The track is squarely inside the 1969–1977 classic-funk window, came out on a major label (A&M) with full chart and radio presence, topped the R&B chart, and is funk-forward rather than rock-, jazz-, or Latin-fused. The fact that it's a polished, Quincy Jones-produced hit is more "classic funk at its commercial peak" than any sub-flavor. Source for era and definition: project's own On Genres & Regions.md.

2. **Jazz-Funk.** A reasonable alternative because Quincy Jones is a jazz producer, Lee Ritenour is a jazz-fusion guitarist, and the track's spacey solo section, longer-than-radio length (4:58), and harmonic sophistication all lean jazz-funk. But it leans on a strong vocal chorus and a short single edit, which the project's definition frames as more classic-funk than jazz-funk. — Source: project's On Genres & Regions.md.

3. **Psychedelic Funk.** A stretch, but defensible given the song's surreal lyric ("strawberry letters," a girl on a flying carpet) and Ritenour's spacey outro, which descend directly from Shuggie Otis's psychedelic-soul original. Not the strongest fit because the Brothers Johnson production is too polished and conventional to feel hallucinatory. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strawberry_Letter_23 (genre listed as "psychedelic soul, psychedelic rock" for the Otis original)

**Suggested:** Classic Funk.
