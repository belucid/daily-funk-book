James Brown was a larger-than-life figure who cast a long shadow. Some artists, like Bootsy Collins, had to break away from Brown rather than try to share his spotlight. Others, including saxophonist Maceo Parker and trombonist, bandleader, and musical director Fred Wesley, found ways to step forward from inside the machine. One of those ways was through the James Brown-produced JB’s albums, which featured the band without the frontman.

“Little Boy Black” comes from the JB’s 1974 album *Breakin’ Bread*. Fred Wesley opens the track by thanking listeners for buying their records, then follows with a proto-rap that, to my ear, sets the template for Morris Day and Jerome’s comic interplay in The Time a decade later.

James Brown is credited only as producer, but his voice is clearly audible in the second half of the song. Even with Brown’s stamp all over it, and even with the album on his own label, it is hard to imagine him putting this much focus on multiple trombone solos on one of his own records.

The album marked the end of the Fred Wesley era of the JB’s. He left soon after to join George Clinton and Parliament-Funkadelic.

- **Exact release month of Breakin' Bread.** Sources conflict: Apple Music lists January 10, 1974; multiple retailer and review sites list October 1974. October fits the album's internal chronology (the "Rockin' Funky Watergate" single followed Nixon's August 1974 resignation), while the January Apple Music date looks like a default digital-reissue date. Treating the year as 1974 without a firm month.
- **Exact writer credits for "Little Boy Black."** Discogs release pages were blocked to WebFetch. Aggregated summaries from Discogs results list the album's writing credits as a rotating pool of Fred Wesley, James Brown, St. Clair Pinckney, Bob Both, and Deidre Brown across the eight tracks; Deezer's metadata names James Brown as the composer of "Little Boy Black." Most other J.B.'s originals of this era carry a Brown/Wesley co-credit, so that's the likely pairing, but I could not land an original-label credit scan to confirm.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"James Brown's band recycles James Brown."** "Little Boy Black" is Fred Wesley building a whole track out of Brown's own catalog: the chorus lyric rewrites "I Don't Want Nobody to Give Me Nothing" ("I don't need nobody tell me how to blow my horn"), the rhythm bed borrows from "Down and Out in New York City," and the guitar line nods at "Good Foot." The theme could be: when James Brown's universe is the only language you speak, how do you say something new inside it? (Sources: https://www.funkmysoul.gr/fred-wesley-the-j-b-s-1974-breakin-bread/ and aggregated search descriptions.)

2. **"The last great J.B.'s album."** Breakin' Bread is routinely called "the last great J.B.'s album" by reviewers. By 1974 Wesley was months away from walking out to join George Clinton. The entry could frame "Little Boy Black" as a band at its absolute peak right before the exit. (Sources: https://www.funkmysoul.gr/fred-wesley-the-j-b-s-1974-breakin-bread/, https://rateyourmusic.com/release/album/fred-and-the-new-j_b_s/breakin-bread/)

3. **"Proto-rap on People Records."** Most Breakin' Bread tracks open with a Fred Wesley spoken monologue, and contemporaries flag "Little Boy Black," "I Wanna Get Down," and the title track as carrying "early go-go and rap-styled elements." The theme: here's a 1974 funk record that's already doing what rap and go-go would be named for a few years later.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. You'll hear the chorus line "I don't need nobody tell me how to blow my horn." Fred Wesley is quoting James Brown's own "I Don't Want Nobody to Give Me Nothing." Is Wesley paying tribute, showing off, or taking a quiet jab at the boss?

2. Lock onto Fred Thomas's bass line. It never stops repeating. Is the rest of the band riding it, fighting it, or ignoring it?

3. This track was recorded in 1974, a full five years before "Rapper's Delight" and before D.C. had a name for go-go. Listen for the moments that sound like hip-hop or go-go before either genre existed. Where do you hear them?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- The track's hook, "I don't need nobody tell me how to blow my horn…" is Fred Wesley rewriting the chorus of James Brown's 1969 hit "I Don't Want Nobody to Give Me Nothing (Open Up the Door, I'll Get It Myself)." — Source: https://www.funkmysoul.gr/fred-wesley-the-j-b-s-1974-breakin-bread/
- The rhythm bed is close to Brown's 1973 "Down and Out in New York City" from the Black Caesar soundtrack, and the guitar line echoes "Good Foot" with a passing reference to "Mind Power." The whole track is built out of Brown's own musical vocabulary. — Source: https://www.funkmysoul.gr/fred-wesley-the-j-b-s-1974-breakin-bread/
- James Brown's voice is audible on "Little Boy Black" itself, even though Brown is billed as producer, not performer, for the Breakin' Bread sessions. — Source: https://www.funkmysoul.gr/fred-wesley-the-j-b-s-1974-breakin-bread/
- Fred Thomas anchors the song with a "persistent bass hook," and the band delivers "gospelfide vocalizing" behind it — a looser group-vocal feel than the tighter call-and-response of earlier J.B.'s sides. — Source: https://www.funkmysoul.gr/fred-wesley-the-j-b-s-1974-breakin-bread/
- Streaming masters (Spotify, Deezer, Apple Music) time the track at 4:32; the original LP back-cover listing shows 3:54, suggesting the streaming version preserves more of the intro or tail than the 1974 LP cut. — Sources: https://www.deezer.com/en/album/74437452, https://open.spotify.com/track/3DvvtufdDb1Oo4oQ22Hi2r

#### Album

- Breakin' Bread is widely called "the last great J.B.'s album," released on James Brown's People label (catalog PE 6604) while Wesley was already drifting toward George Clinton's orbit. Wesley formally left Brown's organization in 1975. — Sources: https://www.funkmysoul.gr/fred-wesley-the-j-b-s-1974-breakin-bread/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Wesley
- Most tracks open with a Fred Wesley spoken-word "reminiscence" dubbed over the intro — an unusual production choice for a 1974 funk LP that puts the record in conversation with what would soon become rap. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_J.B.%27s
- Contemporary description credits Breakin' Bread, particularly "Little Boy Black," "I Wanna Get Down," and the title track, with "early go-go and rap-styled influences," predating Chuck Brown's go-go recordings and the first commercial rap singles. — Source: https://www.funkmysoul.gr/fred-wesley-the-j-b-s-1974-breakin-bread/
- The album was recorded and mixed at Sound Ideas Studio A in New York City, with additional work at A&R Studios (NYC) and Criteria Studios (Miami). Engineer was Bob Both. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/master/52746-Fred-The-New-JBs-Breakin-Bread (aggregated metadata)
- Personnel credited on Breakin' Bread include Fred Wesley (trombone, arranger), Maceo Parker, Jimmy Parker, St. Clair Pinckney, Russell Crimes, "Sweet" Charles Sherrell, Fred Thomas (bass), John "Jabo" Starks (drums), and percussionists Johnny Griggs and John Morgan. — Source: Aggregated Discogs/retailer metadata
- The album's reissue on Get On Down (GET54074) describes it as an "air-tight, eight course meal" of funk grooves, framed as a Nixon-era socially conscious record, with the title track calling for family reconnection during hard economic times. — Source: https://www.banquetrecords.com/fred-wesley-%26-the-new-jb%27s/breakin%27-bread/GET54074

#### Artist

- The J.B.'s served as James Brown's backing band from roughly 1970 through the early 1980s, producing parallel instrumental albums under various "J.B.'s" and "Fred Wesley & the J.B.'s" billings, almost all released on Brown's own People Records. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_J.B.%27s
- Fred Wesley (born July 4, 1943, Columbus, Georgia; raised in Mobile, Alabama) was the band's music director, arranger, trombonist, and primary composer from 1968 through 1975, and is credited as a key architect of the band's shift from soul to funk. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Wesley
- Bassist Fred Thomas, whose hook drives "Little Boy Black," was hired out of Small's Paradise in Harlem in 1971 after Brown heard him play; he went on to record with Brown for more than thirty years. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Thomas_(bassist)
- Wesley's 1973 J.B.'s single "Doing It to Death" was certified gold by the RIAA in July 1973, the band's highest commercial peak. Breakin' Bread followed about a year later. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Wesley
- After leaving Brown in 1975, Wesley spent several years with George Clinton's Parliament-Funkadelic, including leading the Horny Horns, and later joined the Count Basie Orchestra in 1978. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Wesley

### Genre Suggestions

Per the book's definitions in `Matter/On Genres & Regions.md`:

1. **Classic Funk.** The strongest fit. Breakin' Bread was on a major-label pipeline (People via Polydor), produced by James Brown, and the record's primary identity is funk. "Little Boy Black" sits squarely inside the 1968–1977 classic era the book defines, and its DNA is literally recycled James Brown funk. — Sources: book's own definition; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_J.B.%27s

2. **Deep Funk.** Also defensible. Breakin' Bread was not a commercial success on par with earlier J.B.'s records, and "Little Boy Black" was never a single; its audience is largely crate-diggers and funk completists who found it via reissues and samples. The book's "delayed recognition" test applies. — Source: book's definition; album's commercial trajectory

3. **Psychedelic Funk.** A weaker but interesting case. The spoken-word intros, the recycled-Brown collage construction, and the "menacing horn heavy" tone share something with P-Funk's spoken theatricality, and Wesley was about to join Clinton within months. Probably not the right label, but worth noting.

My lean: **Classic Funk** as primary, with Deep Funk as the defensible alternative if the author wants the "crate-dig rediscovery" angle.

### Region Suggestions

1. **US - Southeast (status quo).** James Brown was raised in Augusta, Georgia, and ran his operation from there; Fred Wesley was born in Columbus, Georgia and raised in Mobile, Alabama; the People Records operation and the J.B.'s core identity is rooted in Brown's Georgia/Carolina Southeast base.
