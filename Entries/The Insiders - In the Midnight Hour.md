### Open Research Questions

The author left no `open:` lines in this entry. The single most important unanswered question raised by the research is recorded here anyway.

**Who were The Insiders?** Unresolved. No source anywhere identifies the personnel, the origin, or even whether "The Insiders" was a real working band as opposed to a one-off studio name for James Brown's players.

What I did, and what it produced:

- Discogs artist page for The Insiders (3) has an entirely blank profile: https://www.discogs.com/artist/808436-The-Insiders-3
- The James Brown SuperFan Club, which catalogues People 45s release by release, has a page for this exact record and offers nothing beyond "Funky soul covers from the The Insiders" plus the James Brown production credit: https://supersoulsound.com/jbsuperfan/1974-people-45-the-insiders-in-the-midnight-hourlonely-teardrops/
- Bob Both's own published discography (he engineered the record) does not list The Insiders at all: https://www.recordproduction.com/features/bob-both-discography
- No AllMusic artist page, no Wikipedia article, no MusicBrainz recording. A MusicBrainz API query for artist "The Insiders" + recording "In the Midnight Hour" returns zero results: https://musicbrainz.org/ws/2/recording/?query=artist:%22The%20Insiders%22%20AND%20recording:%22In%20the%20Midnight%20Hour%22&fmt=json
- 45cat has an artist page but blocks automated retrieval (HTTP 403 both directly and via proxy): https://www.45cat.com/artist/the-insiders
- Searches of Billboard-era trade coverage, soul/funk collector forums, and JB fan blogs turned up nothing beyond sale listings.

**The strong circumstantial answer** is that The Insiders were the J.B.'s (or an ad hoc studio band built around them) under a throwaway name. The evidence:

1. James Brown produced it, and Fred Wesley — Brown's musical director — arranged the A-side. https://www.discogs.com/release/4750265-The-Insiders-3-In-The-Midnight-Hour-Lonely-Teardrops
2. People was doing exactly this at exactly this moment. PE 637 (seven catalogue numbers earlier) is **The Devils** — "The X-Sorcist" b/w a cover of Booker T. & the M.G.'s' "Hip Hug-Her" — and multiple sources state plainly that The Devils *were* the J.B.'s under a pseudonym, with a probable lineup of Fred Wesley, Maceo Parker, Lew Soloff, Joe Farrell, Pee Wee Ellis, Joe Beck, Gordon Edwards, Jimmy Madison and Sue Evans. https://kaydeerecords.com/products/the-devils-the-x-orcist-hip-hug-her and https://supersoulsound.com/jbsuperfan/1974people-45-the-devils-the-x-sorcisthip-hug-her/
3. PE 642 is **The Progressors** — another name that appears once and never again. Discogs label listing: https://www.discogs.com/label/1191202-People-8
4. The 2005 crate-digger compilation *Nothing But Funk Volume 5 (10 JB Produced Funky Selections 1965-1976)* files The Insiders' "In The Midnight Hour" alongside The Devils' "Hip Hug-Her" and JB's Wedge's "Bessie" — i.e. it treats all three as products of the same JB studio machine. https://www.discogs.com/release/1487641-Various-Nothing-But-Funk-Volume-5-10-JB-Produced-Funky-Selections-1965-1976

I could not find a source that states this outright for The Insiders specifically. **The J.B.'s-under-a-pseudonym reading is inference from the pattern, not a documented fact, and should be written as such.**

**A second, smaller unresolved item:** Discogs' "The Insiders (3)" entry also carries a 1972 private-press 45, "An American Trilogy" b/w "Come Along With Me" (no label, NR2554-1, produced by Stan Gunselman, noted "Recorded in Nashville"), and a 1987 Westwood One radio transcription disc shared with The Beat Farmers. https://www.discogs.com/release/12440149-The-Insiders-3-An-American-Trilogy — Neither has any plausible connection to a 1974 James Brown production. Almost certainly the Discogs artist entry has been mis-merged from three different acts. Do not repeat "The Insiders also cut a version of 'An American Trilogy'" as fact.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

**"Soul is the song, funk is the treatment."** The entry's own premise, sharpened: the notes, the words, the chord changes here are 100% Wilson Pickett's 1965 Stax record. Everything that makes this version funk was added afterward, by arrangement. Because the source is so famous and so exactly preserved, the record works as a controlled experiment — the only variable changed is the rhythmic philosophy. It lets you define funk by subtraction: play the same song, strip the Memphis delayed backbeat, put everything on the one, and hear what happens.

**"The men with no names."** People Records in 1974 was quietly issuing 45s by acts that did not exist — The Devils, The Progressors, The Insiders — all produced by James Brown, all almost certainly the J.B.'s in a false moustache. That opens up a genuinely strange corner of funk history: a band so prolific and so contractually tangled that it had to release records under aliases, and an obscurity that is not accidental but manufactured. The Insiders' anonymity is the story.

**"Covering the competition."** Brown's shop reaching over to Memphis and taking Stax's crown jewels — "In the Midnight Hour" here, "Hip Hug-Her" on the Devils' B-side — is not neutral repertoire choice. It's Cincinnati/Augusta funk annexing Memphis soul, six months apart, on the same label. What does it mean when the Godfather has his band cover Booker T. and Wilson Pickett rather than write something new?

**"The B-side is a graveyard."** Flip it over and you get "Lonely Teardrops," Jackie Wilson's 1958 hit — the record whose royalties bankrolled Berry Gordy's founding of Motown, and the song Jackie Wilson was singing when he collapsed on stage from a heart attack in 1975, a year after this 45. One obscure 7" quietly holds the seed money of Motown and the last words of Jackie Wilson's career.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

Note: the `listening mission` field is currently empty.

- Wilson Pickett's original famously puts the accent on the *two* — Jerry Wexler literally danced "the Jerk" in the Stax studio to show the band what he wanted. Play the 1965 record, then play this one. Where has the emphasis moved, and at what moment do you stop hearing a soul record and start hearing a funk record?
- Everything on this record is borrowed except the arrangement. Ignore the melody entirely for one pass and listen only to the drums and bass. If you took the vocal away, would you be able to name the song?
- The horn charts are Fred Wesley's. Listen for the difference between a Memphis horn line — long, warm, singing along with the vocal — and a J.B.'s horn line, which stabs and gets out of the way. Which one is this, and does it ever switch?
- It runs three minutes and eleven seconds. That's a 45 built for radio, not a groove built to stretch. Does the track feel cut short to you, or exactly right? Compare with any J.B.'s A-side of the same year.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- The record is produced by James Brown and arranged by Fred Wesley (A-side), engineered by Bob Both and mastered by Stuart J. Romaine at Frankford/Wayne, NYC — a full James Brown production team applied to a group nobody can identify. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/4750265-The-Insiders-3-In-The-Midnight-Hour-Lonely-Teardrops
- The label copy on the 45 carries the credit line "James Brown — Minister Of New New Super Heavy Funk," one of Brown's rotating self-bestowed titles, stamped onto a record he does not appear on. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/4750265-The-Insiders-3-In-The-Midnight-Hour-Lonely-Teardrops
- Production is credited jointly to "James Brown and National Progressive Sound." I could find no documentation anywhere of what National Progressive Sound was — a production company, a studio, or a Brown-controlled shell. Worth flagging to a fact-checker as an unverified label credit. **Background knowledge, but no source found.**
- The two sides are both covers of other labels' hits: a Stax A-side and a Brunswick/Motown-lineage B-side. Neither is an original. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/master/579183-The-Insiders-3-In-The-Midnight-Hour-Lonely-Teardrops
- The B-side "Lonely Teardrops" is arranged not by Wesley but by Dave Matthews, the New York jazz arranger — different arranger for each side of the same 45. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/4750265-The-Insiders-3-In-The-Midnight-Hour-Lonely-Teardrops
- The promo pressing (white label, "For DJ Use Only Not For Sale") puts "In The Midnight Hour" on *both* sides — People was pushing this as the A-side hard. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/2180606-The-Insiders-3-In-The-Midnight-Hour
- The original: recorded at Stax, Memphis, on 12 May 1965; released June 1965 on Atlantic; #1 R&B, #21 Hot 100. Its rhythmic signature — the delayed backbeat — came from Atlantic president Jerry Wexler getting up in the studio and dancing "the Jerk" to show the band where the accent went. Steve Cropper: "this was the way the kids were dancing; they were putting the accent on two." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_the_Midnight_Hour
- "Lonely Teardrops" (Jackie Wilson, Brunswick, released 17 November 1958) was written by Berry Gordy, his sister Gwen Gordy and Roquel "Billy" Davis under the pseudonym Tyran Carlo. Gordy used the money it made to found Motown within a year. It was also the last song Jackie Wilson ever performed: he collapsed from a heart attack mid-phrase, on "my heart is crying," in 1975. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lonely_Teardrops
- The track has a real collector following for a record with no known artist: 65 Discogs wantlists against 67 copies in collections on the stock pressing alone. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/4750265-The-Insiders-3-In-The-Midnight-Hour-Lonely-Teardrops

#### Artist

- Nothing verifiable is known about The Insiders. See Open Research Questions above. This is genuinely unusual — most 1974 major-distributed 45s leave *some* trace.
- Context for the label: People Records was founded by James Brown in 1971, its first release being "Escape-ism" that May. Ownership passed to Polydor on 1 July 1971 as part of Brown's contract deal. It ran to over fifty singles and nine albums before closing in 1976, making it the longest-lived of Brown's own labels. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/label/1191202-People-8 and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Record_labels_owned_by_James_Brown
- People deliberately released material by "performers associated with the James Brown Revue" — Lyn Collins, Bobby Byrd, Sweet Charles, Maceo, the J.B.'s — with Brown himself often playing organ or singing backing uncredited. The Insiders fit that pattern exactly. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Record_labels_owned_by_James_Brown
- Brown's band recording under aliases has deep precedent: his group cut "(Do the) Mashed Potatoes" as "Nat Kendrick & The Swans," and the J.B.'s appeared variously as Fred Wesley & The J.B.'s, Maceo and the Macks, A.A.B.B., The First Family and The Last Word. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_J.B.%27s
- The catalogue numbers on either side of PE 644 place the record precisely in the J.B.'s run: PE 643 is Fred Wesley & The J.B.'s "Rockin' Funky Watergate," PE 645 is Sweet Charles' "Dedicated To The One I Love," PE 648 is Fred & The New J.B.'s "Breakin' Bread." — Source: https://www.discogs.com/label/1191202-People-8
- Bob Both, the engineer, became James Brown's recording engineer, A&R director and production supervisor at age 20 in 1972, and over five years worked on 17 albums and more than 69 singles for Brown and his stable. — Source: https://www.recordproduction.com/interviews/bob-both

### Genre Suggestions

The `genre` field is currently empty. Reading the definitions in `Matter/On Subgenres.md`:

**Suggestion 1 — Deep Funk (recommended).** The book defines deep funk as "less a sound than a commercial fate": tracks overlooked on release that found their audience later through crate-digging DJs, where a well-known label means "a poorly selling record with limited distribution," and "the defining trait of deep funk is the delayed recognition." This record is close to the platonic case. It charted nowhere, the group left no trace, and its entire afterlife is via diggers: comped by the specialist reissue label Funky People in 2005 on a record explicitly marketed as JB-produced rarities, and now sitting at 65 wantlists on Discogs. https://www.discogs.com/release/1487641-Various-Nothing-But-Funk-Volume-5-10-JB-Produced-Funky-Selections-1965-1976

**Suggestion 2 — Classic Funk.** The counter-case is real. 1974 sits squarely inside the book's 1969–1977 classic window; People was Polydor-distributed, not a private press; it got a full promo pressing pushed to DJs; and its primary identity is funk, not funk fused with something else. Discogs styles it "Soul, Funk." https://www.discogs.com/release/2180606-The-Insiders-3-In-The-Midnight-Hour — The tension between the two labels is itself worth a sentence in the entry: this record had every advantage of the commercial pipeline and still vanished.

**Suggestion 3 — Gospel Funk / others: no.** Nothing supports any other category. If the author wants a third option it would be a straight "Soul" tag, which the book does not offer — which is arguably the point of the entry.

### Region Suggestions

The `region` field was empty; I have set it to `US - New York`, a value that appears verbatim in `Planning/Field Values.md`.

**Suggestion 1 — US - New York (chosen).** The physical evidence on the disc points to New York: the runout is stamped "F/W NYC SJR," i.e. mastered at Frankford/Wayne Mastering Labs, New York, by Stuart J. Romaine. The engineer, Bob Both, came out of Polydor's New York office and was Brown's New York–based engineer and A&R director from 1972. The B-side arranger credited on the label, Dave Matthews, was a New York jazz arranger. And the parallel People pseudonym single, The Devils' "The X-Sorcist," is reported to have used a New York session cast — Lew Soloff, Joe Farrell, Joe Beck, Gordon Edwards, Jimmy Madison, Sue Evans. https://www.discogs.com/release/4750265-The-Insiders-3-In-The-Midnight-Hour-Lonely-Teardrops , https://www.recordproduction.com/interviews/bob-both , https://kaydeerecords.com/products/the-devils-the-x-orcist-hip-hug-her

**Suggestion 2 — US - Southeast.** If the author prefers to tag by the creative source rather than the studio, this record is a James Brown production arranged by Fred Wesley — the Augusta, Georgia axis. Defensible, and consistent if other JB-stable entries in the book use it. Worth checking against how the book tags the neighbouring J.B.'s entries so the family stays coherent.

**Suggestion 3 — US - Ohio.** Weak but not absurd: People began in the King Records orbit in Cincinnati, and Brown's sound is historically a Cincinnati product. By 1974, however, People's first five releases were long past and distribution had been Polydor's since July 1971, so Ohio is the least supportable of the three. https://www.discogs.com/label/1191202-People-8
