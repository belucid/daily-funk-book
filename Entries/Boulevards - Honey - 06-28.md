Boulevards’ fifth album, *Carolina Funk: Barn Burner on Tobacco Road*, was released on June 28, 2024. Boulevards is the nu-funk project of Jamil Rashad, built around a rotating cast of players from the retro-funk scene. 

The title deserves a moment. "Carolina Funk" is a straightforward shout-out to Rashad's hometown of Raleigh, North Carolina.  "Barn burner" is a trickier term to unpack. You're most likely to hear it today to describe a thrilling outcome, especially in sports. The term began in nineteenth-century politics to describe radical and destructive solutions, inspired by a story about a farmer willing to burn down his barn to get rid of the rats inside.

"Tobacco Road" comes from a 1932 Erskine Caldwell novel about rural poverty. The phrase later became associated with North Carolina’s tobacco country and its cluster of college basketball rivals, where Duke, UNC, NC State, and Wake Forest compete for local supremacy on the courts.

Today's song, "Honey," reminds us that humor was a staple element of many classic funk acts, from Parliament-Funkadelic to Rick James. Prince and the Time carried that comic thread into the 1980s and 1990s, and Rashad keeps it alive here.

OPEN: history of "tobacco road", where did the name come from, what is the road, originally, today

**RESOLVED:** The phrase comes from Erskine Caldwell's 1932 novel *Tobacco Road* (adapted into a long-running 1933 Broadway play and a 1941 John Ford film). The "tobacco roads" were literal: dirt paths in the Savannah River valley worn into the land by rolling heavy wooden hogsheads (casks) of tobacco from inland plantations down to river landings and steamboats over many decades. Caldwell's novel was set among desperately poor rural sharecroppers in Georgia, so "Tobacco Road" entered American English as shorthand for Southern rural poverty and hardscrabble backcountry life. Today the term has migrated to North Carolina (the country's tobacco-production heartland) and lives most visibly in college sports, where "Tobacco Road" names the cluster of ACC basketball rivals (Duke, UNC, NC State, Wake Forest) packed into a small radius of the old tobacco belt. For a Raleigh, NC artist titling a "Carolina Funk" album *Barn Burner on Tobacco Road*, the phrase does double duty: literal Carolina tobacco country plus the down-home, hard-luck Southern connotation. — Sources: https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Tobacco_Road ; https://nsjonline.com/article/2020/04/whats-in-a-name-the-origins-of-tobacco-road-colleges-nicknames/ ; https://www.ourstate.com/tobacco-soldiers/

OPEN: history of "barn burner", what is that term from, what did it originally signify, now?

**RESOLVED:** The term traces to an old folktale (recorded in John Russell Bartlett's 1848 *Dictionary of Americanisms*) about a Dutch farmer who burned down his own barn to get rid of a rat infestation, i.e. an absurdly drastic fix that destroys the thing you are trying to save. That "willing to burn it all down" image became a political label: by 1844 "Barnburners" were the radical, reform-minded faction of the New York State Democratic Party, opposed to the establishment "Hunkers" and fierce enough to blow up the party over issues like the extension of slavery. The now-dominant modern sense (an intensely exciting event or contest, especially in sports) is later, first cited around 1934. So "Barn Burner" on a funk record signals a scorching, high-energy performance, with a faint historical wink at recklessness and radical heat. — Sources: https://www.etymonline.com/word/Barn-burner ; https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/barn-burner ; https://grammarist.com/idiom/barn-burner/

### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"The nu-funk supergroup."** "Honey" isn't a lone Raleigh artist chasing a retro sound; it's a summit of the modern funk-revival scene. Producer/guitarist Sergio Rios (Orgone), guitarist JJ Whitefield (Poets of Rhythm, the German band that basically invented deep-funk revival), keyboardist Dan Hastie (Orgone), co-writer Blake Rhein (Durand Jones & The Indications), horn arranger Mike Buckley (Daptone/Budos Band), and backing vocalist Kendra Morris (Colemine) all show up. The angle: a 2024 track that is a who's-who of the people keeping classic funk alive. Source: https://boulevardscowboy.bandcamp.com/album/carolina-funk-barn-burner-on-tobacco-road
- **"Live to tape, on purpose."** For this album Rashad ditched his usual production-forward approach and cut the band live, straight to tape, for the first time, chasing "the truest me" and the feel of his live show. A good hook for why a brand-new record sounds like a 1972 45. Sources: https://www.boulevards.band/bio ; https://glidemagazine.com/302485/boulevards-keeps-old-school-funk-alive-with-steamin-carolina-funk-barn-burner-on-tobacco-road-album-review/
- **"From punk pit to funk pocket."** Rashad came up in Raleigh's punk and metal scene before returning to funk, and his father was an R&B radio DJ raised on Philly soul. "Honey," a slow-burn "making-love-funk" number, is the sound of that full circle. Sources: https://waltermagazine.com/art-and-culture/boulevards/ ; https://open.spotify.com (bio) ; https://www.spin.com/2022/03/boulevards-jamil-rashad-interview/

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- This was cut live to tape by a band of funk-revival veterans, not built in a computer. Listen for the little human imperfections, the room, the loose horn entrances, that a modern "in the box" production would have scrubbed out. What tells you there are real people in a room?
- "Honey" is a slow-jam seduction track. Track Mike Buckley's horn and flute arrangement: when do the horns punch and when do they sigh? Decide whether they're playing the pillow talk or teasing it.
