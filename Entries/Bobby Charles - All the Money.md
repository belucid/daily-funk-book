### Open Research Questions

No `open:` / `Open:` / `OPEN:` items were present in the entry body. Two questions surfaced during research that the author may want to weigh in on:

1. **Title discrepancy — "All the Money" vs. "He's Got All the Whiskey".** The track titled `All the Money` on current streaming reissues (Spotify "Bobby Charles [w/ Bonus Tracks]", Apple Music "Bobby Charles (Bonus Track Version)") is the same recording released in 1972 as `He's Got All the Whiskey` on the original Bearsville LP (catalog BR 2104). Same runtime (5:17), same songwriter (Bobby Charles / Robert Guidry), same recording. The chorus pivots between "he's got all the whiskey" and "he's got all the money" — Rhino/Warner appears to have retitled the digital reissue using the latter phrase. I could not find a clean public statement explaining why the rename happened (best guess: removing the alcohol reference for streaming-platform friendliness or marketing distinctiveness). Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Charles_(album), https://open.spotify.com/track/44ENmWvaX1fwj2dpspTjeC, http://therockasteria.blogspot.com/2018/05/bobby-charles-bobby-charles-1972-74-us.html, https://theband.hiof.no/albums/bobby_charles_palermo.html (which already lists it as "He's Got All the Money" — suggesting the title slippage is older than the streaming era).
2. **Exact month/day of the album's 1972 release.** Multiple sources confirm 1972 and August 1972 has been cited by at least one secondary source, but I could not find a primary citation pinning the exact street date. The "Small Town Talk" single is documented as a 1972 Bearsville release as well (b/w "Tennessee Blues").

### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"The retitled song."** Lean into the fact that the song the author chose under one name (`All the Money`) is the same recording the world has known for fifty-plus years as `He's Got All the Whiskey`. That little act of cultural sanitation by the rightsholders becomes a way into the song's actual subject — concentrated wealth and the bitterness of the have-nots — which the new title accidentally clarifies.
- **"The lost Band album."** Bobby Charles is routinely called "the lost Band album" because four-fifths of The Band, plus Dr. John, David Sanborn, Ben Keith, and Amos Garrett, played on a record that flopped commercially and got buried by an Albert Grossman fallout. Frame the entry around the gap between who's on the record and how few people heard it.
- **"The fugitive from Abbeville."** Charles wrote "See You Later, Alligator" as a Louisiana teenager, then spent the late 1960s and early 1970s on the run — a 1971 marijuana bust in Nashville sent him to a town he picked off a map called Woodstock, where Albert Grossman happened to be living. The whole record is a fugitive's record, made by a man who couldn't go home.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- "Listen for the hand-claps and Charles's wordless moans in the first thirty seconds, before the horns come in. What does it tell you that a song called 'All the Money' opens by stripping itself down to a body in a room?"
- "Track David Sanborn, Garth Hudson, and John Simon on horns. They don't blow a riff — they trade short, breathy stabs around the vocal. Try to hear it as a fourth voice answering the chorus, not as a backing arrangement."
- "Count how many times the word 'all' lands on the downbeat. Charles isn't singing about whiskey or money in particular — he's singing about totality. Where does the word 'all' stop sounding like a quantity and start sounding like an accusation?"

### Interest and Surprise

### Track

- The track currently titled `All the Money` on Spotify, Apple Music, and other streaming services is the same recording released on the original 1972 LP as `He's Got All the Whiskey` (5:17, same songwriter, same master). Both titles refer to one song whose chorus pivots between the two phrases. — Source: https://open.spotify.com/track/44ENmWvaX1fwj2dpspTjeC and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Charles_(album)
- The arrangement opens with acoustic guitars and hand-claps and Charles's "wordless moans" before the horn section (Garth Hudson, David Sanborn, John Simon) enters with a "stunning and breathy series of blasts." — Source: https://somethingelsereviews.com/2022/05/29/bobby-charles-self-titled-1972-album/ and https://talkfromtherockroom.com/2016/10/bobby-charles-small-town-talk-1972-self.html
- The song was covered by British folk-blues singer John Martyn on his 1998 album *The Church with One Bell*, where it appears under its original title "He's Got All the Whiskey." — Source: https://www.johnmartyn.info/song/hes-got-all-whiskey

### Album

- The 1972 self-titled album is widely known as "the lost Band album": four of The Band's five members (Rick Danko, Levon Helm, Garth Hudson, Richard Manuel) play on it, with Danko and John Simon co-producing alongside Charles. Robbie Robertson is the lone absentee. — Source: https://somethingelsereviews.com/2022/05/29/bobby-charles-self-titled-1972-album/
- The supporting cast also includes Dr. John, David Sanborn, Ben Keith (Neil Young's pedal steel player), Amos Garrett, Bob Neuwirth, and Geoff Muldaur — a Woodstock all-star roster Albert Grossman assembled at Bearsville Studios. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Charles_(album)
- Despite favorable reviews and the personnel, the album sold poorly. A falling-out with Grossman scuttled distribution and a planned second Bearsville record went unreleased; it eventually surfaced in 2011 as part of a Rhino Handmade / Light in the Attic three-disc box set with twenty-five bonus tracks. — Source: https://variety.com/2010/music/news/singer-bobby-charles-dies-at-71-1118013835/ and https://theseconddisc.com/2011/07/07/bobby-charles-homemade-songs-become-handmade-project/
- Catalog number on the original US Bearsville LP is **BR 2104**; the album was released in 1972. — Source: https://www.45cat.com/vinyl/album/br2104

### Artist

- Bobby Charles was born Robert Charles Guidry on February 21, 1938 in Abbeville, Louisiana (Vermilion Parish). He grew up on Cajun music until he saw Fats Domino play live at fifteen, which he said "changed my life forever." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Charles and https://64parishes.org/entry/bobby-charles
- At seventeen, he wrote "See You Later, Alligator" and got a telephone audition with Leonard Chess. When Chess Records signed him sight-unseen they assumed from his voice that he was Black; Bill Haley & His Comets' cover hit No. 6 on Billboard in 1956. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/See_You_Later,_Alligator and https://64parishes.org/entry/bobby-charles
- He also wrote "Walking to New Orleans" and "It Keeps Rainin'" for Fats Domino and "(I Don't Know Why) But I Do" for Clarence "Frogman" Henry. Charles couldn't read music or play an instrument fluently — he hummed parts and told other musicians "I'll tell you when you hit the right one." — Source: https://64parishes.org/entry/bobby-charles
- In 1971 Charles was arrested on a marijuana charge in Nashville. Rather than face the case he jumped bail, opened a map of New York, picked the town called Woodstock essentially at random, and ended up in a shared house there. That's how he met Albert Grossman and The Band, which led directly to the 1972 album. — Source: https://www.npr.org/2012/06/13/150960729/the-untold-story-of-singer-bobby-charles (search excerpt)
- He performed "Down South in New Orleans" with Dr. John and The Band at The Last Waltz on November 26, 1976; his footage was filmed by Scorsese but cut from the final 1978 theatrical release. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Charles and https://64parishes.org/entry/bobby-charles
- Charles died at home in Abbeville, Louisiana on January 14, 2010, age 71, from complications related to diabetes (he was also in remission from kidney cancer). — Source: https://variety.com/2010/music/news/singer-bobby-charles-dies-at-71-1118013835/ and https://www.today.com/popculture/walking-new-orleans-songwriter-dies-71-1C9398304

### Genre Suggestions

The entry currently has `Country Funk` tagged. Reviewing against `Matter/On Genres.md`:

- **Country Funk (currently selected).** Defensible by the broadest reading: the album is a Louisiana songwriter's roots-soaked record made by Band-affiliated musicians during exactly the 1972 window when country-funk was being made (the *Country Funk* compilations later canonized this kind of crossover). The horn-arranged groove of "All the Money / He's Got All the Whiskey" is more swamp/New Orleans R&B than country, but Charles's writing voice — Cajun, twangy, plainspoken — pulls it into country-funk territory. Rationale source: Matter/On Genres.md and https://somethingelsereviews.com/2022/05/29/bobby-charles-self-titled-1972-album/
- **Classic Funk.** The track was released in 1972 by a major-distribution label (Bearsville/Warner) with a horn-driven groove, hand-clap pocket, and a funk-forward feel. It sits squarely inside the 1969–1977 classic-funk window. The argument against: classic funk usually has a more aggressive bass and drum pocket, while this track is loose and swampy. Source: Matter/On Genres.md
- **Deep Funk.** A real possibility. The album sold poorly on release, was rediscovered by crate-diggers decades later, and was finally reissued in 2011 as a deluxe Light in the Attic box set — the exact "delayed recognition" pattern the book's definition of deep funk requires. Source: Matter/On Genres.md and https://theseconddisc.com/2011/07/07/bobby-charles-homemade-songs-become-handmade-project/

If forced to a single tag, **Country Funk** is reasonable; **Deep Funk** is arguably the most accurate based on the book's definition. The author may want to consider whether the commercial-fate criterion or the sonic criterion takes precedence here.

### Region Suggestions

Reviewing against `Matter/On Regions.md` and `Planning/Field Values.md`:

- **US - Louisiana (set).** Bobby Charles was born, raised, and died in Abbeville, Louisiana. Even though the record was cut in Woodstock with non-Louisiana sidemen, the artist's voice, songwriting, and sensibility are unambiguously Louisiana. The book's region rule is "represent the track's origins and be defensible" — and Louisiana is the most defensible origin for any Bobby Charles entry. Source: https://64parishes.org/entry/bobby-charles
- **US - Northeast.** A plausible alternate. The record was made at Bearsville Studios in Woodstock, NY, and most of the musicians on it (The Band members, Sanborn, Garrett, Simon) were operating out of upstate New York at the time. If the book is privileging "where the recording happened," Northeast wins. Source: https://somethingelsereviews.com/2022/05/29/bobby-charles-self-titled-1972-album/
- **US - New York.** Same case as Northeast, but pinpointed. New York is listed as a permitted region. Less defensible than Louisiana given Charles's biography.

Recommendation: keep **US - Louisiana**. The artist's identity is the strongest signal.
