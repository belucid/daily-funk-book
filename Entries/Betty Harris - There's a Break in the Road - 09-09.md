Betty Harris was born September 9, 1939, in Orlando, Florida. In her last recorded song, "There’s a Break in the Road," it feels like two parallel songs are fighting for your ear. Producer Allen Toussaint and Harris are aiming for a late-1960s soul single, but the session band, freshly formed as The Meters, seem to have other plans.

The Meters laid down a nasty, funk-forward rhythm, though without Zigaboo Modeliste on drums. The groove demands attention and competes for the soul of the track. Calling it "backing" almost misses the point, especially since they cut the track without Harris in the room.

In 1969 and the years that followed, The Meters would create some of the greatest classic funk instrumentals ever recorded. To me this feels like a Meters instrumental trying to break free.

1. **"The ghost drummer"**: James Black, not Zigaboo Modeliste, played drums on this session, a mystery that confused funk collectors for years. 2. **"The singer who wasn't in the room"**: Harris flew into New Orleans, recorded her vocals on tracks Toussaint had already laid down, and left before mixing. The entry already hints at this separation. The theme could dig into what it means to record a vocal performance on top of a band you never played with, and how the tension between the two performances is what makes the track electric.

3. **"Last call at Sansu"**: This was Betty Harris's final recording with Allen Toussaint and her last release before she walked away from music in 1970, dispirited by lack of commercial success and unpaid royalties. The theme could frame the track as a farewell that nobody heard at the time.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- The track was described by the "Songs from So Deep" blog as "as raw a soul record as has ever been made," with distorted guitar feedback, audibly distorting snare drum and horns, and "post-Hendrix trills." The band sounds like it's "holding the whole thing together only by force of will." — Source: https://songsfromsodeep.wordpress.com/2014/03/13/break-in-the-road-betty-harris/
- There has been a longstanding mystery among collectors over whether Zigaboo Modeliste or James Black played drums. The Home of the Groove blog confirmed it was James Black, not Modeliste, alongside Meters members George Porter Jr. (bass) and Leo Nocentelli (guitar). — Source: https://homeofthegroove.blogspot.com/2006/10/who-you-gonna-call.html
- The B-side of the original SSS International 45 (SSS 766) was "All I Want Is You." A promo pressing exists with "There's a Break in the Road" on both sides. — Source: https://www.45cat.com/record/sss766
- Susan Tedeschi covered the track on her 2008 album *Back to the River*. In December 2015, Betty Harris herself joined the Tedeschi Trucks Band on stage at College Street Music Hall in New Haven, CT to perform the song live, dedicated to the recently deceased Allen Toussaint. — Source: https://www.setlist.fm/setlist/tedeschi-trucks-band/2015/college-street-music-hall-new-haven-ct-3f2811f.html
- Soul Jazz Records reissued the track as a limited 7" single (SJR343-7, limited to 500 copies) paired with "Show It." — Source: https://soundsoftheuniverse.com/sjr/product/theres-a-break-in-the-road

#### Album
- This track was never released on an album during its original release. It was a single only. It later appeared on the 2016 Soul Jazz Records compilation *The Lost Queen of New Orleans Soul* (SJRLP345), which gathered Harris's Sansu singles from 1964-1969. — Source: https://soundsoftheuniverse.com/sjr/product/the-lost-soul-queen-of-new-orleans
- The 1969 UK-only vinyl compilation *Soul Perfection* (Minit label) now commands $200-$300 on the collector market. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betty_Harris

#### Artist
- Betty Harris was born in Orlando, FL but raised in Alabama. Her father, Reverend Rufus Crews, was a booking agent for gospel artists including Sister Rosetta Tharpe and Sam Cooke. — Source: https://www.betty-harris.com/about
- Harris's first recording (1958) was as lead singer of the girl group The Hearts, produced by Zelma Sanders. She was mentored by Big Maybelle (Mabel Louise Smith). — Source: https://www.betty-harris.com/about
- In 1963, Harris walked into songwriter Bert Berns's office at 1650 Broadway and told him he could have done better with his production of Solomon Burke's "Cry to Me." She slowed the tempo and turned up the soul. Berns stopped her mid-audition to book studio time. The resulting record reached #23 on the Billboard Hot 100 and #10 R&B. — Source: http://bertberns.com/album/betty-harris/
- Harris made over 20 recordings with Toussaint between 1965 and 1969, but only "Nearer to You" (1967) achieved national chart success (#85 Billboard). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betty_Harris
- Toussaint and his business partner Marshall Sehorn never paid Harris the royalties due to her, but she owns the sound recording copyrights for all her Sansu recordings. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betty_Harris
- Harris retired from music in 1970 to raise a family and did not return until 2005, a 35-year hiatus. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betty_Harris
- Harris currently resides in Middletown, Connecticut. — Source: https://www.betty-harris.com/about
- James Black, the drummer on this session, was a jazz polymath who played trumpet and piano as well as drums. He worked with Nat Adderley, Cannonball Adderley, Horace Silver, Lionel Hampton, Yusef Lateef, and Freddie Hubbard. He received two NEA grants as a composer. He died of a drug overdose on August 30, 1988, and was buried in an unmarked grave in New Orleans. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_N._Black
- Allen Toussaint reportedly said about James Black that he'd brought in several other drummers to try to play a track and no one could do it; James sat down, looked at the music, and played it through on the first shot. — Source: https://musicrising.tulane.edu/discover/people/james-black/

### Genre Suggestions

Current genre value: `Deep Funk`

1. **Deep Funk** (keep current): This is the strongest fit. The track was a commercially unsuccessful regional single on a small distribution deal, overlooked on release and rediscovered decades later by crate-diggers and DJs. It fits the book's definition of deep funk perfectly: "tracks that were overlooked on release and only found their audience later through crate-digging, DJs, and dance floors."
   - Source: Entry's own genre definition; collector market evidence from Discogs and Soul Jazz Records reissue

2. **Classic Funk**: The track falls within the classic funk era (1968-1977) and features the core vocabulary of funk: syncopated rhythm section, horn arrangements, and a groove-first approach. The musicians (the proto-Meters) went on to define classic funk. However, Harris's commercial invisibility pushes this toward deep funk rather than classic funk.
   - Source: Genre definition in On Genres & Regions.md

3. **Psychedelic Funk**: Multiple sources describe the track as having a "raw / psychedelic feel embellished with creative mic feedback and edgy Hammond organ arrangements" and "post-Hendrix trills." The guitar feedback and distortion throughout give the track a psychedelic character that is distinct from typical deep funk or classic funk.
   - Source: https://www.soulstrut.com/Archive/betty-harris-theres-a-break-in-the-road; https://songsfromsodeep.wordpress.com/2014/03/13/break-in-the-road-betty-harris/

**Recommendation**: Deep Funk is correct and should remain the primary genre. Psychedelic Funk could be considered as a secondary tag if multi-genre tagging is permitted.
