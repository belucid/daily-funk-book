“Amen, Brother” is a funky instrumental built around the gospel song “Amen,” popularized by Jester Hairston’s arrangement for the 1963 Sidney Poitier film *Lilies of the Field*. The Winstons' song is also the most sampled recording in history, with over 7,500 verified samples, thanks to a seven-second drum break that became known as the Amen break.

The Winstons were a multiracial sextet from Washington, D.C. that formed from the core of Otis Redding's and Curtis Mayfield's touring bands. Their A-side, “Color Him Father,” was a hit, reaching #7 on the Hot 100, winning the Grammy for Best R&B Song, and going gold. The unheralded B-side, “Amen, Brother,” was reportedly knocked out in 20 minutes, yet contained seven seconds that rewired hip-hop.

In the 1980s, breakbeat records circulated to feed DJs and early hip-hop producers. The track’s second life began when Breakbeat Lou Flores and Lenny Roberts included “Amen, Brother” on Volume 1 of _Ultimate Breaks and Beats_ in 1986.

Bandleader and songwriter Richard Spencer only learned in 1996 that “Amen, Brother” had been sampled thousands of times. By then, it was too late to sue for copyright infringement, so he received no royalties. The Winstons' drummer, Gregory Coleman, died homeless and penniless in Atlanta on February 5, 2006, likely unaware of the revolution his seven seconds sparked.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "Amen, Brother" is a funked-up instrumental cover of the gospel standard "Amen," written by Jester Hairston for the 1963 Sidney Poitier film *Lilies of the Field*. Source: https://alijamieson.co.uk/2021/12/31/can-i-get-amen-winstons-classic-break-examined/
- The track was reportedly composed in about 20 minutes as B-side filler, built on top of a guitar riff Curtis Mayfield had played for Richard Spencer. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amen_break
- The famous drum break lands at approximately 1:26 into the track, runs four bars (about 6–7 seconds), and features a delayed snare in bar three and an open crash cymbal in bar four. Gregory Coleman performed it live, not overdubbed. Source: https://alijamieson.co.uk/2021/12/31/can-i-get-amen-winstons-classic-break-examined/ and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amen_break
- Tempo sits around 130–136 BPM in the original recording, a rare sweet spot that works sped-up as jungle (~170 BPM) and slowed-down as classic hip-hop tempos. Source: https://songbpm.com/@the-winstons/amen-brother and https://alijamieson.co.uk/2021/12/31/can-i-get-amen-winstons-classic-break-examined/
- Recorded to an Ampex 440 half-inch 4-track at 15 ips, drums captured with a Neumann U87 on overheads, Altec 633 on kick, and RCA BK-5 ribbon on snare. The loose tom tuning gives the break its sympathetic ring, a key part of why samplers loved it. Source: https://alijamieson.co.uk/2021/12/31/can-i-get-amen-winstons-classic-break-examined/
- Spencer later described Coleman's solo as "a composite of a couple that Greg played," meaning the most-sampled drum break in history was stitched together in the room. Source: https://www.20k.org/episodes/amenbreak

#### Album

- *Color Him Father* (Metromedia MD 1010, 1969) was recorded at LeFevre Sound Studios in Atlanta, produced by Don Carroll with Rodney Mills engineering. The album conductor/arranger was Emory Gordy Jr., the future Nashville country producer. Source: https://archive.org/details/the_winstons-color_him_father-vinyl-1969
- The album is mostly covers of late-'60s hits, Nina Simone's "I've Gotta Be Me," Sly's "Everyday People," Toussaint's "The Greatest Love," Gamble-Huff's "Only the Strong Survive," plus Spencer's original title track and the instrumental "Amen, Brother." It's the only full-length album The Winstons released. Source: https://archive.org/details/the_winstons-color_him_father-vinyl-1969
- The A-side "Color Him Father" was the commercial success: gold record (RIAA certified July 24, 1969), No. 7 on the Billboard Hot 100, No. 2 on the R&B chart, and it won Spencer the 1970 Grammy for Best R&B Song. The B-side was ignored. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Winstons and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_Him_Father

#### Artist

- The Winstons were a multiracial sextet based in Washington, D.C., rare for the period and a reason the band struggled to book Southern venues and eventually dissolved in 1970. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Winstons
- The core of the band came out of Otis Redding's road band and Curtis Mayfield's Impressions circuit, Spencer, Coleman, and guitarist Quincy Mattison played behind Redding, then all six members toured as the Impressions' backing band before stepping out on their own. Source: http://www.soulwalking.co.uk/Winstons.html
- Bandleader Richard Lewis Spencer (tenor sax, lead vocals) was born May 3, 1942, in Wadesboro, North Carolina. He began as a church organist at 13, later studied classical piano. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Lewis_Spencer
- After The Winstons broke up in 1970, Spencer left music entirely. He earned a BA in political science, an MS in labor management relations, coursework toward a PhD at Howard, and worked for the Washington Metro until 2000. He then became a Baptist minister and Montgomery County, MD, high school teacher. He published a novel, *The Molasses Tree*, in 2003. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Lewis_Spencer
- Drummer Gregory Coleman died homeless and broke in Atlanta in 2006, likely never having heard the breakbeat-jungle revolution his seven seconds kicked off. Source: https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-news/amen-break-creator-getting-long-delayed-compensation-for-iconic-sample-118818/ and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_C._Coleman
- Spencer only learned "Amen, Brother" had been sampled thousands of times in 1996, past the three-year statute of limitations for copyright infringement. He received no royalties. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amen_break
- A 2015 GoFundMe launched by UK DJ Martyn Webster raised more than £24,000 (initial reports $4,500; later totals exceeded $30,000) for Spencer as a belated gesture of acknowledgement. Source: https://www.engadget.com/2015-02-20-amen-break-sample-crowdfunding-campaign.html and https://www.20k.org/episodes/amenbreak
- The track's second life began when Breakbeat Lou Flores and Lenny Roberts included "Amen, Brother" on volume 1 of *Ultimate Breaks and Beats* (Street Beat Records, 1986). They edited the break by pitching it from 45 rpm down to 33 rpm, recording it, then playing it back at 45, a small trick that made the break easier to chop and loop. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultimate_Breaks_and_Beats and https://www.passionweiss.com/2014/11/21/amen-brother-breakbeat-lou-and-the-legacy-of-ultimate-breaks-and-beats/
- Salt-N-Pepa's "I Desire" (1986) is among the first known sample uses; N.W.A.'s "Straight Outta Compton" (1988), Mantronix's "King of the Beats" (1988), and Rob Base & DJ E-Z Rock's "Keep It Going Now" all followed. Oasis sampled it on "D'You Know What I Mean?" (1997); Christopher Tyng baked it into the *Futurama* theme (1999). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amen_break and https://www.whosampled.com/sample/5049/Oasis-D'you-Know-What-I-Mean%3F-The-Winstons-Amen,-Brother/
- Nate Harrison's 2004 audio-essay art piece *Can I Get an Amen?* is the canonical long-form meditation on the break's journey from B-side to cultural infrastructure, and is ironically itself an object lesson in uncontrolled distribution. Source: https://archive.org/details/NateHarrisonCanIGetAnAmen

### Genre Suggestions

Current `genre` field is empty. Two candidates:

1. **Deep Funk (strongest case).** The book defines deep funk by "commercial fate," overlooked on release, discovered later through crate-digging and DJs. That's exactly the arc of "Amen, Brother": ignored in 1969 as a B-side, then resurrected in 1986 on Breakbeat Lou's *Ultimate Breaks and Beats* Vol. 1 and elevated by hip-hop DJs and producers. It is the platonic ideal of delayed recognition. Source: On Genres & Regions.md, plus https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultimate_Breaks_and_Beats
2. **Classic Funk.** The track was released on a major distributor (Metromedia, RCA-distributed), with a Grammy-winning A-side and wide chart presence. It sits inside the 1968–1977 classic-funk window, and its instrumental funk vocabulary (horn-led melody over a tight drum-bass pocket) fits the classic-funk definition. The case here is weaker because the specific track "Amen, Brother" was not the hit, but if the author frames the book's classic-funk definition at the release level rather than the reception level, it qualifies.
3. **Gospel Funk (weaker, but interesting curveball).** The piece is literally a funked-up instrumental of a gospel standard ("Amen," by Jester Hairston). The book defines gospel funk as "gospel music that absorbed the funk vocabulary, or funk musicians turning their groove toward praise." This fits that second clause cleanly. Source: https://alijamieson.co.uk/2021/12/31/can-i-get-amen-winstons-classic-break-examined/

**Recommended:** Deep Funk. The narrative of this entry is inseparable from the track's delayed recognition, and Deep Funk is defined by exactly that arc.
