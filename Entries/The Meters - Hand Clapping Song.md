### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"The four-second loop that built a genre"** — A meditation on how a single, modest-charting New Orleans single (Billboard Hot 100 #89) became one of the most sampled drum-and-vocal sources in hip-hop after Street Beat Records included it on Ultimate Breaks & Beats Vol. 8 (1986). The angle: what gets remembered isn't always what topped the charts. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Meters, https://www.stereogum.com/2052486/handclapping-song-the-meters-art-neville/columns/breaks-with-tradition/
- **"A little softer, a little louder"** — Frame the entry around the call-and-response dynamic structure of the vocal performance and how it instructs the listener to participate. It's the first Meters album with full vocals — most are instrumental — and this track wears its claps and call-outs as the song's whole architecture. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Struttin%27
- **"The house band makes a record"** — Use the song as a window into the Meters' double life: by day, the rhythm section for every Allen Toussaint / Marshall Sehorn session at Cosimo Matassa's Jazz City studio in New Orleans; by night, their own records on Josie. Sehorn and Toussaint produced this very track. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Meters

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- Listen for the moment Art Neville's voice drops to a whisper on "a little softer" and then explodes on "a little louder." How does Zigaboo Modeliste's drum part change underneath those volume directions, and how does that micro-arrangement turn a riff into a song?
- The handclaps are doing two jobs at once: they're a percussion layer and an instruction to the audience. Pay attention to where the claps lock with Modeliste's snare and where they fall on their own — when do they stop being decoration and start being the beat?
- Listen for what's missing. There's almost no chord progression — Leo Nocentelli's guitar and George Porter Jr.'s bass orbit the same one-chord groove for 2:56. What keeps it from getting boring? Try to spot the small variations each player slips in.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- "Hand Clapping Song" peaked at #89 on the Billboard Hot 100 and #26 on the Hot R&B/Hip-Hop Songs chart in 1970 — a modest commercial showing for what would become one of the most-sampled funk records ever. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Meters
- The single was released as Josie 45-1021 with "Joog" on the B-side (some pressings paired it with "Chicken Strut"). — Sources: https://www.45cat.com/record/451021, https://www.discogs.com/release/1445334-Meters-Hand-Clapping-Song-Chicken-Strut
- The track has been sampled "over 500 times" according to Stereogum, including early hip-hop landmarks like Salt-N-Pepa's "Beauty And The Beast" (1986), Audio Two's "I Get The Papers" (1990), Redman's "Da Funk" (1992), Tony! Toni! Toné!'s "Gangsta Groove" (1993), and A Tribe Called Quest's "Clap Your Hands." — Source: https://www.stereogum.com/2052486/handclapping-song-the-meters-art-neville/columns/breaks-with-tradition/
- It was included on Ultimate Breaks & Beats Vol. 8 (Street Beat Records, 1986), the producer-grade compilation series that put crate-digger funk breaks directly into hip-hop studios from 1986–1991. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Ultimate-Breaks-&-Beats/Hand-Clapping-Song/
- The track was written by all four Meters — George Porter Jr., Leo Nocentelli, Joseph "Zigaboo" Modeliste, and Art Neville — and produced by Marshall Sehorn and Allen Toussaint. — Source: https://themusicalhype.com/the-meters-hand-clapping-song-throwback-vibez/
- Tempo: ~89 BPM, in the key of A. — Source: https://songbpm.com/@the-meters/hand-clapping-song

#### Album
- *Struttin'* (Josie JOS-4012) was released June 1970, the Meters' third studio album and their first to prominently feature vocals — Art Neville sings lead on "Wichita Lineman," "Darling, Darling, Darling," and "Ride Your Pony." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Struttin%27
- The album was recorded at Cosimo Matassa's studio in New Orleans, the room where most of the city's R&B canon (Little Richard, Fats Domino, Lee Dorsey, etc.) had already been cut. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Struttin%27
- It peaked at #200 on the Billboard 200 and #32 on the Billboard R&B Albums chart; the lead single "Chicken Strut" reached #11 R&B. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Meters, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Struttin%27
- Cash Box (June 20, 1970) placed *Struttin'* in its "Pop Best Bets," and Robert Christgau gave the album a B+, calling the Meters' feel "pure second-line, as elliptical as a saint's march." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Struttin%27
- AllMusic's Stephen Erlewine noted "Joog" and "Hand Clapping Song" as the stylistic outliers on a record otherwise rooted in tight instrumental funk. — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/struttin-mw0000650493

#### Artist
- The Meters formed in New Orleans in 1965 — Art Neville (keys), Leo Nocentelli (guitar), George Porter Jr. (bass), and Zigaboo Modeliste (drums) — and became the in-house rhythm section for Allen Toussaint and Marshall Sehorn's Sansu Enterprises, playing on records for Lee Dorsey, Dr. John, Labelle, and Paul McCartney's *Venus and Mars*. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Meters
- They opened for the Rolling Stones on the Stones' 1975 Tour of the Americas, which dramatically widened their audience. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Meters
- The original quartet (with Cyril Neville added later) cut its run short in 1977; the band reunited at various points and received a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Meters, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Nocentelli
- Art Neville died in New Orleans on July 22, 2019, at age 81. — Source: https://www.npr.org/2019/07/22/744150388/art-neville-a-new-orleans-icon-dead-at-81

### Genre Suggestions

The book's genre list (Field Values) does not include `genre` for this entry yet. Considering the book's definitions in `Matter/On Genres.md`:

1. **Classic Funk** (strongest case) — *Struttin'* (1970) sits squarely in the 1969–1977 "classic era" the book defines. The Meters were on a well-distributed indie label (Josie), got radio play and a Billboard Hot 100 chart entry, and the track's primary identity is funk, not funk fused with another genre. This is exactly what the book calls Classic Funk. — Source: `Matter/On Genres.md`
2. **Deep Funk** (secondary case) — The song under-performed commercially (peaked #89 pop) and only achieved its outsized cultural status through later crate-digging and hip-hop sampling — the book's exact definition of "delayed recognition." A reasonable secondary framing if the author wants to lean into the sample story. — Sources: `Matter/On Genres.md`, https://www.stereogum.com/2052486/handclapping-song-the-meters-art-neville/columns/breaks-with-tradition/
