Yesterday you met the first of our two funky Lonnie Smiths. Today’s song, “A Chance for Peace,” is by the other one: Lonnie Liston Smith Jr., born in Richmond, Virginia. He played tuba, trumpet, and piano in high school before settling on the piano. After stints with Art Blakey, Pharoah Sanders, and Miles Davis, he launched his own career as a bandleader in 1973.

His funkiest work, and the records I would point you at, are the mid-1970s releases attributed to his band, Lonnie Liston Smith & The Cosmic Echoes. *Visions Of A New World* is one of the best examples of his spiritually infused brand of jazz-funk, with vocals by Lonnie Liston Smith and yet another Smith, the band's vocalist, Donald Smith. This Smith is actually related: Donald is Lonnie Liston Smith's younger brother.

Yesterday’s decoder ring was the turban. Today’s is the middle name. If there’s no “Dr.” honorific or Sikh turban, but you do see “Liston” in the credits, you’ve found the jazz pianist. His catalog is less consistently funky than Dr. Lonnie Smith’s, so look for his mid-1970s recordings with the Cosmic Echoes.

- **Which version is "the" track — album or single edit?** "A Chance For Peace" opens the 1975 album *Visions Of A New World* at 5:18, and was also issued as a 7" single (Flying Dutchman DB-10392) edited down to 3:24 b/w "Sunset." I set `track length` to the album version (5m 18s), which is the definitive/full recording; the single edit (3m 24s) is noted for the author. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visions_of_a_New_World and https://www.discogs.com/release/3023718-Lonnie-Liston-Smith-The-Cosmic-Echoes-A-Chance-For-Peace

### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"Peace as a groove."** In 1975, coming out of Vietnam-era America, Smith frames spiritual optimism ("a chance for peace," "visions of a new world") as danceable Fender Rhodes funk rather than protest. The angle: how spiritual jazz-funk carried social hope in sound rather than slogan.
- **"The sideman steps out."** Smith spent the late 1960s as a pianist behind Pharoah Sanders and Miles Davis before Bob Thiele coaxed him into his own name on Flying Dutchman. This track is the sound of a supporting player becoming a bandleader with a signature cosmic style.
- **"The family band."** The vocal warmth here is Donald Smith, Lonnie's brother, on voice and flute — an angle on the Cosmic Echoes as a genuine collective and a family affair.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- The song opens with a hard funk edge before the Fender Rhodes and wordless vocals melt it into something floating. Mark the exact moment the "funk" tips into "cosmic" — what changes first, the drums or the keys?
- Donald Smith's voice is used almost like a horn, not a lyric line. Follow the voice for one pass and decide: is he singing to you, or playing an instrument?
- Compare the 3:24 single edit to the 5:18 album version. What did the edit sacrifice, and does the shorter cut actually punch harder on a 45?

### Interest and Surprise

### Track

- "A Chance For Peace" was issued as a 7" single on Flying Dutchman (DB-10392), backed with "Sunset," and edited to 3:24 from the album's 5:18 — the sleeve/label explicitly notes it is "from the 'Visions of a New World' album." — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/3023718-Lonnie-Liston-Smith-The-Cosmic-Echoes-A-Chance-For-Peace
- The track has been recycled by later producers and DJs: it was sampled by Fried Tofu on "Let's Make Love" and reworked by Tasun, part of a long tail of crate-digger interest in Smith's Flying Dutchman catalog. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Lonnie-Liston-Smith/A-Chance-for-Peace/ and https://soundcloud.com/tasun/lonnie-linston-smith-a-chance
- The lead vocal is wordless/textural, sung by Donald Smith, who also plays flute — the voice functions as color rather than narrative lyric. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visions_of_a_New_World

### Album

- *Visions Of A New World* was recorded in just two sessions — July 18 and 20, 1975 — at Electric Lady Studios, the New York studio Jimi Hendrix built. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visions_of_a_New_World
- Smith's Flying Dutchman albums became one of the most heavily sampled bodies of work in hip-hop; the most famous is Digable Planets' "Rebirth of Slick (Cool Like Dat)"/"Pacifics" lineage drawn from Smith's catalog, and other *Visions* cuts like "Summer Nights" were sampled by producers including Moodymann (as Kenny Dixon Jr., "January," 1996) and Ras G. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Lonnie-Liston-Smith/A-Chance-for-Peace/ (WhoSampled artist catalog) and search results at https://www.whosampled.com/Lonnie-Liston-Smith/
- The album was produced by Bob Thiele — the producer famous for John Coltrane's *A Love Supreme* — alongside Smith himself, and released on Thiele's Flying Dutchman imprint through RCA. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visions_of_a_New_World

### Artist

- Lonnie Liston Smith grew up in a gospel household in Richmond, Virginia: his father sang with The Harmonizing Four, a well-known gospel quartet. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lonnie_Liston_Smith
- Before leading the Cosmic Echoes, Smith earned a music-education degree from Morgan State University (1961) and played piano/keyboards behind Pharoah Sanders and Miles Davis. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lonnie_Liston_Smith
- The Cosmic Echoes formed in 1973 with a globe-spanning lineup that included tabla (Badal Roy) and tamboura (Geeta Vashi) alongside jazz players — a genuinely "cosmic," cross-cultural ensemble. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lonnie_Liston_Smith
