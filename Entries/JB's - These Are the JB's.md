Resolved during research:
- Which version of the track should anchor the entry? Three co-exist: the November 1970 King 45 ("Pts. 1 & 2," ~4:45), the 3:01 edit on *Food For Thought* (People, 1972), and the 5:50 "Pt. 1 and Pt. 2" on the lost *These Are The J.B.'s* LP (Now-Again, 2014). The *Food For Thought* edit is the most widely available stream and the canonical commercial release, so the metadata is anchored there; the mission and themes below lean on the richer backstory of the shelved album.
- Recording date discrepancy: Now-Again's own liner-sourced page gives **September 9, 1970** at King Studios, Cincinnati. Zero to 180 refers loosely to "September 10, 1970" in one post and "May 19, 1970" in another (the May date actually belongs to "The Grunt"). September 9, 1970 is the date supported by the most authoritative source (Now-Again's release page drawing on Alan Leeds' liner notes). Sources: https://www.nowagainrecords.com/the-j-b-s-these-are-the-j-b-s/ · https://www.zeroto180.org/birth-of-the-jbs-king-records/

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **The lost album, 44 years in the vault.** The whole story hinges on King SLP 1126: test-pressed in 1971, shelved when James Brown's catalog moved to Polydor, surviving as only two known copies until Now-Again issued it in 2014. A track whose release history is as funky as its groove.
2. **Bootsy at the launchpad.** This is the original J.B.'s lineup at full tilt: Bootsy on bass, his brother Catfish on guitar, Clyde Stubblefield on drums, about six months before Bootsy quit Brown's band and drifted into George Clinton's orbit. You're hearing the seed of P-Funk still inside the James Brown machine.
3. **A band staking its claim.** The title is literal, the track is a mission statement: James Brown's new hired guns announcing themselves on wax. The theme could focus on how funk bands named themselves into existence, from "Cold Sweat" to "Mother Popcorn" to this introductory anthem.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. Lock in on Bootsy's bass from bar one. This is the sound that will walk out of James Brown's band within months and help build P-Funk. Where do you hear the future in this 1970 groove?
2. The title track is a band staking its claim on wax. Count how many bars go by before anything but the rhythm section enters, and listen for what instrument finally carries the "announcement."
3. If you can, queue up the 3:01 *Food For Thought* edit back-to-back with the 4:45 Pt. 1 & 2 single (or the 5:50 version on the 2014 reissue). Where does the band stretch, and does the longer take earn its extra minutes?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- Recorded September 9, 1970 at King Studios, Cincinnati, Ohio, produced by James Brown, engineered by Ron Lenhoff. Source: https://www.nowagainrecords.com/the-j-b-s-these-are-the-j-b-s/
- Personnel on the session: William "Bootsy" Collins (bass), Phelps "Catfish" Collins (guitar), Clyde Stubblefield (drums), with Bobby Byrd, horn players Clayton "Chicken" Gunnells, Darryl "Hasaan" Jamison, and Robert McCollough, plus conga player Johnny Griggs among the extended band. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_J.B.%27s
- Issued as the single "These Are The J.B.'s (Pts. 1 & 2)" on King in November 1970. Billboard's Ed Ochs tipped the 45 in his "Soul Sauce" column for the week of October 24, 1970. Source: https://www.zeroto180.org/the-jbs-debut-polydor-not-king/
- The 3:01 edit on *Food For Thought* (1972) is the version most people have streamed for fifty years; the longer "Pt. 1 & 2" edits live on the 1970 single and the 2014 Now-Again LP. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_for_Thought_(The_J.B.%27s_album) · https://open.spotify.com/track/0oKWt2gJTUWJTzG86XyGIZ · https://open.spotify.com/track/2ULadsNUyftKeLQ0jBm6Ob
- Songwriting credit on the 2014 release goes to "The J.B.'s" collectively, uncommon for James Brown productions, where Brown usually claimed the line. Source: https://www.nowagainrecords.com/the-j-b-s-these-are-the-j-b-s/

#### Album
- The track's "true" home was the never-released King LP *These Are The J.B.'s* (catalog SLP 1126), scheduled for July 1971. When James Brown's catalog was sold to Polydor, the album was shelved. Only two known test pressings survive. Source: https://www.zeroto180.org/the-jbs-debut-polydor-not-king/
- The lost album was finally issued commercially on **November 28, 2014** by Now-Again Records (NA 5119), limited to 3,000 copies, mastered from the original 2-inch tape by Dave Cooley at Elysian Masters, with a 12-page book of liner notes by Alan Leeds. Source: https://www.nowagainrecords.com/the-j-b-s-these-are-the-j-b-s/
- When Polydor took over, they assembled a broader 1972 debut, *Food For Thought* (People PE 5601), released June 1972. Only two tracks carried over from the shelved King LP: "The Grunt" and "These Are The J.B.'s (Pt. 1)." Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_for_Thought_(The_J.B.%27s_album)
- Now-Again's Egon called the lost album "a Rosetta Stone of funk's dominant idioms." Source: https://www.nowagainrecords.com/the-lost-james-brown-bootsy-collins-album/

#### Artist
- The J.B.'s in this incarnation were actually **The Pacemakers**, a Cincinnati band led by Bootsy and Catfish Collins. In March 1970, after most of James Brown's veteran musicians walked out over a pay dispute, Bobby Byrd flew down in a Learjet and picked them up inside 45 minutes. Their first gig was backing Brown with no rehearsal. Source: https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2013/11/when-bootsy-collins-met-james-brown
- The "original" J.B.'s tenure with Brown lasted only about 11 months (March 1970 to March 1971), but during that window they cut "Sex Machine," "Super Bad," "Soul Power," "The Grunt," and this track. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bootsy_Collins · https://www.sisterfromanotherplanet.com/bootsy-collins-lsd-was-a-big-part-of-why-i-left-james-browns-band/
- Bootsy later cited LSD and Brown's relentless demands ("you have to play the one") as central reasons for leaving the band and eventually joining George Clinton's P-Funk collective. Source: https://www.guitarworld.com/artists/bassists/bootsy-collins-album-of-the-year-funkateer
- Fred Wesley joined the J.B.'s in December 1970 and would eventually lead the group; the "These Are The J.B.'s" session pre-dates his arrival. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_J.B.%27s

### Genre Suggestions

1. **Classic Funk** (strongest case). This is James Brown's own backing band on a James Brown-produced track, released in funk's mainstream commercial pipeline (1970 King single, 1972 People LP) during the core classic-funk window. Chart/radio presence, major-label distribution, and the stylistic center of the vocabulary all line up. Source: `Matter/On Genres & Regions.md` definition of Classic Funk.
2. **Deep Funk** (secondary case, depending on which version the author leans on). The underlying *These Are The J.B.'s* album is the textbook deep-funk story: overlooked on release, rumored among collectors for 40+ years, finally surfaced through crate-dig curation at Now-Again. If the entry's angle is the lost album rather than the *Food For Thought* edit, Deep Funk is defensible.
3. I'd resist Jazz Funk here. The track is tight, pocket-forward, and short on improvisation in the jazz sense, despite the instrumental cast.

### Region Suggestions

Current value: **US - Southeast**.

1. **US - Southeast** (current, defensible). James Brown was the bandleader, producer, and the center of gravity for everything released under the J.B.'s name. His operations, publishing, and the original label relationship all trace back through Augusta, Georgia. Sticking with Southeast honors the Brown organization's center.
2. **US - Ohio** (strong alternative case). The band on the track is the Cincinnati-based Pacemakers (Bootsy and Catfish Collins); the session happened at King Studios, Cincinnati; the intended label was King, a Cincinnati institution. For a book where region "represents the track's origins," Ohio is the most literal answer. Sources: https://www.nowagainrecords.com/the-j-b-s-these-are-the-j-b-s/ · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catfish_Collins
3. **US - Midwest** is available if you want a less state-specific tag that still captures the Cincinnati grounding.

Recommendation: keep **US - Southeast** unless the writing theme centers Bootsy and the Cincinnati lineage, in which case **US - Ohio** is the better fit. I have not edited the field.
