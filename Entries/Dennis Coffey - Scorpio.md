### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The session guitarist steps out front."** Coffey was a Funk Brother — uncredited for years on Temptations, Edwin Starr, and Freda Payne hits — and "Scorpio" is what happened when the studio guy finally cut his own record. The framing lets you talk about Detroit's invisible architects and the moment one of them was suddenly on Soul Train.

2. **"Babbitt's one-take Everest."** The bass solo on "Scorpio" runs nearly half the song's length, was cut in a single take, and became the audition piece for working Detroit bassists ("if you couldn't play it, you couldn't get a gig"). This frames the entry around a specific, vivid musician moment and lets you point readers exactly where to listen.

3. **"The break that built a genre."** From Paul Winley's 1979 *Super Disco Brakes* compilation through Public Enemy, LL Cool J, Young MC, and Mark Ronson, "Scorpio" is one of the structural beams of hip-hop. Frame it as a 1971 instrumental hit that quietly became the future.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

Field is empty. Suggestions:

1. Around 1:25 the band drops out and Bob Babbitt takes a bass solo that runs almost half the record. Listen for the percussionist who shouts "Grab it, Babbitt!" in the background. He cut the whole solo in one take — does it sound improvised, or worked out?

2. The opening hook is not one guitar, it's nine, overdubbed and stacked across three octaves with fuzz and wah. Try to hear it as a horn section instead of guitars. Coffey said he arranged the parts the way he'd write for trombones.

3. This is the rhythm section that played on "Cloud Nine" and "Band of Gold." With no vocals to focus on, listen to what Uriel Jones and Pistol Allen do behind Babbitt's solo. Where do they push, where do they lay back?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- The opening guitar hook is nine guitar parts overdubbed across three octaves; Coffey arranged the fuzz tones the way he'd voice a horn section, with the wah-wah pedal mimicking trombones — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorpio_(instrumental) and https://www.songfacts.com/facts/dennis-coffey/scorpio
- The track was originally just titled "Song #4" before Coffey landed on a zodiac concept (other tracks became "Capricorn's Thing" and "Taurus") — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/dennis-coffey/scorpio
- Bob Babbitt's bass solo runs roughly 90 seconds — nearly half the song's 3:59 length — and was recorded in a single first take on his 1965 Fender Precision strung with La Bella flatwounds — Source: https://www.guitarworld.com/features/bob-babbitt-bass-solo-on-scorpio
- During Babbitt's solo you can hear percussionist Eddie "Bongo" Brown and tambourine player Jack Ashford shouting encouragement (the famous "Grab it, Babbitt!"); producer Mike Theodore kept the mic-bleed in the final mix — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/dennis-coffey/scorpio and https://www.guitarworld.com/features/bob-babbitt-bass-solo-on-scorpio
- Babbitt routinely placed a damp household sponge under the bridge to deaden the strings, a trick he used for R&B and gospel sessions — Source: https://www.guitarworld.com/features/bob-babbitt-bass-solo-on-scorpio
- "Scorpio" was canonized in early hip-hop via Paul Winley's 1979 compilation *Super Disco Brakes* and went on to be sampled by Public Enemy ("Night of the Living Baseheads"), LL Cool J ("Jingling Baby"), Young MC ("Bust a Move"), Geto Boys, House of Pain, Queen Latifah, and Mark Ronson, among many others — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorpio_(instrumental) and https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2015/02/scorpio-loop-history/
- The break also became a staple of Bristol's late-90s jungle/drum & bass scene through Roni Size, DJ Die, SUV, and DJ Krust of the Full Cycle crew — Source: https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2015/02/scorpio-loop-history/
- Featured in the *Better Call Saul* episode "Inflatable" (2016) — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorpio_(instrumental)
- Recorded at GM Recording Studios in East Detroit; mixed at RCA Recording Studios in New York — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorpio_(instrumental)

#### Album

- *Evolution* peaked at #36 on the Billboard 200 and #13 on the Best-Selling Soul LPs chart, on the strength of "Scorpio" — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_(Dennis_Coffey_album)
- Sussex catalog number SXBS 7004; ten tracks totaling roughly 32:50; produced by Mike Theodore — Source: https://www.bsnpubs.com/buddah/sussex.html and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_(Dennis_Coffey_album)
- Personnel reads like a Funk Brothers reunion: Bob Babbitt (bass), Uriel Jones and Richard "Pistol" Allen (drums), Earl Van Dyke (piano), Jack Ashford (tambourine), Eddie "Bongo" Brown (congas) — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorpio_(instrumental)
- Sussex Records, founded by Clarence Avant in late 1969, folded in July 1975 over $62,000 in unpaid taxes — making *Evolution* a relic of one of the most short-lived but vital Black-music indies of the era — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sussex_Records

#### Artist

- Dennis Coffey was a Funk Brother who introduced hard-rock textures to Motown — distortion, Echoplex tape-loop delay, and the wah-wah pedal — on tracks like the Temptations' "Cloud Nine," "Ball of Confusion," and "Psychedelic Shack," plus Edwin Starr's "War" and Freda Payne's "Band of Gold" — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Coffey
- On January 8, 1972 he became the first white artist ever to perform on *Soul Train*, playing "Scorpio" — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Coffey and https://www.setlist.fm/setlist/dennis-coffey/1972/soul-train-studios-los-angeles-ca-b86990e.html
- "Scorpio" was certified gold by the RIAA on December 9, 1971 — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Coffey
- Coffey also played lead guitar on Sixto Rodriguez's 1970 album *Cold Fact* and scored the 1974 blaxploitation film *Black Belt Jones* — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Coffey
- He cut his first session in 1955 at age 15 (backing Vic Gallon on "I'm Gone") and is still active — published memoir *Guitars, Bars and Motown Superstars* in 2004 — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Coffey

### Genre Suggestions

Current value: **Funk Rock**

1. **Classic Funk (recommend).** "Scorpio" was released in 1971 on a Black-music indie (Sussex), broke into the Billboard Hot 100 top 10 (#6), went gold, and got the artist on *Soul Train*. By the book's own definition, that's the bullseye of classic funk: a track whose primary identity is funk and that lived inside the major commercial pipeline of the 1969–1977 era. The instrumental format does not disqualify it — the rhythm section is straight funk pocket.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorpio_(instrumental)

2. **Funk Rock (current; defensible).** The nine overdubbed guitars, fuzz tones, and wah-wah lead are unmistakably rock-derived textures. Coffey himself was the Funk Brother who brought hard-rock effects into Motown. If you want this entry to do double duty as "where rock guitar meets the Detroit pocket," Funk Rock works.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Coffey

3. **Psychedelic Funk (alternative).** The wall of overdubbed fuzz guitars, the production-as-instrument approach, and Coffey's Motown work on "Cloud Nine" / "Psychedelic Shack" all point this direction. It's a stretch for the headline genre but worth noting as a stylistic ancestry.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Coffey
