The famous "ma-ma-say, ma-ma-sa" chant from yesterday's "Wanna Be Startin' Somethin'" is borrowed from Manu Dibango's 1972 hit "Soul Makossa." The lyrical larceny led to a lawsuit by Dibango, which was settled for one million French francs. Another lawsuit followed in 2009 when Rihanna sampled Michael Jackson's song in "Don't Stop the Music."

Makossa is urban dance music from Cameroon. The word comes from the Duala language where "kossa" means "dance," and "ma kossa" roughly translates to “I dance.” It's used as a shouted encouragement to keep the dancers moving.

The style took shape in the 1950s and 1960s and then evolved in the 1970s by absorbing American soul and funk. On “Soul Makossa,” you can hear both at once: funk in the groove, guitar textures, and horn-led arrangement; makossa in the layered rhythms and vocal chants.

The chant is one of the catchiest moments in the best-selling album of all time, *Thriller*, and it didn't originate with Michael Jackson. As you can hear in today's track, it came from Cameroon.

### Open Research Questions

The entry body contains two open items:
1. "define mokossa, relationship to funk"

**Makossa** is a popular music genre originating in Douala, Cameroon. The word comes from the Duala language: "kossa" means "dance" or "contortion," and "ma kossa" translates roughly to "the contortions" or "(I) dance." It was a cry of joy shouted to encourage dancers. The genre emerged in the 1950s when Nelle Eyoum combined elements of Douala ambasse bey, Bassa assiko, Ghanaian and Nigerian highlife, and Congolese rumba. It features strong electric bass rhythms, prominent brass, guitar, and a common time (4/4) feel at 130-170 BPM. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makossa

Makossa's relationship to funk: Makossa borrowed heavily from funk and disco in its brass sections and bass playing, absorbing those Western influences through the 1970s. Dibango's "Soul Makossa" is literally "soul + makossa," a deliberate fusion of American soul/funk feeling with the Cameroonian dance rhythm. The track's locked groove, wah-wah guitar textures, and saxophone-forward arrangement all carry funk DNA, while the rhythm and vocal chanting are purely makossa. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makossa, https://africanmusiclibrary.org/genre/Makossa

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- "Soul Makossa" was originally the B-side to "Hymne de la 8e Coupe d'Afrique des Nations," a tribute to the Cameroon football team for the 1972 Africa Cup of Nations. The A-side was considered the real record. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_Makossa
- The lyrics were written primarily in Duala (a Cameroonian language) by poet S.M. Eno Belinga. The famous "ma-ma-ko, ma-ma-sa, ma-ko ma-ko-sa" vocal refrain is Duala language, not nonsense syllables. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_Makossa
- It has been called "the most sampled African song in history." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_Makossa
- Rolling Stone ranked it #35 on their 2022 list of "200 Greatest Dance Songs of All Time." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_Makossa
- The track initially flopped in Cameroon. Dibango faced ridicule before it found its audience abroad. — Source: https://www.conorherbert.com/music/manu-dibango
- The demand created by David Mancuso and Frankie Crocker was so intense that at least 23 cover versions appeared to capitalize on it, including one by a group called Afrique on Mainstream Records. Dibango's original and the Afrique cover were on the Billboard Hot 100 at the same time. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_Makossa
- PBS's 2024 series *Disco: Soundtrack of a Revolution* examines the track's significance to disco history. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_Makossa

#### Artist
- Born Emmanuel N'Djoké Dibango on December 12, 1933, in Douala, French Cameroon. His father was Yabassi, his mother was Duala. He noted difficulty identifying completely with either heritage. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manu_Dibango
- He was sent to France at age 15 to study, where he learned piano at the lycée de Chartres before switching to saxophone. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manu_Dibango
- Before his solo career, he performed with the Congolese group African Jazz. — Source: https://bestclassicbands.com/manu-dibango-obituary-soul-makossa-3-24-20/
- "Soul Makossa" made him the first African artist to crack the US Billboard Hot 100 top 40. — Source: https://www.conorherbert.com/music/manu-dibango
- He was nominated for two Grammy Awards at the 16th Annual Grammy Awards (1974): Best R&B Instrumental Performance and Best Instrumental Composition. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manu_Dibango
- Collaborators include Fela Kuti, Herbie Hancock, Bill Laswell, Bernie Worrell, Ladysmith Black Mambazo, King Sunny Adé, Don Cherry, and Sly and Robbie. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manu_Dibango
- He married Belgian actress Marie-Josée ("Coco") in 1957; she died in 1995. They had four children. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manu_Dibango
- Appointed UNESCO Artist for Peace in 2004. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manu_Dibango
- Died March 24, 2020, in Melun, France, from COVID-19. He was 86. He was among the first prominent musicians to die from the virus. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manu_Dibango
- The lawsuit chain: Dibango sued Michael Jackson in 1986 for using the "mama-say" chant in "Wanna Be Startin' Somethin'" (1982), settling for one million French francs. When Rihanna sampled Jackson's version in "Don't Stop the Music" (2007), Dibango sued both artists again in 2009. The Paris court ultimately rejected the motion against Rihanna after Dibango had previously accepted credit on her releases. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_Makossa, https://www.cbc.ca/news/entertainment/cameroon-singer-sues-jackson-rihanna-for-musical-hook-1.836645
- The David Mancuso discovery story: In 1972, Mancuso found the record in a West Indian/Jamaican record store in Brooklyn and played it at his legendary Loft parties. The response was electric, and the few available copies in NYC were snapped up. DJ Frankie Crocker then broke it on WBLS-FM (New York's most popular Black radio station), creating explosive demand that led Atlantic Records to license it from Fiesta. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_Makossa, https://bestclassicbands.com/manu-dibango-obituary-soul-makossa-3-24-20/

### Genre Suggestions

The genre is currently set to **Afrobeat**.

1. **Afrobeat (current)**: This is the best fit from the available enumeration. While makossa is technically a distinct genre from Fela Kuti's afrobeat, the book's genre definitions note that "Afrobeat" is used as a broad label for African-rooted funk. "Soul Makossa" fuses Cameroonian rhythm with funk/soul elements, fitting the spirit of the Afrobeat category. The book's genre description even notes "I could easily have chosen Afrofunk as the label." This track is precisely the kind of African funk fusion the category is designed to hold.

2. **Jazz-Funk**: Dibango was a jazz saxophonist who played vibraphone and sax, and the track features jazz-inflected horn work over a funk groove. His Grammy nominations were in jazz/R&B categories. However, the track's primary identity is African dance music with funk, not jazz musicians playing funk.

3. **Classic Funk**: The track charted in 1973, within the classic funk era (1968-1977), and it reached the Billboard Hot 100 and Hot Soul Singles charts. However, its identity as Cameroonian makossa makes it a better fit for Afrobeat than Classic Funk.

**Recommendation**: Keep Afrobeat. It's the right call.
