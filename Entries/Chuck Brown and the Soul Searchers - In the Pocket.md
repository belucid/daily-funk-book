### Open Research Questions

The author left no `open:` items in this entry. Three questions surfaced during research:

- **Who is Donald Banks, the credited composer of "In the Pocket"?** AllMusic credits the song to Donald Banks (Chuck Brown himself is not credited as writer on this track). Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/funk-express-mw0001145136 . The most likely identification is the Washington, D.C. singer Donald Banks, known locally as "Jackie Boy," who formed Nature's Creation with his two brothers in the mid-1970s and was part of the local go-go scene; he later cut the D.C. go-go record "Status Quo" (1983), which appears on the *Good to Go* soundtrack. Source: https://www.discogs.com/artist/78859-Donald-Banks . **This identification is NOT confirmed** — Discogs does not list him in the *Funk Express* credits, and no interview, liner note, or BMI/ASCAP record I could reach names him in connection with this song. Approach taken: Discogs master/release/reissue JSON (none of the three Discogs releases carry per-track writer credits at all), AllMusic credits page, Wikipedia, MusicBrainz, and targeted web searches on "Donald Banks" + Chuck Brown / go-go / songwriter. Suggested next step for the author: the ASCAP/BMI public repertory search, or the 2021 Raw Venture reissue liner notes by Melissa A. Weber.
- **Was "In the Pocket" ever a single?** No. The only singles pulled from *Funk Express* were "Sticks and Stones" (US R&B No. 55, 1980) and "Come On and Boogie" (did not chart). A Discogs release-type search returned zero 7" or 12" pressings of "In the Pocket." Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Brown_discography and Discogs release search on the master.
- **Is there a precise release date for *Funk Express*?** Discogs, Wikipedia, and AllMusic all give only "1980." MusicBrainz records the US Source Records LP (SOR-3234) and its release group as **September 20, 1980**. Source: https://musicbrainz.org/release-group/d863fed7-fdc3-4d56-819a-69dbf84752d4 . Treat this as a single-source lead, not established fact — MusicBrainz dates of this vintage are frequently taken from the corresponding weekly *Billboard* issue date. I could not open the *Billboard* September 20, 1980 issue on worldradiohistory.com to confirm (the archive URL redirected). Flagged for the author.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"A D.C. record made everywhere but D.C."** This is the most go-go-sounding cut on an album that a Los Angeles label (Source Records, run by Logan Westbrooks) commissioned, that a Houston-born ex-Jazz Crusader (Wayne Henderson) produced, and that Stax's former mastering engineer (Larry Nix) cut in Memphis. The theme is what happens when the most place-specific music in America gets routed through the national record industry — and whether the pocket survives the trip. Sources: https://www.allmusic.com/album/funk-express-mw0001145136 , https://www.discogs.com/release/14713834-Chuck-Brown-The-Soul-Searchers-Funk-Express , https://www.discogs.com/artist/365586-Larry-Nix
- **"What 'in the pocket' actually means."** The title is musician's shop-talk for the exact place where the rhythm section locks — and this is an entry where the title is the lesson. Chuck Brown's whole innovation was refusing to leave the pocket, keeping the beat running between songs so nobody left the floor. An angle that lets the book define its most-used and least-explained word. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go-go , https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/chuck-brown
- **"The album after the hit."** *Bustin' Loose* went gold and put a No. 1 R&B single on the board; *Funk Express* charted nowhere at all, and Chuck Brown would not release another studio album for fourteen years. AllMusic notes it "fared well in the Washington, D.C./Baltimore area" — which is the whole story of go-go's national ceiling in one sentence. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Brown_discography , https://www.allmusic.com/album/funk-express-mw0001145136
- **"Lorton to the pocket."** Brown learned guitar in prison — he traded cigarettes for the instrument at Lorton Correctional Complex, then played the yard's Saturday shows. An origin story that reframes the groove as something built out of very long stretches of time. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Brown , https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/chuck-brown

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- The band never lets the groove stop to breathe. Time how long you can go without hearing a full stop, then ask: is this a song with a beat, or a beat with a song laid on top of it?
- Follow only the congas and cowbell for one pass, ignoring Chuck's guitar and the horns. That percussion layer is the thing that made go-go go-go — can you hear it doing something a straight funk drummer wouldn't?
- Play this back-to-back with "Bustin' Loose" from the year before. The hit is tighter and shorter; this one is six minutes with room to wander. Which one sounds more like a live D.C. band, and which one sounds more like a record label?
- Producer Wayne Henderson was a jazz trombonist with the Crusaders before he ran boards. Listen for where the horn arrangement gets too neat for a street band. Is that a compromise or an upgrade?

### Interest and Surprise

### Track
- "In the Pocket" is credited to composer Donald Banks — not to Chuck Brown, who wrote his own biggest hit "Bustin' Loose." — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/funk-express-mw0001145136
- AllMusic's Alex Henderson singles it out as one of the album's "infectious go-go grooves," alongside "Come on and Boogie" and "Sticks and Stones." — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/funk-express-mw0001145136
- At 6:08 it is the second-longest cut on the LP, beaten only by the closing Crusaders remake — a long leash for a record made under a hit-chasing major-distributed label. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/14713834-Chuck-Brown-The-Soul-Searchers-Funk-Express
- It was never issued as a single and sat unreissued for 41 years until the 2021 Raw Venture remaster brought it to streaming. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Brown_discography , https://www.discogs.com/release/19267150-Chuck-Brown-The-Soul-Searchers-Funk-Express

### Album
- *Funk Express* was produced by **Wayne Henderson**, the founding trombonist of the Jazz Crusaders, who quit the band in 1976 to produce full time. AllMusic frames the pairing bluntly: "Henderson's days as a full-time bop trombonist were a thing of the past." — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/funk-express-mw0001145136 , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayne_Henderson_(musician)
- The album closes with a remake of the Crusaders' own 1976 hit "Keep That Same Old Feeling" — Henderson producing a D.C. go-go band covering his old group. He also wrote the ballad "Time Has No Ending." — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/funk-express-mw0001145136
- The LP was mastered by **Larry Nix**, who had been Stax Records' mastering engineer until 1975 and then ran L. Nix & Co. inside Ardent Studios in Memphis. His initials are cut into the runout: `MCA-2292 A1 -L.NIX-`. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/artist/365586-Larry-Nix , https://www.discogs.com/release/14713834-Chuck-Brown-The-Soul-Searchers-Funk-Express
- Commercially it was a total whiff: no Billboard 200, no Independent Albums, no Hot R&B placement — a hard fall from *Bustin' Loose*, which went RIAA Gold and hit No. 31 pop / No. 5 R&B the year before. Only "Sticks and Stones" charted, at No. 55 R&B. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Brown_discography
- AllMusic notes it nonetheless "fared well in the Washington, D.C./Baltimore area," and describes "Sticks and Stones" as go-go crossed with George Clinton's P-Funk. — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/funk-express-mw0001145136
- Robert Christgau's B+ is widely attributed to *Funk Express* (Wikipedia's ratings box cites it), but Christgau's own artist page shows that B+ attached to ***Bustin' Loose*** (Source, 1979), with no *Funk Express* review present at all. Treat the Wikipedia rating as unreliable. — Source: http://www.robertchristgau.com/get_artist2.php?id=2595 vs https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funk_Express
- The 2021 Raw Venture remaster added four previously unreleased tracks cut at the same time, including a cover of Johnny "Guitar" Watson's "I Want to Ta Ta You Baby," with liner notes by Melissa A. Weber (New Orleans' DJ Soul Sister). — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/19267150-Chuck-Brown-The-Soul-Searchers-Funk-Express , https://www.allmusic.com/album/funk-express-mw0001145136
- Source Records was a Los Angeles label founded by Black music-industry executive **Logan H. Westbrooks**, who landed an MCA distribution deal in 1978 and used it to break "Bustin' Loose" nationally. — Source: https://www.washingtonpost.com/blogs/arts-post/post/a-chat-with-logan-westbrooks-the-man-who-helped-break-chuck-browns-bustin-loose/2012/06/01/gJQAi8196U_blog.html (headline and summary; full text paywalled/403), corroborated by https://www.bsnpubs.com/mca/mcadist/source.pdf (BSN Pubs' Source Records discography, MCA-distributed labels series)

### Artist
- Chuck Brown (Charles Louis Brown) was born **August 22, 1936** in Gaston, North Carolina, moved to D.C. at six, and was living on the streets by fifteen. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Brown
- He served eight years at Lorton Correctional Complex on a murder conviction he said was self-defense, and got his first guitar there by trading cigarettes for it — then played the prison's weekly Saturday shows. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Brown , https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/chuck-brown
- Before the Soul Searchers (formed 1966) he played with the Earls of Rhythm and **Los Latinos** — the Latin band that supplied the conga-and-timbale vocabulary go-go would be built on. — Source: https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/chuck-brown
- Go-go's core innovation is refusing to stop: by the mid-1970s Brown was running one song into the next over an unbroken percussion bed so nobody would leave the dance floor, and he named the style himself — "you had go-go clubs and go-go girls, but they didn't have go-go music, so I just decided to call this go-go music." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go-go , https://www.offbeat.com/news/chuck-brown-on-the-birth-of-go-go/
- Brown repeatedly credited Grover Washington Jr.'s "Mister Magic" (1974) as the seed of the go-go beat, while also saying he and Washington both took it from a Black church gospel beat. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go-go
- The Soul Searchers' 1974 instrumental "Ashley's Roachclip" contains one of the most-sampled drum breaks in recorded music, later carrying Eric B. & Rakim's "Paid in Full," among many others. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Brown , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashley%27s_Roachclip
- Nelly's 2002 No. 1 "Hot in Herre" interpolates "Bustin' Loose," which is also the Washington Nationals' home-run song at Nationals Park and a victory anthem for the Capitals and Wizards. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bustin%27_Loose_(song)
- He was named a **2005 NEA National Heritage Fellow**, received D.C.'s Mayor's Arts Award in 2000, and got his first Grammy nomination in 2011 — at seventy-four. — Source: https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/chuck-brown , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Brown
- He died **May 16, 2012** at Johns Hopkins Hospital of multiple organ failure, aged 75. D.C. renamed the 1900 block of 7th Street NW "Chuck Brown Way" in 2009, opened Chuck Brown Memorial Park in 2014, and Mayor Vincent Gray declared August 22 "Chuck Brown Day." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Brown , https://www.nbcwashington.com/news/local/chuck-brown-day-in-washington/1927035/

### Genre Suggestions

Current value: `Go-Go`. Working definitions from `Matter/On Subgenres.md`.

1. **Go-Go (keep — recommended).** The book defines go-go as "a specific time and place: early 1980s Washington, D.C." with "a conga-and-cowbell-driven beat and a call and response between the singer and the crowd," with the band standing in for the crowd on studio recordings. *Funk Express* is a 1980 D.C. record whose personnel includes a dedicated conga/percussion chair (Gregory Gerran) plus a second percussionist on timbales (Leroy Fleming), and AllMusic names "In the Pocket" specifically as one of the album's "infectious go-go grooves." Wikipedia's first genre tag for the album is go-go. This is the correct primary label and needs no change. Sources: https://www.allmusic.com/album/funk-express-mw0001145136 , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funk_Express
2. **Classic Funk (alternative).** The book draws classic funk as roughly 1969–1977, music "released into the commercial pipeline of major labels, well-distributed singles and albums." *Funk Express* is 1980 — just outside the window — but it is a textbook major-pipeline record: an LA label with an MCA distribution deal, a professional outside producer, Memphis mastering, and a hit-single strategy. Dusty Groove describes it as "more of a straight-ahead funk affair than his later Go-Go records." The case is real but the date and the D.C. specificity both cut against it. Source: https://www.dustygroove.com/item/985547/Chuck-Brown-The-Soul-Searchers:Funk-Express-with-bonus-tracks (via search snippet; the page itself 403s to automated fetch)
3. **Jazz Funk (alternative).** Wikipedia's second genre tag for the album, and it is not arbitrary: the producer is a founding Jazz Crusader, the album closes with a Crusaders remake, one A-side is subtitled "A Fantasy of Fusion," and Gip Noble arranged strings. Against it: the book's jazz-funk definition centers on "funk played by jazz musicians whose earlier careers were in more traditional jazz styles," and the Soul Searchers are not that band — the jazz is in the production chair, not on the bandstand. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funk_Express , https://www.allmusic.com/album/funk-express-mw0001145136

**Recommendation: leave `genre` as `Go-Go`.**
