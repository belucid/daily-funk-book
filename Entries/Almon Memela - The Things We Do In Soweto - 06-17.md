“Soweto” is an acronym for South Western Townships, the cluster of segregated Black neighborhoods southwest of Johannesburg. In 1955, thousands gathered in the Kliptown area of Soweto to adopt the Freedom Charter, a founding document of the anti-apartheid movement. Police broke up the meeting, and 156 leaders were arrested and tried for treason.

The road from the Freedom Charter to the dismantling of apartheid laws on June 17, 1991, was long and brutal. Music from Soweto played a part, carrying the township’s voice beyond South Africa and drawing global attention.

Today’s song, “The Things We Do in Soweto,” comes from Almon Memela’s 1975 album *Funky Africa*. Memela moved to Soweto in 1956 to work in the gold mines. He taught himself to play guitar on homemade instruments, then worked as a guitarist, teacher, and record label talent scout. A year after *Funky Africa*, Soweto erupted in student protests over the enforced use of the white minority's Afrikaans language in schools, followed by a deadly police crackdown. This track captures the everyday music, the “things we do,” in the township just before Soweto became a global rallying cry for freedom and resistance.

### Open Research Questions

OPEN: tell me more about Soweto. 5+ interesting facts/stories.

**Research Resolution — Soweto (2026-05-26):**

1. **The name is a bureaucratic acronym that became a global symbol.** "Soweto" is an English syllabic abbreviation for **South Western Townships**, the cluster of segregated Black areas south-west of Johannesburg. It was coined in a 1949 naming competition initiated by William Carr, chair of Johannesburg's non-European Affairs committee, and formally adopted in **1963**. A made-by-committee administrative label became, within a generation, the most recognized place name in the global anti-apartheid struggle. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soweto ; https://www.britannica.com/place/Soweto

2. **It was born from gold, then forcibly relocated by a plague.** Soweto grew out of the same 1885 Witwatersrand gold rush that created Johannesburg. Black labor was concentrated near the mines, then physically pushed away from the city centre after a **1904 bubonic plague outbreak in the Brickfields area** gave colonial authorities the pretext they wanted: they evacuated Black workers 25 km south to Klipspruit, fenced the area, and razed the original neighborhood. The "townships" began as a plague-sanitation maneuver and hardened into apartheid policy. Sources: https://www.britannica.com/place/Soweto ; https://www.southafrica.net/za/en/travel/article/the-vibrant-history-of-soweto

3. **The Freedom Charter was adopted in Soweto a year before any of this had a name.** On **June 26, 1955**, ~3,000 delegates from the Congress Alliance gathered in **Kliptown** (now part of Soweto), in a field surrounded by chicken-wire so they could legally call it a private meeting. They ratified the **Freedom Charter** — "South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white" — the founding document of the anti-apartheid movement and later the platform of the ANC. Police broke the gathering up on day two; 156 of its leaders were arrested in the 1956 Treason Trial that followed. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_the_People_(1955)

4. **Vilakazi Street is the only street on Earth where two Nobel Peace Prize laureates have lived.** Nelson Mandela (Nobel 1993) lived there from 1946 to 1961, and Desmond Tutu (Nobel 1984) moved in in 1975 — the same year Almon Memela cut *Funky Africa*. The street is named for Dr. Benedict Wallet Vilakazi, the first scholar to publish poetry in Zulu. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutu_House ; https://www.cnn.com/2015/02/24/business/soweto-tourism-vilakazi-street

5. **June 16, 1976: the Soweto Uprising began as a school-language protest and ended apartheid's international standing.** ~20,000 Black students walked out of school over the imposition of **Afrikaans as the medium of instruction** — a language almost none of them spoke at home. Police opened fire. **176 children were killed on June 16 alone**; an estimated 575 dead nationally by February 1977. Twelve-year-old **Hector Pieterson** was among the first killed; **Sam Nzima's photograph** of his body being carried by a fellow student went around the world and is widely credited as the image that decisively turned international opinion against the apartheid regime. June 16 is now South Africa's national Youth Day. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soweto_uprising ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hector_Pieterson ; https://time.com/4365138/soweto-anniversary-photograph/

6. **It's enormous — and was deliberately built tiny.** Soweto covers roughly **200 square kilometres** across **30+ townships**, with a population of ~1.27 million in the 2011 census and estimated near 1.9 million today (the largest Black urban settlement in South Africa). The dominant housing stock is the **"matchbox house"** — government-built two- or three-room concrete boxes from the 1930s onward, originally with **no sewage and no electricity**. Most were tiny enough that residents poured decades of personal investment into plastering walls and adding interior doors. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soweto ; https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00020189608707853

7. **The Orlando Power Station lit Johannesburg while Soweto sat in the dark.** British engineers built the **Orlando Power Station** in 1942 in the middle of what would become Soweto. For more than five decades it sent electricity north to Johannesburg while most Sowetans had no electricity at all. Decommissioned in 1998, the cooling towers were repainted as massive murals and are now a Soweto landmark (Orlando Towers, used for bungee jumping). The visual is hard to beat: two giant power-station chimneys that powered the city that wouldn't power the township they stood in. Sources: https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/orlando-towers ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Orlando,_Soweto

8. **It's a soccer cathedral and a music laboratory.** Soweto is home to two of South Africa's largest football clubs — **Kaizer Chiefs** and **Orlando Pirates** — whose **Soweto Derby** is one of the most-watched fixtures on the African continent. One Soweto suburb is also credited as the **founding place of kwaito**, the slowed-down, low-end-heavy house/hip-hop hybrid that became the defining sound of post-apartheid youth in the 1990s. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soweto

**Why this matters for the entry.** Memela cut "The Things We Do In Soweto" in **1975** — two decades after the Freedom Charter was signed in Kliptown, one year before the Uprising, in a township that had been calling for self-determination since before it had its current name. "The things we do" reads very differently when you know that the place where they were done was built by plague evacuation, lit by a power station that didn't serve it, and produced both a Nobel-laureate street and the world's most famous photograph of a child shot dead in protest.

OPEN: when did apartheid end in South Africa?

**Research Resolution — End of Apartheid (2026-05-26):**

There is no single date — apartheid ended in stages over roughly four years, with three distinct "endings" depending on what you mean.

**The political opening (February 1990).** On **February 2, 1990**, State President **F. W. de Klerk** used his opening address to Parliament to unban the ANC, the South African Communist Party, the Pan Africanist Congress, and 30+ other organizations, and to announce that Mandela would be released. Nine days later, on **February 11, 1990**, Nelson Mandela walked out of Victor Verster Prison after **27 years** behind bars. This was the political point of no return, but apartheid was still the law of the land. Sources: https://history.state.gov/milestones/1989-1992/apartheid ; https://www.history.com/articles/end-apartheid-steps

**The legal repeal (June 1991).** Between late 1990 and mid-1991, Parliament tore out apartheid's statutory spine: the **Population Registration Act** (which classified every South African by race at birth), the **Group Areas Act** (which forced racial segregation of residential areas — the law that built Soweto), and the **Land Acts** (which restricted Black land ownership to ~13% of the country). The repeals were largely complete by **June 17, 1991**, the most commonly cited "legal end of apartheid" date. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartheid ; https://www.history.com/articles/end-apartheid-steps

**The white referendum (March 1992).** On **March 17, 1992**, white South Africans were asked in a referendum whether de Klerk should continue negotiating an end to apartheid. The result: **68.7% yes, 31.2% no**. De Klerk the next day: *"Today we have closed the book on apartheid."* This was a political mandate, not a constitutional change, but it removed any white claim that reform lacked popular support. Source: https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/march-17/south-africa-votes-to-end-apartheid

**The democratic ending (April 1994).** The new **interim Constitution** took effect on **April 27, 1994**, and for the first time every adult South African — regardless of race — could vote in a national election. The ANC won. **Nelson Mandela was inaugurated as South Africa's first Black president on May 10, 1994.** This is the date most South Africans treat as the actual end of apartheid; it's now celebrated as **Freedom Day**. Sources: https://history.state.gov/milestones/1989-1992/apartheid ; https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2024/4/27/south-africa-30-years-after-apartheid-what-has-changed

**The short answer.** If forced to pick one date: **April 27, 1994** is the popular answer (first non-racial election), **June 17, 1991** is the legal answer (apartheid statutes repealed), and **February 2, 1990** is the political answer (the day de Klerk announced it would happen).

**Why this matters for the entry.** *Funky Africa* (1975) was made nineteen years before South Africa's first free election — under apartheid, on a major label, by a Black producer documenting "the things we do in Soweto." That the album then sat largely unheard until reissues in 2015 and 2022 is a small reminder that the cultural recovery from apartheid is still in progress decades after the political one ended.

**Prior research (resolution of an earlier version of this OPEN question, about Heritage Day):** Retained below in case it's still useful to the writing.

**When it started.** Heritage Day was first celebrated on **24 September 1995**, under Nelson Mandela's post-apartheid Government of National Unity. It emerged from a political compromise. The original Public Holidays Bill (1994) did not include 24 September. In KwaZulu-Natal, the date had long been observed as **Shaka Day**, commemorating the presumed death of the Zulu King Shaka in 1828. When the Inkatha Freedom Party (IFP), the majority-Zulu opposition party, objected to its exclusion, Parliament's compromise was to keep the date but rename and broaden it — instead of honoring one cultural figure, it became a day for *all* South Africans to celebrate the country's diverse cultural inheritance. Mandela in 1996: "we did so because we knew that our rich and varied cultural heritage has a profound power to help build our new nation." Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heritage_Day_(South_Africa) ; https://sahistory.org.za/article/heritage-day-braai-day-or-shaka-day-whose-heritage-it-anyway ; https://sahistory.org.za/dated-event/first-celebration-national-heritage-day

**What's done on the day.** It's a public holiday. Typical activities include cultural events, traditional dress, music, dance, and gatherings at heritage sites. By far the dominant popular observance is the **braai** — the South African barbecue. In 2005, Jan Scannell ("Jan Braai") launched a campaign to rebrand the holiday as **National Braai Day**, framing the communal fire as a unifier across race and class lines. Archbishop Desmond Tutu became its patron in 2007, calling it a chance for citizens to do "one thing together" irrespective of "politics, of your culture, of your race." The National Heritage Council initially rejected the rebrand as trivializing but endorsed it in 2008. The day is still officially Heritage Day; "Braai Day" is the parallel popular name. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heritage_Day_(South_Africa) ; https://braai.com/ ; https://www.npr.org/2021/09/24/1040353464/south-africa-braai-day

**Political complexity.** The holiday remains contested. As the SA History Online piece notes, "for some, the Great Trek signifies courage; for others, it represents invasion." In an eleven-language nation still negotiating apartheid's legacy, "one person's heritage often constitutes another's trauma." Some Zulu communities still observe the day as Shaka Day. Source: https://sahistory.org.za/article/heritage-day-braai-day-or-shaka-day-whose-heritage-it-anyway

**Equivalents in other cultures.** The closest international analogues are official holidays designed to celebrate a *nation's* multicultural diversity, rather than the heritage of a single ethnic group:
- **Canada — Canadian Multiculturalism Day** (June 27, established 2002): an official day recognizing Canada's multicultural heritage and contribution. The most structurally similar holiday to Heritage Day. Source: https://www.canada.ca/en/canadian-heritage/services/important-commemorative-days/canadian-multiculturalism-day.html
- **Australia — Harmony Day** (March 21, since 1999): a day of cultural diversity celebration, deliberately aligned with the UN International Day for the Elimination of Racial Discrimination. Source: https://www.harmony.gov.au/
- **United States — no single equivalent.** Instead, heritage months are distributed across the calendar: Black History Month (February), Asian American & Pacific Islander Heritage Month (May), Hispanic Heritage Month (Sep 15 - Oct 15), Native American Heritage Month (November). Source: https://www.archives.gov/news/topics/heritage-months
- **United Nations — World Day for Cultural Diversity for Dialogue and Development** (May 21): the global, supranational version of the same idea. Source: https://www.un.org/en/observances/cultural-diversity-day

Single-figure precedents (analogous to the original Shaka Day before it was broadened):
- **Spain / Latin America — Día de la Hispanidad / Día de la Raza** (October 12): a date with similarly fraught politics over whose heritage is being celebrated.
- **USA — Indigenous Peoples' Day / Columbus Day** (second Monday of October): also a renaming battle over the same date.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- Around the 2:30 mark, listen to how the guitar line and the percussion start trading the lead. This isn't a typical American funk arrangement — there's no horn section pushing the song. What's holding it together instead?
- The album opens with a cover of Donny Hathaway and Leroy Hutson's "The Ghetto" and closes with this song. Listen back to back with Hathaway's original. What did Memela keep, and what did he replace with something Sowetan?

### Album
- Funky Africa was recorded at Superdisc Studios in Johannesburg and produced by Memela himself — unusual for a Black South African musician on a major label in 1975. — Source: https://www.flatinternational.org/template_volume.php?volume_id=206
- The album opens with a cover of Donny Hathaway and Leroy Hutson's "The Ghetto" (retitled "Funky Africa (The Ghetto)"), a direct nod to American soul-funk being absorbed into the township sound. — Source: https://almonmemela.bandcamp.com/album/funky-africa
- The original liner notes describe the album as capturing "the call of the wild and the scream of the Jumbo; the throb of the bush and the roar of Commissioner Street" — an explicit framing of rural Africa colliding with downtown Johannesburg. — Source: https://almonmemela.bandcamp.com/album/funky-africa
- The original 1975 LP listed no musician credits at all; later research suggests pianist Bheki Mseleku may have played on the album (he composed track "Big Mama"), but the personnel for "The Things We Do In Soweto" specifically remain undocumented. — Source: https://www.backseatmafia.com/album-review-almon-memela-funky-africa-a-south-african-soul-jazz-classic-revived/
- The album achieved "holy grail" status among collectors before being reissued in 2022 by We Are Busy Bodies (under license from Gallo Record Company), in a limited run of 700 copies. — Sources: http://electricjive.blogspot.com/2011/03/almon-memela-funky-africa-1975.html and https://almonmemela.bandcamp.com/album/funky-africa

### Artist
- Almon Sandisa Memela was born in Donnybrook, KwaZulu-Natal in 1936, was self-taught on homemade guitars, and went to Johannesburg in 1956 to work the gold mines before pivoting to a music career. — Source: https://sisgwenjazz.wordpress.com/2022/04/04/4738/
- He took guitar lessons at Dorkay House in 1958 but was already skilled enough that Union Artists asked him to teach instead. — Source: https://sisgwenjazz.wordpress.com/2022/04/04/4738/
- He played in the original King Kong pit band and did uncredited session work behind Miriam Makeba and the Skylarks in the 1950s. — Source: https://sisgwenjazz.wordpress.com/2022/04/04/4738/
- He led at least four named groups across genres: Almon's Jazz Eight (1963 onward), Almon's Jazz Kings, the mbaqanga/bump group Abafana baMaswazi, and the soul outfit A.M. Stragglers. — Source: http://flatint.blogspot.com/2011/03/almon-memela-discography.html
- By the time Funky Africa came out he had become a producer and talent scout at WEA Records South Africa — i.e. he was effectively producing himself on a major label. — Source: https://sisgwenjazz.wordpress.com/2022/04/04/4738/

### Genre Suggestions

- **Afrobeat (currently selected).** Per the book's own definition, Afrobeat is "used as a broad label for any funk rooted in Africa." A South African funk track from a Johannesburg studio is squarely inside that umbrella. This is the strongest fit given the book's available labels.
- **Jazz Funk** — also defensible. Memela came up through the Dorkay House jazz scene, led "Almon's Jazz Eight" from 1963, and the album is often categorized as "soul-jazz-funk" by reviewers (see Backseat Mafia review). The musical traits (instrumental focus, room for improvisation, no vocal chorus) line up with the book's Jazz Funk definition. The argument against is that the book scopes jazz-funk primarily to American jazz musicians making the funk crossover; Memela's South African context fits Afrobeat more naturally.
- **Deep Funk** — also defensible on the "commercial fate" criterion. The book defines deep funk as music "overlooked on release and only found their audience later through crate-digging DJs." Funky Africa was a 1975 commercial flop that became a "holy grail" record for collectors and didn't reach wider audiences until 2015 (Strut compilation) and 2022 (Busy Bodies reissue). However, since Afrobeat covers the geographic-stylistic axis better, Deep Funk feels secondary.
