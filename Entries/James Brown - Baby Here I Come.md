### Open Research Questions

The author left no `open:` markers in this entry. Three questions came up during research that the author should know about.

**1. Where and when was "Baby Here I Come" actually recorded?**

No source states this outright, but the evidence points hard at **the after-show set at Bell Auditorium, Augusta, Georgia, on the night of October 1, 1969**. The chain:

- Wikipedia's Motherlode infobox gives an album-wide recording span of December 12, 1967 – September 14, 1972, and assigns a studio or venue to most tracks — but **not** to track 9, "Baby Here I Come." It is one of the few tracks left unlocated. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherlode_(James_Brown_album)
- Wikipedia's per-track personnel for track 9 is: James Brown (vocals); Joseph Davis and Richard "Kush" Griffith (trumpet); Fred Wesley (trombone); Maceo Parker (sax); Eldee Williams (tenor); St. Clair Pinckney (tenor/baritone); Alfonzo "Country" Kellum and Jimmy Nolen (guitar); "Sweet" Charles Sherrell (bass); John "Jabo" Starks (drums). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherlode_(James_Brown_album)
- That is, name for name, the band on the October 1, 1969 Augusta tapes. Discogs' credit list for *Live At Home With His Bad Self—The After Show* names Sherrell, Stubblefield, Starks, Melvin Parker, Nolen, Kellum, Eldee Williams, Pinckney, Maceo Parker, Joseph Davis, Richard "Kush" Griffith and Fred Wesley. Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/14465298-James-Brown-Live-At-Home-With-His-Bad-SelfThe-After-Show
- Side A, track 1 of that 2019 Record Store Day LP is "Baby Here I Come—False Start & Complete Take" (10:46), and the release notes say it is "the after-show set, featuring extended versions of rare (released) cuts, new mixes." A 4:27 version had already been "released" — on Motherlode. Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/14465298-James-Brown-Live-At-Home-With-His-Bad-SelfThe-After-Show
- "Baby Here I Come" does **not** appear on the main 2019 album *Live At Home With His Bad Self*, i.e. it was not part of either of the two public sets that night — only the private after-show. Source: https://www.discogs.com/master/1629021-James-Brown-Live-At-Home-With-His-Bad-Self
- SecondHandSongs dates the first recording of the song to 1969. Source: https://secondhandsongs.com/performance/18650 (page is behind Cloudflare; the date was read from the search-result summary and should be spot-checked by a fact checker).

**Recommendation:** treat "recorded October 1, 1969, Bell Auditorium, Augusta, Georgia, after-show set" as very likely but not documented in a single citable sentence. The definitive source would be the liner notes to the 2019 *Live At Home With His Bad Self* box (Alan Leeds and Harry Weinger produced the reissue) or the 2003 *Motherlode* expanded-edition booklet. Neither is online. What I tried: Wikipedia wikitext, all Discogs releases of both albums via the API, the uDiscover and PR Newswire announcements, Record Store Day's listing (403), AllMusic (403), WhoSampled (403), SecondHandSongs (Cloudflare), and jamesbrown.com (404 on the track page).

**2. Is the Motherlode edit the same performance as the 2019 "complete take"?**

Almost certainly yes — 4:27 versus 10:46 with a false start — but no source says "edited from." Worth an ear test: play them back to back.

**3. A contradiction inside Wikipedia's own infobox.**

The Motherlode infobox lists track 6 ("Can I Get Some Help") under *both* Bell Auditorium, Augusta (venue, "Track 5 & 6") and Criteria Studios, Miami (studio, "Track 6 & 13"). One of those is wrong. It does not affect track 9, but it is a caution against leaning on that infobox's track-number mapping — the numbering also appears to follow the 13-track 2003 expanded edition, not the 11-track 1988 original. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherlode_(James_Brown_album)

### Writing Theme Suggestions

The `writing theme` field is empty. Three candidates:

- **"The band that played after everybody went home."** October 1, 1969 was James Brown's homecoming to Augusta — the town where he had shined shoes and done time as a teenager. He played two full sets, both taped. Then he sent the audience home, and called his exhausted band back onstage to play some more, for nobody. "Baby Here I Come" is what came out of that. The engineers later dubbed crowd noise onto the empty-hall material. Sources: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/james-brown-live-at-home-with-his-bad-self-album/, https://www.discogs.com/release/14465298-James-Brown-Live-At-Home-With-His-Bad-SelfThe-After-Show
- **"Nineteen years in the vault, six months to a hip-hop record."** Brown shelved the whole Augusta project. "Baby Here I Come" sat unheard until Motherlode in 1988. Within roughly a year, EPMD had lifted its horn stabs for "The Big Payback" on *Unfinished Business* (1989). A track nobody wanted in 1969 was raw material the moment it finally existed. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherlode_(James_Brown_album), https://www.whosampled.com/sample/30975/EPMD-The-Big-Payback-James-Brown-Baby,-Here-I-Come/
- **"The last night of the old band."** Five months later, on March 9, 1970, in Columbus, Georgia, Brown fired nearly the whole orchestra over a pay dispute and hired a teenage Bootsy Collins and the Pacemakers. Six weeks after that they cut "Sex Machine." This recording is a photograph of the band Brown was about to throw away — Maceo, Fred Wesley, Jimmy Nolen, Clyde Stubblefield, Sweet Charles Sherrell — and part of why he shelved it is that by the time it was ready, it sounded like the past. Sources: https://x.com/Bootsy_Collins/status/1900561454555615414, https://ultimateclassicrock.com/james-brown-get-up-sex-machine/, https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/james-brown-live-at-home-with-his-bad-self-album/

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "Baby Here I Come" was not on either of the two public sets Brown played in Augusta that night; it belongs to the private after-show. It opens side A of the 2019 Record Store Day companion LP as "Baby Here I Come—False Start & Complete Take," running 10:46. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/14465298-James-Brown-Live-At-Home-With-His-Bad-SelfThe-After-Show
- Engineers were instructed to dub cheering onto the late-night empty-hall recordings to make them sound like a concert. — Source: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/james-brown-live-at-home-with-his-bad-self-album/
- The track went unreleased for roughly nineteen years, surfacing on the 1988 compilation *Motherlode*, where the US LP notes read: "Compilation of tracks recorded between 1967 & 1973. A1-B3 previously unreleased." — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/655779-James-Brown-Motherlode
- EPMD sampled it on "The Big Payback" (*Unfinished Business*, Fresh, 1989), layering it with samples of Brown's "The Payback" and "Soul Power." The "Baby, Here I Come" element runs from 0:00 and throughout. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/sample/30975/EPMD-The-Big-Payback-James-Brown-Baby,-Here-I-Come/
- Other documented samples: Cassius, "Mister Eveready"; K-Boss, "Strong Island"; 90 MPH, "What a Blessing." — Sources: https://www.whosampled.com/sample/700598/Cassius-Mister-Eveready-James-Brown-Baby,-Here-I-Come/, https://www.whosampled.com/sample/237781/K-Boss-Strong-Island-James-Brown-Baby,-Here-I-Come/, https://www.whosampled.com/sample/410200/90-MPH-What-a-Blessing-James-Brown-Baby,-Here-I-Come/ (WhoSampled blocks automated fetching; a fact checker should open these in a browser to confirm.)
- Sole writing credit: James Brown. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherlode_(James_Brown_album)

#### Album

- *Motherlode* was made as a follow-up to the surprisingly successful 1986 rarities compilation *In the Jungle Groove*, and it landed in the year Brown's life came apart. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherlode_(James_Brown_album)
- Robert Christgau graded it A− in *The Village Voice* on March 14, 1989, and in 2007 called it "the finest of the classic [James Brown] comps." — Sources: http://www.robertchristgau.com/xg/cg/cgv289-89.php, http://www.robertchristgau.com/xg/rs/brown-07.php
- Ratings elsewhere: AllMusic 4/5, eMusic 4.5/5, *Q* 4/5, *The Rolling Stone Album Guide* 4/5. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherlode_(James_Brown_album)
- A 2003 expanded CD added an unedited "Can I Get Some Help," plus "You've Changed" (1969) and an 11:53 alternate mix of "Bodyheat." That expanded edition did not reach vinyl until a 2LP set on March 8, 2019. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherlode_(James_Brown_album), https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/james-browns-complete-expanded-motherlode-rarities-collection-makes-vinyl-debut-with-new-2lp-edition-300781220.html
- Compilation producers: Tim Rogers and Cliff White, with Brown credited as producer of the recordings; liner notes by Cliff White. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/655779-James-Brown-Motherlode

#### Artist

- The October 1, 1969 Bell Auditorium date was a deliberate homecoming: Brown grew up poor in Augusta and had been jailed there as a teenager. He intended the tapes to become a holiday-season live album and the cornerstone of a return to his roots. — Source: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/james-brown-live-at-home-with-his-bad-self-album/
- The album never came out. On March 9, 1970, before a show in Columbus, Georgia, Brown replaced nearly his entire orchestra — Fred Wesley and Maceo Parker included — after complaints about pay and conditions, keeping only drummer John "Jabo" Starks. — Source: https://x.com/Bootsy_Collins/status/1900561454555615414
- He hired the Pacemakers out of Cincinnati, including a 17-year-old bassist named William "Bootsy" Collins and his brother Phelps "Catfish" Collins, renamed them the J.B.'s, and on April 25, 1970 cut "Get Up (I Feel Like Being A) Sex Machine" at Starday-King in Cincinnati. The new sound made the Augusta tapes sound dated, so Brown scrapped them and issued *Sex Machine* instead. — Sources: https://ultimateclassicrock.com/james-brown-get-up-sex-machine/, https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/james-brown-live-at-home-with-his-bad-self-album/
- 1988, the year "Baby Here I Come" finally reached the public, was the year of Brown's PCP-fueled two-state car chase (September 24, 1988) and his conviction. On December 15, 1988 he began a six-year sentence in South Carolina as inmate 155413; he was released in 1991. — Sources: https://time.com/3629041/james-brown/, https://www.upi.com/Archives/1988/12/15/Soul-singer-James-Brown-was-convicted-Thursday-of-two/9805598165200/, https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/december-15/james-brown-begins-his-prison-sentence
- The complete Augusta show was finally released on October 25, 2019 for its 50th anniversary, newly mixed, as *Live At Home With His Bad Self* (Republic/UMe); the after-show set followed as a 5,000-copy RSD Black Friday LP on November 29, 2019. — Sources: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/james-brown-live-at-home-with-his-bad-self-album/, https://www.discogs.com/release/14465298-James-Brown-Live-At-Home-With-His-Bad-SelfThe-After-Show

### Genre Suggestions

The `genre` field is empty. Per `Matter/On Subgenres.md`:

- **Classic Funk (primary recommendation).** Musically this is the 1969 James Brown Orchestra at full strength — the band that wrote the core vocabulary — captured inside the 1969–1977 window the book draws around classic funk. Its identity is funk, not funk fused with anything else. The complication is that "released into the commercial pipeline" does not describe a track that sat in a vault for nineteen years. Source: `Matter/On Subgenres.md`; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherlode_(James_Brown_album)
- **Deep Funk (strong alternative).** The book defines deep funk as "less a sound than a commercial fate... the defining trait of deep funk is the delayed recognition." That is exactly this record: shelved, forgotten, then rediscovered — and rediscovered specifically by hip-hop producers hunting samples, which is the American half of the definition verbatim. The friction is that deep funk usually implies a record that *was* released and overlooked; this one was never released at all. If the author is willing to stretch "overlooked on release" to "never released," Deep Funk tells the truer story. Sources: `Matter/On Subgenres.md`; https://www.whosampled.com/sample/30975/EPMD-The-Big-Payback-James-Brown-Baby,-Here-I-Come/
- **Not recommended: Psychedelic Funk, Jazz Funk, Soul Jazz.** No fit — this is a straight big-band funk vamp with Brown vocals.

The cleanest answer is probably **Classic Funk** with the deep-funk story carried in the prose, since the book already files four other James Brown entries under Classic Funk and consistency across the JB entries has value.

### Region Suggestions

The `region` field was empty; I wrote **`US - Southeast`**, which is a valid value in `Planning/Field Values.md`.

- **US - Southeast (written, recommended).** The performance took place at Bell Auditorium in Augusta, Georgia — Brown's hometown, and the whole point of the session. Georgia has no dedicated region in the enumeration, so `US - Southeast` is the closest valid value. It also matches the four existing James Brown entries and the Bobby Byrd entry, all of which use `US - Southeast`. Source: https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/james-brown-live-at-home-with-his-bad-self-album/
- **US - Ohio (rejected).** Defensible only if one takes the label/business view: King and Starday-King in Cincinnati were where most Brown material of this era was cut and pressed. But this particular recording was not made there, and Polydor released it out of New York. Not a good fit.
- **US - New York (rejected).** Polydor's US operation and the 1988 mixing/editing at PolyGram Studios and Sigma Sound NYC would justify it if the book tagged by release rather than origin. `Matter/On Regions.md` says the choice should "represent the track's origins," which points back to Augusta. Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/7072893-James-Brown-Motherlode

**No case for extending the enumeration.** Georgia is well served by `US - Southeast` here, and Brown's other entries already sit there.
