Donald Byrd was born on December 9, 1932, in Detroit, Michigan. His album *Donald Byrd and 125th Street, N.Y.C.* is a standout late-period jazz-funk album. Released in 1979, it includes some disco tracks, but overall it's a compelling listen. You heard a different side of this album on October 22 with “Morning.”

The album cover is a painting by former NFL player turned artist Ernie Barnes. He also painted covers for Curtis Mayfield and The Crusaders, and one of his “The Sugar Shack” hangs on the apartment wall in the TV show *Good Times*. 

The singer you hear on today’s track, “People Suppose to Be Free,” is not Byrd, but Jim Gilstrap, who handles the album’s vocals. If he sounds familiar, it may be because he also sang the theme to *Good Times*.

The song can be heard as a response to the Rascals’ 1968 #1 hit “People Got to Be Free.” Ten years later, Byrd swaps the demanding “got” for the resigned “suppose,” trading the righteous insistence of the 1960s for the tired resignation of the late 1970s. More tellingly, the social message in the chorus gets undercut by verses that make the freedom question feel personal, even romantic.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

**1. "The title that talks back"**
Byrd and Wakefield named this track "People Suppose to Be Free," an unmistakable echo of The Rascals' 1968 #1 hit "People Got to Be Free." The Rascals' song was written after a threatening encounter with hostile locals in the South and became a four-million-selling anthem for racial tolerance. Byrd's title, eleven years later, feels like a response: people are "supposed" to be free, but are they? The shift from "got" to "suppose" turns an optimistic anthem into something more questioning. This could frame the whole entry.

**2. "The songwriter behind the curtain"**
Kathy Wakefield co-wrote this track with Byrd, and her resume is staggering: she co-wrote The Supremes' "Nathan Jones," the Grammy-winning "One Hundred Ways" (James Ingram/Quincy Jones), Michael Jackson's posthumous hit "Love Never Felt So Good," and The Jacksons' "Torture." Her presence on a jazz-funk deep cut is unexpected and could make for a compelling angle.

**3. "A hard bop giant goes pop"**
This is the second entry from the same album (the first being "Morning" on October 22), so the entry can lean into a different angle. Where "Morning" showcases Clare Fischer's instrumental genius, "People Suppose to Be Free" is a vocal track with lyrics and a message. The contrast between the two tracks from the same record could frame the story of an album (and an artist) that refused to sit in one lane.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- "People Suppose to Be Free" was co-written by Donald Byrd and Kathy Wakefield. The title is a clear echo of The Rascals' 1968 #1 hit "People Got to Be Free," which sold over four million copies and spent five weeks atop the Billboard Hot 100. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_Got_to_Be_Free
- The Rascals' original was inspired by a threatening encounter with hostile locals in Fort Pierce, Florida, when the band's tour vehicle broke down and they were harassed for their long hair. After its release, The Rascals committed to performing only at concerts that also featured African American acts, canceling shows that didn't meet this condition. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_Got_to_Be_Free
- Co-writer Kathy Wakefield has an extraordinary career spanning decades of pop and R&B songwriting. Her credits include The Supremes' "Nathan Jones," James Ingram's Grammy-winning "One Hundred Ways" (produced by Quincy Jones), The Jacksons' "Torture," and Michael Jackson's posthumous hit "Love Never Felt So Good" (which made Jackson the first artist to have a top 10 Billboard Hot 100 single in five different decades). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kathleen_Wakefield; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Love_Never_Felt_So_Good

#### Album
- The album cover features a painting by Ernie Barnes, the former NFL offensive lineman turned painter famous for "The Sugar Shack" (displayed on the wall in the TV show *Good Times*). Barnes also painted covers for Curtis Mayfield's *Something to Believe In* and The Crusaders' *Ghetto Blaster*. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernie_Barnes
- The album was arranged by Wade Marcus, a Motown veteran and Blue Note in-house arranger who also produced The Blackbyrds, and worked with Stevie Wonder, Eddie Kendricks, The Dramatics, and Peaches & Herb. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wade_Marcus
- Vocalist Jim Gilstrap, who sings on this album, is the voice on the opening two lines of Stevie Wonder's "You Are the Sunshine of My Life" and sang co-lead on the theme from the TV series *Good Times*. His solo hit "Swing Your Daddy" charted in 1975. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Gilstrap
- Both Pete Christlieb (saxophone) and Ernie Watts (flute) on this album were members of the Tonight Show Band under Doc Severinsen, where they played together for approximately 20 years. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernie_Watts; https://petechristlieb.com/pete-christlieb-biography/
- Clare Fischer, who plays keyboards and alto saxophone across the album, also composed "Morning" (track 7). Fischer's wider credits include orchestral arrangements for Prince across multiple albums and soundtracks, and three Grammy wins. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clare_Fischer
- The album was produced by Donald Byrd himself, credited as "Blackbyrd Productions Inc." and recorded at The Sound Factory, Hollywood, CA in August-September 1979. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Byrd_and_125th_Street,_N.Y.C.

#### Artist
- Donald Byrd's full birth name was Donaldson Toussaint L'Ouverture Byrd II, named after Haitian revolutionary leader Toussaint L'Ouverture. His father was a Methodist minister. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Byrd
- Byrd mentored a young Herbie Hancock in New York, taking him "under his wing" and advising him to retain his publishing rights rather than surrendering them to Blue Note. That advice paid off enormously when Hancock's "Watermelon Man" became a hit. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Byrd
- At Howard University in 1973, Byrd co-produced The Blackbyrds from his own students, a fusion group that had hits with "Happy Music" and "Walking in Rhythm." *Black Byrd* (1973) became Blue Note's best-selling album for many years. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Byrd
- At North Carolina Central University, Byrd helped establish the first bachelor's degree program in Jazz Studies in North Carolina in 1977. His students included future Cameo bassist Aaron Mills (who later collaborated with OutKast and Cee Lo Green) and keyboardist Chip Crawford (now accompanist for jazz vocalist Gregory Porter). — Source: https://www.ncarts.org/blog/2019/04/12/when-byrd-flew-north-carolina-central-university
- Byrd earned a J.D. in 1976 and a Ph.D. in music education in 1982, in addition to his B.M. from Wayne State University and M.M. from the Manhattan School of Music. He was named an NEA Jazz Master in 2000. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Byrd
- Byrd performed with Lionel Hampton before finishing high school, and his first professional recording was in 1949 at Fortune Records in Detroit. He joined Art Blakey's Jazz Messengers as Clifford Brown's successor while still attending the Manhattan School of Music. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Byrd

The track has prominent vocals, a message-driven lyric, and a commercial sheen courtesy of Wade Marcus's arrangements. Some reviews describe the album's sound as embracing "styles from disco to straight-ahead soul balladry." If the author hears this track leaning more toward the vocal, commercial side of funk rather than the jazz-inflected instrumental side, Classic Funk could work. — Source: https://theseconddisc.com/2017/04/13/love-byrd-big-break-collects-funky-jazz-soul-of-donald-byrds-complete-elektra-years/

**3. Deep Funk**
The album received mixed reviews and never achieved the commercial success of Byrd's Blue Note work or later Elektra hits like "Love Has Come Around." As a vocal jazz-funk track from a record that was largely overlooked, it fits the deep funk profile of delayed recognition through crate-digging. However, the Elektra label and Byrd's name recognition work against a pure deep funk classification. — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/donald-byrd-and-125th-street-nyc-mw0002214265
