### Open Research Questions

The author left no `open:` items in this entry. The questions below are the ones this research raised and could not fully close.

1. **Where is the band actually "from"?** Sources contradict each other badly, and the author should pick a line and stick to it.
	- Wax Poetics: all three members "originated from the Eastern Shore of Maryland before migrating to the tristate area." Source: https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/article/the-skull-snaps-it-s-their-turn/
	- Red Bull Music Academy: Culley and Waters were "known from the D.C.-area soul group the Diplomats." Source: https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2019/02/loop-history-its-a-new-day/
	- Wikipedia: the group "migrated to Newark, New Jersey." Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skull_Snaps
	- Discogs artist profile: "American soul / funk band founded in New York by Ervin Waters and Samm Culley, both originally from Maryland." Source: https://www.discogs.com/artist/48439-Skull-Snaps
	- The Urban Daily: "a funk band from the Bronx." Source: https://theurbandaily.com/610022/sunday-soul-sample-its-a-new-day/
	- **Best resolution:** Maryland-born, Newark/NYC-based, New Jersey-recorded. The Bronx claim appears to be an outlier and is uncorroborated elsewhere; treat it as unreliable.
2. **Exact album release date in 1973.** Discogs gives only "1973." A secondary aggregator surfaced "February 1973," but that value could not be confirmed against a primary source (Billboard listings, trade ads, or label documentation), so it is not recorded here as fact. Approach taken: Discogs release and master JSON, Wikipedia, AllMusic, RateYourMusic (403 to fetch), Billboard-targeted web searches. All returned year only.
3. **Was Ed Stasium a band member or the engineer?** Wikipedia's infobox lists Ed Stasium among the members alongside Waters, Bragg and Culley (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skull_Snaps), and Discogs mirrors this in its members list (https://www.discogs.com/artist/48439-Skull-Snaps). But the release credits on Discogs list him strictly as "Engineer" (https://www.discogs.com/release/560501-Skull-Snaps-Skull-Snaps), and every long-form feature — Wax Poetics, The Vinyl Factory, Red Bull — describes Skull Snaps as a **trio** with Stasium as the young trainee engineer. **Best resolution: Stasium was the engineer, not a member.** The Wikipedia/Discogs member listing is almost certainly a data error propagated between the two.
4. **Birth dates for Culley, Waters and Bragg.** Not found in any source. Bragg's death date (June 18, 2007) is documented; birth dates are not. Approach taken: Wikipedia, Discogs artist pages, Wax Poetics, The Vinyl Factory, Red Bull, obituary searches on Legacy.com. Recorded as unresolved.
5. **Chart performance of the "My Hang Up Is You" / "It's a New Day" single.** No Billboard R&B or Hot 100 entry could be found. Absence of chart data is consistent with the narrative that GSF folded almost immediately and the record vanished. Documented as: no evidence of any chart placement.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The two bars that ate hip-hop."** The entry's most obvious hook: a three-minute soul-funk album cut whose opening two-measure drum figure went on to be sampled roughly 500 times, while the men who played it got nothing. The angle lets you write about how value in recorded music migrated from the song to the fragment.
2. **"Anonymity as strategy."** The record has no band photo — just a skull. Samm Culley says that was deliberate: "black bands weren't getting a lot of play" in 1973, so they built a cover a rock fan would pick up blind (https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/article/the-skull-snaps-it-s-their-turn/, https://www.thevinylfactory.com/features/untold-story-behind-one-of-the-most-sampled-breaks-in-history). The camouflage worked so well that for twenty years nobody knew who Skull Snaps were — including the producers looping them.
3. **"It was a warm-up."** "It's a New Day" started as the band's soundcheck routine, the thing they played to get loose before the real work began (https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/article/the-skull-snaps-it-s-their-turn/). The most consequential funk recording of the group's life was the throat-clearing. A good frame for how funk's canon gets made by accident.
4. **"Deep funk isn't a sound, it's a fate."** The book's own definition of deep funk is delayed recognition. Skull Snaps is the purest possible case study: one album, one day of recording, a label that folded, fifteen years of silence, then a Connecticut crate-digger named Dooley-O.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. The first two bars are the most sampled thing you will hear this year — roughly 500 records are built on them. Listen once for the break. Then start the track again and listen to everything *after* it. What kind of song did they think they were making?
2. Ed Stasium said of the drum intro, "There's no more room for anything else on that drum intro; everything is maxed" (https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/article/the-skull-snaps-it-s-their-turn/). Listen for the compression — the way the room collapses in and springs back between hits. Is that a drummer, or is that an engineer?
3. This is three men — bass, guitar, drums — and nothing else. Count the instruments as you listen and see if you believe it. Where does your ear insist there must be a fourth player?
4. The whole album was cut in a single day, one take per song. Listen for the moment where you can hear that: a slightly late entrance, an unrepaired edge. Does it hurt the record or make it?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "It's a New Day" started life as the band's live warm-up routine, not a composition. Samm Culley: "George would play that beat... and then I'd start to play on bass, and Erv would start to play on guitar." — Source: https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/article/the-skull-snaps-it-s-their-turn/
- The track was the **B-side**. GSF 6891 led with "My Hang Up Is You"; "It's a New Day" was flipped to the back. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/2304935-Skull-Snaps-My-Hang-Up-Is-You-Its-A-New-Day
- Discogs credits the writing to "Ineffie Woods" (label copy reads "I. Woods") — a name that appears nowhere else in the band's story and is not one of the three members. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/master/153860-Skull-Snaps-Skull-Snaps
- The break was found by Connecticut rapper Dooley-O (Allen Jackson) in 1988; his cousin Stezo put it on wax first with "It's My Turn" (1989), engineered by Paul C. That was the first commercial release of what became roughly **500** uses. — Sources: https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2019/02/loop-history-its-a-new-day/ and https://www.whosampled.com/sample/2430/Stezo-It's-My-Turn-Skull-Snaps-It's-a-New-Day/
- Many later producers sampled the break **from Stezo's record rather than from Skull Snaps** — a copy of a copy that became the standard. — Source: https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2019/02/loop-history-its-a-new-day/
- The list of records built on those two bars spans genres and decades: Eric B. & Rakim, Gang Starr ("Take It Personal"), Das EFX, Ol' Dirty Bastard ("Hippa to da Hoppa"), Digable Planets, Lords of the Underground ("Funky Child"), Camp Lo ("Cooley High"), Diamond D, The Pharcyde, Onyx, DJ Shadow, The Prodigy ("Poison"), Panjabi MC, and Linkin Park. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skull_Snaps, https://www.whosampled.com/Skull-Snaps/It's-a-New-Day/, https://www.whosampled.com/sample/397/The-Prodigy-Poison-Skull-Snaps-It's-a-New-Day/
- Engineer Ed Stasium on the density of the sound: "There's no more room for anything else on that drum intro; everything is maxed." The drum tone came from a Teletronix LA-2A limiter and a Pultec EQP equalizer. — Source: https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/article/the-skull-snaps-it-s-their-turn/
- The band re-released the track themselves in 2006 as *It's a New Day Redux*, a 12" of remixes pairing Skull Snaps with Underdog, Steinski, DiscoLobos and Audible Doctor. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/768086-Skull-Snaps-Its-A-New-Day-Redux

#### Album

- The entire nine-track LP was recorded **in one day**, essentially one take per song, at Venture Sound Studios in New Jersey (Tony Camillo's room, in Somerville) in early 1973. — Sources: https://www.thevinylfactory.com/features/untold-story-behind-one-of-the-most-sampled-breaks-in-history and https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/article/the-skull-snaps-it-s-their-turn/
- The album cover shows a skull and no band photograph — a deliberate act of racial camouflage. Culley: "If you went into a record shop and saw that album... you're a rock fan, you'd say, 'Damn, let me take that home.'" — Source: https://www.thevinylfactory.com/features/untold-story-behind-one-of-the-most-sampled-breaks-in-history
- Personnel and credits from the original pressing (GSF-S-1011): produced by George Kerr, arranged by Bert Keyes and the group, engineered by Ed Stasium with assistant David Domanich, **executive producer Lloyd Price**. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/560501-Skull-Snaps-Skull-Snaps
- A 1995 CD reissue on Charly Records removed the band's credits entirely, further severing the group from its own record. — Source: https://www.thevinylfactory.com/features/untold-story-behind-one-of-the-most-sampled-breaks-in-history
- The 2005/2006 CD reissue added five bonus tracks: "Al's Razor Blade," "Ain't That Lovin' You," "On Top of It," "Soul Makossa," and "She's the One." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skull_Snaps
- AllMusic rates the album 3 out of 5. — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/skull-snaps-mw0000940801 (also https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skull_Snaps)

#### Artist

- Skull Snaps were previously **The Diplomats**, an R&B close-harmony group that released eleven singles between 1963 and 1970 and played the Apollo and Carnegie Hall. They also worked briefly as Soul Three. — Sources: https://www.thevinylfactory.com/features/untold-story-behind-one-of-the-most-sampled-breaks-in-history and https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/article/the-skull-snaps-it-s-their-turn/
- The name came from a fan. Culley: "You guys do more than just blow our minds, you snap our skulls." — Source: https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/article/the-skull-snaps-it-s-their-turn/
- They were a **trio**: Samm Culley (bass, percussion, vocals), Ervan Waters (guitar, vocals), George "Buzzy" Bragg (drums, vocals). Their signature was making three men sound like a full band. — Sources: https://www.discogs.com/release/560501-Skull-Snaps-Skull-Snaps and https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2019/02/loop-history-its-a-new-day/
- Red Bull reports that Bragg had previously played sessions with **Jimi Hendrix**. No corroborating source was found for this claim, and it should be handled carefully. — Source: https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2019/02/loop-history-its-a-new-day/
- Immediately after the Skull Snaps sessions, the same group cut a cover of Manu Dibango's "Soul Makossa" for Buddah under yet another name, **All Dyrections** — which Discogs lists as an official alias. — Sources: https://www.discogs.com/artist/48439-Skull-Snaps and https://www.discogs.com/artist/387106-All-Dyrections
- GSF Records was founded in 1972 in New York, headed by former ABC Records president Larry Newton with **Lloyd Price** as head of A&R. It lasted under two years and folded shortly after the Skull Snaps LP came out, which is a large part of why the record disappeared. — Sources: http://www.cvinyl.com/labelguides/gsf.php and https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/article/the-skull-snaps-it-s-their-turn/
- Ed Stasium, the trainee engineer on this session, went on to engineer Talking Heads' *Talking Heads: 77* and the Ramones' *Leave Home* (both 1977) and to produce Living Colour's *Vivid*. He also engineered Gladys Knight & the Pips' "Midnight Train to Georgia." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Stasium
- The band received **no royalties** from the sampling boom and remained anonymous until their 2005–06 reunion on Ten12 Records, which brought back Erv Waters, Sam Culley and George Bragg. — Sources: https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/article/the-skull-snaps-it-s-their-turn/ and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skull_Snaps
- George "Buzzy" Bragg — the man who played the break — died on **June 18, 2007**, a year after the reunion. — Source: https://theurbandaily.com/610022/sunday-soul-sample-its-a-new-day/

### Genre Suggestions

The `genre` field is currently empty. Book definitions are from `Matter/On Subgenres.md`.

1. **Deep Funk — strongest recommendation.** The book defines deep funk as "less a sound than a commercial fate": overlooked on release, found later by crate-digging DJs hunting samples, often on small-label records with limited distribution, with delayed recognition as the defining trait. Skull Snaps satisfies every clause. GSF was a tiny label that folded within two years of founding (http://www.cvinyl.com/labelguides/gsf.php); the album sank without a chart entry; it was rediscovered by a Connecticut digger in 1988 and became one of the most sampled records in hip-hop (https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2019/02/loop-history-its-a-new-day/). If any entry in this book is deep funk, it is this one.
2. **Classic Funk — defensible, but a weaker fit.** The 1973 date sits squarely inside the book's 1969–1977 classic window, and the track's primary identity is funk rather than funk fused with another genre. But the book's classic-funk definition specifically requires "the commercial pipeline of major labels, well-distributed singles and albums, radio play, concert tours, and chart presence," and emphasizes that the point is "not on later rediscovery." Skull Snaps had none of the former and is entirely defined by the latter. Recommend against, unless the author wants to make the point that the record *sounds* like classic funk while its life story is the opposite.
3. **Psychedelic Funk — mentioned only to dismiss.** The skull artwork invites the association, but the music has nothing psychedelic about it: no surreal lyrics, no alter egos, no invented worlds. The cover was commercial camouflage aimed at rock buyers (https://www.thevinylfactory.com/features/untold-story-behind-one-of-the-most-sampled-breaks-in-history), not a statement of style. Do not use.

**Suggested value: `Deep Funk`.**

### Region Suggestions

The `region` field was empty and has been set to `US - Northeast`, a value that appears verbatim in `Planning/Field Values.md`.

The complication is that the sources disagree (see Open Research Questions #1). Weighing them:

1. **`US - Northeast` — recommended and applied.** New Jersey is where the record actually happened. Wikipedia has the group migrating to Newark (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skull_Snaps), and the album was cut in a single day at Venture Sound Studios in Somerville, NJ (https://www.thevinylfactory.com/features/untold-story-behind-one-of-the-most-sampled-breaks-in-history, https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/article/the-skull-snaps-it-s-their-turn/). New Jersey has no dedicated entry in the enumeration, so `US - Northeast` is the correct closed-list value. It also has the virtue of covering the broader tristate reality of the band's working life.
2. **`US - New York` — the serious alternative.** Discogs describes the band as "founded in New York" (https://www.discogs.com/artist/48439-Skull-Snaps), GSF Records was a New York label (http://www.cvinyl.com/labelguides/gsf.php), and one source places the band in the Bronx (https://theurbandaily.com/610022/sunday-soul-sample-its-a-new-day/). If the author's convention is to tag by label city rather than studio, this is the value to use. The Bronx claim is uncorroborated and should not carry weight on its own.
3. **`US - Southeast` — origin-of-the-musicians reading.** Wax Poetics states all three members came from Maryland's Eastern Shore (https://magazine.waxpoetics.com/article/the-skull-snaps-it-s-their-turn/), and Discogs confirms Waters and Culley as "originally from Maryland." Red Bull instead places the Diplomats in the D.C. area. Only recommended if the author tags by birthplace, which `Matter/On Regions.md` suggests is one defensible option among several. Note that Maryland sits awkwardly across the Southeast/Northeast boundary in any case.

**No case for extending the enumeration.** New Jersey is genuinely absent from `Planning/Field Values.md`, but `US - Northeast` covers it cleanly and the state does not have a distinct enough funk identity in this book to warrant its own entry the way Michigan or Minnesota do. Flagging it only so the author knows the specificity was lost on purpose.
