This song exists because Lonnie Jordan literally spilled his wine on the mixing console. The spill forced the band to record in another studio, and the chaos provided content for the track’s loose, half-spoken vocal. If singer Eric Burdon sounds like he is talking his way through a dream, it may be because he was asleep on the studio floor and woke up, then sang on the take that made the record. The result was so improvised that the whole band received a writing credit.

Burdon wanted "Magic Mountain" as the A-side of the single. Although the song is about Mount Tamalpais in California, some radio stations misread the title as a drug reference and would not play it. “Spill the Wine” got the airplay instead, becoming War’s first hit and Burdon’s last Top 10 hit.

Today, we remember Eric Burdon as the iconic frontman for the Animals and War as a major funk band, each leaving a distinct mark on popular music. Their two records together were an unexpected but perfect collaboration. Burdon was the British Invasion star trying to build a post-Animals solo life in America, and War was a recently reconstituted band operating as a backing unit for hire. Their three-day recording session, from January 2 through 4, 1970, is the moment their two stories overlapped and became a breakout record.

### Open Research Questions

**Resolution (2026-03-27):** All claims reviewed against sources. Found three issues requiring author attention and one minor phrasing suggestion:

**1. ⚠️ "he was asleep on the studio floor until Jerry Goldstein hung a microphone over him and woke him to sing the take that made the record"**
This reverses the sequence. Sources (Medium/The Riff, corroborated by multiple retellings) say Burdon "stretched out on the floor for a short nap but *broke into song*," and *then* Goldstein "brought over a microphone" to capture it. Burdon wasn't woken up to sing; he started singing spontaneously and Goldstein rushed to record it. Suggested rewrite: "...he was napping on the studio floor when he broke into song and Jerry Goldstein rushed a microphone over to capture the take that made the record."
- Source: https://medium.com/the-riff/eric-burdons-baffling-spill-the-wine-explained-1280d17d805c

**2. ⚠️ "The B-side, 'Spill the Wine,' got the airplay instead"**
On every known pressing (Discogs, Wikipedia), "Spill the Wine" is the A-side and "Magic Mountain" is the B-side. The story is that Burdon *preferred* "Magic Mountain" and wanted it to be the focus, but when radio wouldn't touch it, "Spill the Wine" became the promoted track. It was never released as a B-side. Suggested rewrite: something like "Radio ignored 'Magic Mountain,' so 'Spill the Wine' became the hit instead."
- Source: https://www.discogs.com/master/116932-Eric-Burdon-And-War-Spill-The-Wine; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spill_the_Wine

**3. ⚠️ "the first of their three records together"**
Needs qualification. Burdon and War made two original studio albums during their active partnership: *Eric Burdon Declares "War"* (1970) and *The Black-Man's Burdon* (1970). A third album, *Love Is All Around*, was released by ABC Records in 1976 from previously unreleased recordings made during the 1969-1971 sessions, years after Burdon had left the band. Wikipedia's article on *The Black-Man's Burdon* calls it "the last album by the group before Burdon left." Saying "three records" is technically defensible but implies an active three-album partnership that didn't exist. Consider "the first of their two studio albums" or, if you want to include *Love Is All Around*, clarify its nature.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Burdon_Declares_%22War%22; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Burdon (lists three albums); https://www.allmusic.com/album/love-is-all-around-mw0000618540

**4. Minor: "War was a newly formed band"**
Not exactly wrong but slightly misleading. The musicians had been playing together since 1962 as the Creators, then Nightshift. They were renamed War when they partnered with Burdon in 1969. "Newly renamed" or "recently reconstituted" would be more precise.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The accident that launched a band."** The entire song exists because Lonnie Jordan spilled wine on a mixing console, forcing the band into a different studio where the track clicked. That origin story, plus the fact that Burdon was napping on the floor when Jerry Goldstein put a mic over him, frames the entry around creative accidents and the way a song can emerge from chaos rather than planning.

2. **"A British voice inside a California funk band."** Burdon was a working-class kid from Newcastle who had fronted the Animals. War was a multiracial Long Beach collective that fused funk, Latin, jazz, and rock. The collision of a British Invasion singer with a band that sounded like the streets of Southern California is a striking angle, especially since Burdon called this his "homage to Mexico."

3. **"The song that was never supposed to be the single."** Burdon intended "Magic Mountain" (about Mount Tamalpais) as the A-side. Radio stations misread the title as a drug reference and refused to play it. "Spill the Wine" was bumped to the A-side by default and became Burdon's last Top 10 hit. The entry could explore how a reluctant single became a career-defining moment.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- The song was never intended to be the A-side single. "Magic Mountain," about Mount Tamalpais, was planned as the lead track, but radio programmers assumed the title was a drug reference and refused to play it. "Spill the Wine" was promoted instead by default. — Source: https://medium.com/the-riff/eric-burdons-baffling-spill-the-wine-explained-1280d17d805c
- The title came from a literal accident: Lonnie Jordan spilled wine on a mixing console at Wally Heider Studios in San Francisco, forcing the band to relocate to another studio, where the track came together. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spill_the_Wine
- Burdon recorded his vocal while lying on the floor during a lunch break. Most of the band had left; producer Jerry Goldstein quickly set up a microphone and Lonnie Jordan played backing keys. The rest of the instrumentation was overdubbed later. — Source: https://medium.com/the-riff/eric-burdons-baffling-spill-the-wine-explained-1280d17d805c
- The sound of a woman speaking Spanish in the background is a friend of Eric Burdon's, adding an unplanned texture to the recording. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spill_the_Wine
- The track hit #1 on Cash Box magazine's Top 100, though it peaked at #3 on the Billboard Hot 100. Billboard ranked it the #20 single of 1970. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spill_the_Wine
- The track received RIAA Gold certification (1,000,000 units). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spill_the_Wine
- An edited radio version omits the middle spoken recitation and one chorus. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spill_the_Wine
- In 2008, Lonnie Jordan called Eric Burdon "the first Latin rapper in pop music" in reference to this track's spoken delivery style. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spill_the_Wine
- Burdon later said the song was "my homage to Mexico (maybe Mexican women) and the feeling I got when I was there. It was Old World Mexico, nothing to do with the Americanized version now." — Source: https://medium.com/the-riff/eric-burdons-baffling-spill-the-wine-explained-1280d17d805c
- The song inspired backstage rituals: promoters began leaving California wine bottles backstage for the band, and they developed a technique for opening bottles without corkscrews. — Source: https://medium.com/the-riff/eric-burdons-baffling-spill-the-wine-explained-1280d17d805c
- De La Soul sampled "Spill the Wine" for "Potholes in My Lawn" (1989). — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Eric-Burdon/Spill-the-Wine/
- Notable cover versions include The Isley Brothers (1971), Michael Hutchence (1996), and a nine-minute live version by Bruce Springsteen & the E Street Band in 2014 at Hope Estate Winery, Hunter Valley, Australia. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spill_the_Wine
- Featured in the films Boogie Nights (1997), Deuce Bigalow: Male Gigolo (1999), and Remember the Titans (2000). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spill_the_Wine
- The B-side "Magic Mountain" was used in the MGM film The Magic Garden of Stanley Sweetheart. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/11059741-Eric-Burdon-And-War-Spill-The-Wine-Magic-Mountain

Goldstein called Burdon the next morning and dragged him to the club that night. ### Genre Suggestions

1. **Classic Funk** (current value) — Strong fit. "Spill the Wine" was released in 1970, squarely within the 1968-1977 classic era. It was a major commercial hit (#3 Billboard, #1 Cash Box, Gold certification), distributed through MGM Records, and received heavy radio play. The groove is unmistakably funk-forward. This is the most defensible choice.
   - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spill_the_Wine

2. **Funk Rock** — Also a strong case. AllMusic classifies the album under "Funk, Latin Rock, Rock & Roll" among other styles. Eric Burdon came from rock (The Animals), and the track blends rock sensibility with funk groove. War is frequently described as "funk rock" in music press. The guitar work and Burdon's rock-singer delivery lean into this territory.
