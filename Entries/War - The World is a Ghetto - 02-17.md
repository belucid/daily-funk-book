---
Per drummer Harold Brown, the idea for today’s song, “The World Is a Ghetto,” came when the band realized that poverty was not confined to literal ghettos. Even the wealthiest enclaves carry their own kind of isolation. Percussionist Papa Dee Allen wrote the resulting song as an argument for unity, not as a complaint from an underclass. It was a powerful message in 1973, and it resonates even more today.

War’s *The World Is a Ghetto* outsold Stevie Wonder’s *Talking Book*, Carly Simon’s *No Secrets*, Seals & Crofts’ *Summer Breeze*, and every other album on *Billboard’s* 1973 year-end chart. The album reached #1 on February 17, 1973, despite the title track being a slow, mournful, ten-minute groove. It was cut down to four minutes for the radio single, but the somber tone remained. The single peaked at #7 on the Billboard Hot 100 and #3 on the Hot R&B Songs chart.

*Rolling Stone* later ranked the album #444 on the 500 Greatest Albums list.

**Q: When exactly was the single released, and when did it enter the Hot 100?**

**A:** Wikipedia's article on the single states it was released in October 1972, while Billboard's own retrospective confirms it entered the Hot 100 the week of November 12, 1972 (16 weeks on chart). ### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"Ten minutes against four"** — The single edit comes in under four minutes; the album version stretches past ten with an extended guitar outro, a saxophone solo, and additional verses. That gap between radio-ready and band-ready is the whole story of why War's records reward deep listening. This frames the entry as a case study in how an album track can be a wholly different animal than its hit single.

2. **"Everybody's neighborhood is a ghetto"** — Per drummer Harold Brown, the song's idea came when the band realized wealthy enclaves carry their own kind of isolation. Papa Dee Allen wrote it as a unity argument, not a complaint. Framing the entry around the inversion of "ghetto" from place-name to universal condition gives the author room to talk about the song's social vision without turning the entry into a sociology lecture.

3. **"Best-selling album of 1973, hardly anyone remembers"** — *The World Is a Ghetto* outsold *Talking Book*, *No Secrets*, and every other 1973 release. Its title track is a slow, mournful, ten-minute groove. Why has the album largely faded from the canonical conversation while shorter, hookier records from the same year stayed in heavy rotation? A theme that invites the author to argue for the album's rediscovery.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. "The single edit of this song is under four minutes. You're hearing the ten-minute album version. Where would you cut it to make a hit? What would you lose?"

2. "Howard Scott called his wah-wah intro his most distinctive guitar moment. Listen to how the rest of the band enters around it — who arrives first, who hangs back, and how long it takes before all seven members are in the room together."

3. "Try to hear the song as a conversation between Charles Miller's saxophone and Lee Oskar's harmonica. Two horns that shouldn't share a bandstand, trading the melody. Where does one hand it off to the other?"

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- Papa Dee Allen, the band's percussionist (not a primary vocalist or guitarist), conceived the song. Drummer Harold Brown said the philosophical seed was the band noticing that wealthy people in affluent neighborhoods felt their own kind of isolation, leading to "the world is a ghetto." — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/war/the-world-is-a-ghetto
- Guitarist Howard Scott considered his wah-wah-pedal intro his most distinctive guitar moment in the War catalog. — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/war/the-world-is-a-ghetto
- The single edit ran under four minutes; the album version is 10:10, with an extended guitar outro, a saxophone solo, and additional verses cut from the single. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_Is_a_Ghetto_(War_song)
- The single peaked at #7 on the Billboard Hot 100 and #3 on the Hot R&B/Hip-Hop Songs chart, and was certified Gold. Its B-side was the album's other long jam, "Four Cornered Room." — Source: https://www.billboard.com/music/rb-hip-hop/war-the-world-is-a-ghetto-billboard-chart-1235551303/
- The song has been covered more than 10 times and sampled in more than 30 songs. Notable covers: George Benson (1977), Ahmad Jamal (1973), James Moody, Will Downing (1991, charted in UK), and a 2012 version by BJ the Chicago Kid featuring Kendrick Lamar. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/War/The-World-Is-a-Ghetto/
- The Geto Boys sampled War's recording for their 1996 single of the same name, which became the group's last charting single. Method Man and A$AP Mob sampled it on "Trillmatic" (2013). Janet Jackson used a sample on "You" (1997). — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_Is_a_Ghetto_(Geto_Boys_song) and https://www.whosampled.com/War/The-World-Is-a-Ghetto/
- Daft Punk's Thomas Bangalter sampled George Benson's cover (not War's original) for his 1996 single "Spinal Scratch." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_Is_a_Ghetto_(War_song)

#### Album

- Recorded at Crystal Industries in Los Angeles in 1972. Producer Jerry Goldstein said the album was built by recording the band every time they jammed and then carving 35-to-40-minute albums out of the takes. Only 29 days passed between the first note and mastering completion. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_Is_a_Ghetto and https://war.com/war-declares-50th-anniversary-celebration-of-the-world-is-a-ghetto-we-shot-rhythms-and-harmonies/
- "The Cisco Kid," the album's other big single, took 36 takes before the band locked it. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cisco_Kid_(song)
- Lonnie Jordan, summing up the album in 2023: "We were waging war against war, but we didn't shoot bullets. We shot rhythms and harmonies." — Source: https://war.com/war-declares-50th-anniversary-celebration-of-the-world-is-a-ghetto-we-shot-rhythms-and-harmonies/
- The album hit #1 on the Billboard 200 on February 17, 1973, and was Billboard's best-selling album of all of 1973, outselling Seals & Crofts' *Summer Breeze*, Stevie Wonder's *Talking Book*, and Carly Simon's *No Secrets*. — Sources: https://number1albums.com/the-world-is-a-ghetto-war-february-17-1973/ and https://www.billboard.com/music/rb-hip-hop/war-the-world-is-a-ghetto-billboard-chart-1235551303/
- Rolling Stone ranked it #444 on the original 500 Greatest Albums list. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_Is_a_Ghetto
- Lee Oskar received credit for the album concept. The cover art, by Howard Miller, shows a Rolls-Royce with a flat tire in a ghetto setting, rendered in blue and black tones. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_Is_a_Ghetto
- A 50th Anniversary Collector's Edition was released on Record Store Day Black Friday (November 24, 2023) as a limited 4,000-copy box set on Rhino, including six previously unreleased session tracks and three LPs of "making of" material. — Source: https://war.com/the-world-is-a-ghetto-50th-anniversary-collectors-edition/

#### Artist

- War's lineup at the time of this recording was famously multi-ethnic: Black, Mexican-American, and Danish (Lee Oskar, born in Copenhagen), reflecting the diverse neighborhoods of South LA. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)
- Every member of War played percussion in addition to their primary instrument, creating their signature dense rhythmic bed. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)
- War's last gig with Eric Burdon was at Ronnie Scott's Club in London on September 16, 1970 — also the last public performance by Jimi Hendrix, who sat in. Burdon left soon after; *The World Is a Ghetto* was the band's third album as a self-led group. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)
- War's music has been sampled so extensively in hip-hop that a 1992 compilation, *Rap Declares War*, was issued to document it. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)
- War received a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame on June 5, 2025. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)

### Genre Suggestions

1. **Classic Funk (current value)** — Strong fit. The track was issued on a major label (United Artists), got heavy radio play, peaked at #7 pop and #3 R&B, and lived inside the best-selling album of 1973. By every measure (commercial pipeline, era, primary musical identity), it sits squarely in this book's definition of classic funk. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_Is_a_Ghetto

2. **Latin Funk** — Reasonable secondary tag. Papa Dee Allen and Harold Brown's percussion layers (congas, timbales) plus the broader War aesthetic put the band squarely in funk-with-Latin-textures territory. However, the title track itself is the album's most ballad-like, jazz-leaning moment, with less foregrounded Latin percussion than "The Cisco Kid" or "City, Country, City." Latin Funk is more defensible for the album overall than for this specific track. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)

3. **Jazz-Funk** — Worth considering. The album version stretches past ten minutes, leans on a saxophone solo from Charles Miller and a harmonica solo from Lee Oskar, and structurally resembles the long-form jazz-funk jams of the era more than a typical funk single. The book's definition emphasizes "longer track lengths, room for extended improvisation, and a rhythm section built to support soloists" — all true here. The counter-argument: the players are not jazz-trained crossovers; they're a funk band stretching out. Classic Funk wins, but Jazz-Funk is the more interesting reframe if the author wants one.

### Region Suggestions

1. **US - California (set)** — Strong, defensible choice. War formed in Long Beach in 1969 and recorded the album in Los Angeles. Long Beach is the band's spiritual home; the album cover even nods to LA neighborhood imagery. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_(band)

2. **US - West** — Available alternative if the author wants to keep California tags scarce. War's sound did absorb the broader Western funk scene (Sly & the Family Stone in SF, Tower of Power in Oakland, the LA studio scene). But there's no upside to going broader when California works.

The current list has no missing region needed here. US - California is the right call.
