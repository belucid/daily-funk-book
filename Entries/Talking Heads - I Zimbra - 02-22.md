Today’s song, "I Zimbra," is a surprise. Talking Heads, the kings of art-punk new wave, find their way into a funk book.

The track doesn't fit into the usual funk mold, and it’s not just the band. There’s a disco feel from the four-on-the-floor kick drum. However, drummer Chris Frantz limits himself to bass drum and hi-hat, while congas, djembe, and talking drum fill in the space with dense, African-influenced polyrhythms. It's funk, just filtered through an avant-garde lens.

The lyrics are intentional nonsense. They are taken from a 1916 Dadaist sound poem, "Gadji beri bimba," by German poet Hugo Ball. Born on February 22, 1886, Ball first performed the poem at Cabaret Voltaire in Zurich in 1916. More than sixty years later, Brian Eno suggested that the band chant the poem, earning the dead poet a songwriter credit alongside Eno and David Byrne.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"When a dead poet fronts a funk band"** — Hugo Ball died in 1927, but his nonsense poem "Gadji beri bimba" became the vocal hook for a funk track 52 years later. The entry could frame how Dada's anti-meaning project accidentally produced something irresistibly physical and danceable. The tension between the poem's intent to destroy language and the track's intent to move bodies is a rich angle.

2. **"The conga player from Central Park"** — David Byrne found percussionist Gene Wilder (not the actor) playing congas in Central Park and brought him into the studio. The entry could use that story as a way into the track's larger theme: a collision of found sounds, found words, and found musicians producing something nobody planned.

3. **"The song that rewired Talking Heads"** — Jerry Harrison called "I Zimbra" the seed that grew into Remain in Light, their masterpiece. The entry could position this track as the hinge point where an art-punk band became a polyrhythmic funk machine, and the moment Brian Eno's influence fully took hold.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- The lyrics are adapted from "Gadji beri bimba," a sound poem by German Dadaist Hugo Ball, first performed at the Cabaret Voltaire in Zurich on July 14, 1916 (Bastille Day). Ball wore a cardboard costume of blue, scarlet, and gold and was carried to the stage in darkness. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Ball
- Brian Eno suggested the band chant Ball's poem over the African-influenced rhythm track they had built. Eno, Byrne, and Ball are all credited as songwriters. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Zimbra
- Chris Frantz's drumming on the track is minimal: he uses only his bass drum and hi-hat, leaving the rhythmic complexity to the hand percussion. — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/talking-heads/i-zimbra (via web search summary)
- The conga player credited as "Gene Wilder" (not the actor) was discovered by David Byrne playing in Central Park and invited to the session. — Source: https://www.songfacts.com/facts/talking-heads/i-zimbra (via web search summary)
- Robert Fripp of King Crimson plays guitar on the track. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Zimbra
- Assistant audio engineer Julie Last provided backing vocals/chanting on the track. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Zimbra
- Additional percussionists include Ari on congas, Hassam Ramzy on surdo, Abdou M'Boup on djembe and talking drum, and Assane Thiam on percussion. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Zimbra
- The track charted at #28 on the U.S. Billboard Disco Top 100. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Zimbra
- "I Zimbra" was featured in the opening scene of Spider-Man: No Way Home (2021), which gave the song a massive spike in popularity with a new generation. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Zimbra
- David Byrne performs the song in his Broadway show and film American Utopia (2020), directed by Spike Lee. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Zimbra
- The track was cut from the theatrical release of Stop Making Sense (1984) but restored for the 1999 DVD release. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Zimbra

#### Album
- Fear of Music was released August 3, 1979 on Sire Records (catalog number SRK 6076). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear_of_Music
- Basic tracks were recorded on just two days, April 22 and May 6, 1979, at Chris Frantz and Tina Weymouth's loft in Long Island City, Queens. A Record Plant Mobile Studio van parked outside and ran cables through their loft window. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear_of_Music
- Overdubs were done at the Hit Factory, Atlantic Studios, RPM Sound Studios, and Record Plant in New York City. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear_of_Music
- The album reached #21 on the U.S. Billboard 200, #33 on the UK Albums Chart. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear_of_Music
- Named best album of 1979 by NME, Melody Maker, and the Los Angeles Times. Pitchfork later rated it 10/10. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear_of_Music
- The original vinyl sleeve was embossed to resemble a New York City manhole cover. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/116489-Talking-Heads-Fear-Of-Music (via web search summary)

#### Artist
- Talking Heads formed in 1975. David Byrne, Chris Frantz, and Tina Weymouth met as freshmen at the Rhode Island School of Design (RISD). They moved to New York and played their first gig opening for the Ramones at CBGB on June 5, 1975. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talking_Heads
- Jerry Harrison (keyboards, guitar) joined afterward, having previously been a member of Jonathan Richman's Modern Lovers. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talking_Heads
- Jerry Harrison named "I Zimbra" as his favorite Talking Heads song and stated: "We also knew that our next album would be a further exploration of what we had begun with 'I Zimbra.'" That next album was Remain in Light (1980). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Zimbra
- Chris Frantz and Tina Weymouth are married; their loft where Fear of Music was recorded was their shared home. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear_of_Music

### Genre Suggestions

The `genre` field is currently set to `Avant-Funk`.

1. **Avant-Funk (current)** — A reasonable fit. The book's genre definitions note Avant-Funk but don't provide a full definition. "I Zimbra" blends art-school conceptualism (Dadaist lyrics, Brian Eno's production, Robert Fripp's guitar) with a dense polyrhythmic groove. It's funk, but filtered through an avant-garde sensibility. The track doesn't fit neatly into any conventional funk subgenre.

2. **Afrobeat** — The track draws heavily on West African polyrhythm and was directly influenced by the African popular music Byrne was listening to. Wikipedia lists Afrobeat as one of the song's genres. The percussion lineup (djembe, talking drum, surdo, congas) and the layered, cyclical structure echo Afrobeat conventions. However, the book's Afrobeat definition centers on "funk rooted in Africa," and Talking Heads are a New York band, which makes this a weaker fit for the book's taxonomy.

3. **Funk Rock** — Talking Heads came from the CBGB punk/new wave scene and used rock instrumentation. But the track's rhythm section is built on hand percussion and polyrhythm rather than rock drumming, so this feels like a stretch.

**Recommendation:** Avant-Funk is the strongest choice given the book's definitions. The track is too conceptually unusual to slot into Classic Funk or Afrobeat as defined in this project.
