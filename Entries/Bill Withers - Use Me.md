Bill Withers assembled toilets for Boeing 747s by day and wrote songs at night. Even after “Ain’t No Sunshine” became a hit, he couldn’t bring himself to quit his day job. Withers said the feeling of being used started with his employer paying him $3 an hour, but this song channels that into something more intimate.

The rhythm section came in fully locked from Charles Wright’s Watts 103rd Street Rhythm Band. They rehearsed in drummer James Gadson’s garage, then cut the whole *Still Bill* album in eight hours for $7,500. The clavinet riff that defines “Use Me” started with Ray Jackson noodling on the keys during rehearsal, one of those magical accidents. By one account, Gadson played the groove continuously for about five minutes while Withers came up with the idea for the song. All the members of the band are credited as co-producers on the album: Jackson, Benorce Blackmon, Melvin Dunlap, and Gadson.

And yes, even with a voice that good, this is funk. Funk does not require lesser vocals. It just does not need the singer to dominate the song. Withers sits tight in the groove and still sounds like a generational soul vocalist.

### Fact-Check Notes (2026-04-02)

1. **"he was too nervous to quit his day job"** — Sources consistently say Withers *refused* to quit because he considered the music business unstable and wanted a fallback, not because of nervousness. The distinction matters: pragmatic caution, not anxiety. The research notes (Artist section) phrase it as "believing the music industry was unstable." Consider changing "too nervous" to "refused" or "too cautious." — Sources: [Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/arts-entertainment/2020/04/04/bill-withers-aint-no-sunshine/), [Far Out Magazine](https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/bill-withers-isolated-vocals-aint-no-sunshine/)

2. **"Disguised as a love song, 'Use Me' came from being paid $3 an hour and feeling used."** — The $3/hour detail and employer connection are verified from the 2009 *Still Bill* documentary. However, in the same documentary Withers said the song "was written from a universal point of view rather than a single person." The song's lyrics are explicitly about a romantic/sexual relationship where both parties use each other, with friends and family disapproving. "Disguised as a love song" implies the employer is the real subject, but it's more accurate to say the workplace feeling fed into the song's broader emotional territory. Consider reframing, e.g.: "Withers said the feeling of being used started with his employer, paying him $3 an hour, but the song channels that into something more intimate." — Sources: [TikTok/@slash citing 2009 Still Bill documentary](https://www.tiktok.com/@slash/video/7332032390920965418), [American Songwriter](https://americansongwriter.com/the-story-and-meaning-behind-use-me-bill-withers-funky-look-at-the-power-dynamics-of-a-relationship/)

3. All other factual claims in the entry body verified as accurate: Boeing toilet seats, the Watts 103rd rhythm section, Gadson's garage rehearsals, eight-hour session for $7,500, Ray Jackson's clavinet noodling.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The band that built itself"** — The entire Still Bill rhythm section came from Charles Wright's Watts 103rd Street Rhythm Band. They rehearsed in Gadson's garage, cut the whole album in eight hours for $7,500, and the clavinet riff that defines "Use Me" came from Ray Jackson just noodling during a rehearsal. The angle: what happens when a band already has a shared vocabulary before they walk into the studio.

2. **"The factory floor to the charts"** — Withers was assembling Boeing 747 toilet seats while writing songs at night. Even after "Ain't No Sunshine" hit, he refused to quit his day job. "Use Me" is only two chords, and it was partly inspired by the feeling of being used by his employer at $3 an hour. The angle: a song about romantic manipulation that started as a feeling about blue-collar exploitation.

3. **"Two chords and a clavinet"** — "Use Me" is built on just Em7 and A7, yet it became one of the most covered and sampled funk tracks in history (40+ covers, 70+ samples). The angle: how radical simplicity, when the groove is right, creates an open invitation for reinterpretation across decades and genres.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- "Use Me" is built on only two chords: Em7 and A7. The entire harmonic content of the song sits on that two-chord vamp, leaving the rhythmic and timbral elements to carry the forward motion. — Source: https://www.jonmaclennan.com/blog/use-me-chords
- The clavinet riff was discovered by accident. Ray Jackson was noodling on the clavinet during a rehearsal in James Gadson's garage, and Withers began writing lyrics over it on the spot. According to Gadson, he played the groove for about five minutes before Withers came up with the song. — Source: https://www.tiktok.com/@slash/video/7332032390920965418 (citing 2003 Mojo interview and 2009 Still Bill documentary)
- The studio mix places Jackson's clavinet hard left and Withers' acoustic guitar hard right, creating a stereo split that gives each instrument its own channel. The bass sits comparatively low in the mix, which is unusual for a funk track. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_Me_(Bill_Withers_song)
- Over 40 artists have covered the song, including Fiona Apple, D'Angelo, Grace Jones (on her 1981 album Nightclubbing, backed by Sly & Robbie), Isaac Hayes, Mick Jagger (with Lenny Kravitz and Flea), Liza Minnelli, Al Jarreau, and Scott Walker. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_Me_(Bill_Withers_song)
- The track has been sampled in over 70 songs, including by Kendrick Lamar, Nas (feat. Kanye West), UGK, Logic, Slum Village, and The Click. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Bill-Withers/Use-Me/sampled/
- Robert Christgau called it "one of the few knowledgeable songs about sex our supposedly sexy music has ever produced." — Source: https://www.robertchristgau.com/get_artist.php?name=Bill+Withers
- The live version on Live at Carnegie Hall (1973) runs over eight minutes and opens the album. The crowd demands a reprise, and the performance grows more energetic by the second. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_at_Carnegie_Hall_(Bill_Withers_album)
- "Use Me" peaked at #2 on both the Billboard Hot 100 and Hot Soul Singles charts, blocked from the top spot. It was certified Gold by the RIAA on October 12, 1972. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_Me_(Bill_Withers_song)
- Withers performed the song on the BBC's Old Grey Whistle Test on November 21, 1972. That performance footage has been preserved and reissued digitally. — Source: https://www.setlist.fm/setlist/bill-withers/1972/the-old-grey-whistle-test-bbc-television-theatre-london-england-3bc750e8.html

#### Album
- The entire Still Bill album was recorded in eight hours at The Record Plant in Los Angeles for just $7,500. Withers told Mojo magazine in 2003: "We cut that whole Still Bill album in eight hours for $7,500. I loved that band." — Source: https://www.tiktok.com/@slash/video/7332032390920965418 (citing 2003 Mojo interview)
- Still Bill reached #1 on the Billboard Soul Albums chart and #4 on the Pop Albums chart. It was certified Gold by the RIAA on September 7, 1972. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Still_Bill
- The album contains both "Lean on Me" (#1 pop and R&B) and "Use Me" (#2 pop and R&B), making it one of the rare albums to produce two massive crossover hits from opposite emotional poles: communal uplift and personal desire. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Still_Bill
- The album was ranked #333 on Rolling Stone's 2020 list of the 500 Greatest Albums of All Time. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Still_Bill
- The budget for Still Bill ($7,500) was only slightly more than the $3,600 budget for his debut Just as I Am, which produced "Ain't No Sunshine." — Source: Background knowledge confirmed by multiple sources referencing these figures.
- All five members of the band are credited as co-producers: Withers, Ray Jackson, Benorce Blackmon, Melvin Dunlap, and James Gadson. This was unusual for the era and reflected the communal nature of the sessions. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Still_Bill

### Genre Suggestions

The entry currently has `Classic Funk` assigned.

1. **Classic Funk** — The strongest fit. "Use Me" was a major crossover hit on a well-distributed label, charting at #2 on both pop and R&B charts in 1972. It sits squarely in the book's definition: "music that defined the core vocabulary and was released into the commercial pipeline of major labels, well-distributed singles and albums, radio play, concert tours, and chart presence." The track was released during the defined classic era (1968-1977). Its primary identity is funk, not jazz-funk or soul jazz. — Source: Chart data from https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_Me_(Bill_Withers_song)

2. **Soul Jazz** — A secondary possibility. Withers' voice and songwriting sensibility come from soul, and the stripped-down instrumentation (clavinet, bass, drums, guitar) echoes the small-group format associated with soul jazz. But the groove is too funk-forward and the track too commercially mainstream to fit the soul jazz label well. The book's definition positions soul jazz as "the earlier, smaller-ensemble side of the story" with jazz improvisation, which doesn't describe this track.

3. **Deep Funk** — Not a fit despite the track's simplicity. "Use Me" was a massive hit on release (#2 pop, Gold certified), so it doesn't meet the deep funk criterion of "delayed recognition" or being "overlooked on release."

**Recommendation:** Classic Funk is correct. No change suggested.
