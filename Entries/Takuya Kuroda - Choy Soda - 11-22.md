CHOYA is a Japanese beverage company founded in 1914 in Osaka, and best known for its plum and yuzu drinks. Today’s track, “Choy Soda,” begins with a laid-back vocal groove, then ratchets up the intensity as sax, synths, and trumpet trade solos. At the end, Takuya Kuroda shouts out his favorite drink, giving the track its name.

Kuroda was born in Japan and moved to Boston and then NYC to study jazz. He spent years in an Afrobeat band, became the lead horn for DJ Premier’s Badder Band, and is here self-producing for a London label release.

“Choy Soda” sits right at the intersection of his influences. You’ll hear real jazz chops, a funky groove, and hip-hop production values. If Miles Davis were still around, I would not be surprised if he was making music that sounded something like this.

### Open Research Questions

Open: Should Choy Soda have been "Choya Soda", the soda brand?

**Resolved (2026-04-08):** Almost certainly a deliberate reference to CHOYA, the well-known Japanese beverage company (founded 1914, Osaka) famous for its ume plum soda and yuzu soda products. The KNKX review confirms the track is about a drink: "Kuroda's own shouted vocals exclaiming his choice of beverage wrap up the song." However, the spelling "Choy Soda" is consistent across every official release (artist-controlled Bandcamp, vinyl pressing FW260, Spotify, Apple Music, and the 2024 Waajeed remix single), ruling out a label typo. Kuroda grew up in Ashiya, near CHOYA's Osaka headquarters, and would certainly know the brand. The truncated spelling appears intentional: likely a playful respelling, phonetic abbreviation, or trademark-avoidance move. No interview was found where Kuroda explains the title directly (searched KNKX, Shepherd Express, Atwood Magazine, High Road Touring retrospective). The answer may live in the physical liner notes or simply in Kuroda's head. ### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The trumpet player who followed hip-hop home"** — Kuroda's entire career arc is about a Japanese jazz trumpeter who moved to New York, spent six years in an Afrobeat band, became the lead horn for DJ Premier's Badder Band, and ended up on a London beat-music label. "Choy Soda" sits at the intersection of all those influences: jazz chops, funk groove, hip-hop production sensibility. The theme could frame him as someone who let genre-crossing collaborations reshape his playing rather than the other way around.

2. **"When the closer breaks the spell"** — "Choy Soda" is the album closer, and it works differently than the five tracks before it. It opens with Corey King's breathy, almost mantra-like vocal (just two repeated lines), lulling you into a chill, then the second half detonates: key changes, a galloping rhythm, an incendiary horn exchange between Kuroda and Craig Hill, and Adam Jackson's breathtaking drum display. The theme could be about tracks that save their wildest move for last.

3. **"A Brooklyn big band in miniature"** — Kuroda self-produced this album and plays trumpet, synth bass, and synths. The band ranges from quintet to octet across tracks. The theme could explore how a self-producing bandleader compresses a big ensemble sound into a compact, six-track mini-album, and what that compression does to the energy.

### Genre Suggestions

1. **Jazz-Funk** (strongest case) — Kuroda is a jazz-trained trumpeter (Berklee, The New School) playing in a funk groove context. The track features extended improvisation, a horn-led frontline, and a rhythm section built to support soloists, all hallmarks of jazz-funk per this book's definition. Multiple reviews describe the album as blending "jazz, funk, post-bop, fusion and hip hop." The jazz-funk label captures the core identity: jazz musicians making funk. Source: https://somethingelsereviews.com/2022/10/18/takuya-kuroda-midnight-crisp-2022/

2. **Nu-Funk** — The book defines nu-funk as "a modern funk revival scene that emerged in the mid-1990s, shaped by DJs and bands capturing the feel of rare funk 45s while making new records in that spirit." Kuroda's music is released on First Word Records, a London beat-music label that won Gilles Peterson's Label of the Year. The album blends live instrumentation with sampling and hip-hop production techniques. This places it squarely in the modern revival ecosystem, even if Kuroda's jazz chops push beyond typical nu-funk. Source: https://kudosdistribution.co.uk/label-focus-first-word-records/

3. **Acid Jazz** — The book defines acid jazz as "a late-1980s and 1990s offshoot that revives 1970s jazz-funk for sampling and club culture." Kuroda's production approach (loops, sampling, beat assembly inside a jazz framework) and his label home (a London club-culture label) echo acid jazz's DNA. However, the improvisation on "Choy Soda" is more extended and raw than typical acid jazz, which "keeps dancers moving with tight grooves, hooks, and atmosphere, not extended improvisation." Weaker fit than jazz-funk. Source: https://somethingelsereviews.com/2022/10/18/takuya-kuroda-midnight-crisp-2022/

**Recommendation:** Jazz-Funk is the strongest fit. The track is led by a jazz-trained trumpeter, features extended horn improvisation over a funk groove, and falls squarely into the "jazz musicians making funk" definition.
