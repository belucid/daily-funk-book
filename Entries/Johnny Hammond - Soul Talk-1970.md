### Open Research Questions

No `open:` items were left by the author. One question the research surfaced and resolved: the file is titled "Soul Talk-1970" with a year suffix, which is not the title of the 1969 album track. The track is the closing cut on the 1970 LP *Black Feeling!* (Prestige PR 7736), and is a rerecording of the title track from the 1969 album *Soul Talk*. The "-1970" suffix is the actual title as printed on the LP. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Feeling!

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The remake nobody asked for, that everybody sampled."** Smith cut the original "Soul Talk" in May 1969 with a quintet, then revisited the tune seven months later with a bigger combo (added trumpet, second sax) and a tighter, funkier pocket. The 1969 version got the album title. The 1970 remake got buried as side-two-track-three on a different album, then turned into a foundational breakbeat for early-90s UK rave and drum 'n' bass producers. The "less canonical" cut became the more culturally generative one.
2. **"The Prestige hinge."** *Black Feeling!* sits at the exact pivot between Prestige's soul-jazz era and what Bob Porter was about to build with the Prestige "jazz funk years." Smith would jump to Kudu the next year as that label's first signing. This track is the sound of an organist standing in the doorway between two scenes.
3. **"Purdie's pocket as the real instrument."** Bernard Purdie is the most-recorded drummer in jazz-funk history, and "Soul Talk-1970" is one of the cleaner showcases for why DJs and crate diggers kept coming back to his Prestige sessions. The hook here is to treat the rhythm section, not the organ solo, as the lead voice.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. The drum break from this track became raw material for Acen, Luke Vibert, 4 Hero, Alec Empire and a generation of UK rave producers. Listen for the moment when Bernard Purdie's groove locks in alone, no organ wash on top of it. Why would 90s producers reach for this six seconds and not, say, a James Brown break?
2. Smith already released a version of "Soul Talk" in 1969. This 1970 take adds a trumpet, a second saxophone, and a slightly tougher pocket. Listen for what the second horn does to the call-and-response. Does the fuller front line make the tune feel funkier, or just busier?
3. The organ here is doing two jobs at once: comping chords under the horns and soloing on top. Track Smith's left hand. When does he stop accompanying himself and let Purdie and Jimmy Lewis carry the floor?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- The track is a self-cover. Smith cut "Soul Talk" with a quintet in May 1969 (9:30 long), then re-recorded it seven months later with a sextet on December 22, 1969, retitled "Soul Talk-1970" and trimmed to 6:20. Both versions were released on Prestige within roughly a year of each other. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_Talk_(Johnny_%22Hammond%22_Smith_album) and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Feeling!
- The drum break was sampled by Acen on "Trip II the Moon (Part 2) (The Darkside...)" in 1992, a breakbeat hardcore single that reached No. 71 on the UK Singles Chart and was later included on Pitchfork's *Pitchfork 500*. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trip_II_the_Moon_(Part_2)
- Other artists who built tracks around the "Soul Talk-1970" break include Luke Vibert (as Plug) on "The Life of the Mind" from the 1996 drum 'n' bass landmark *Drum 'n' Bass for Papa*, plus 4 Hero, Alec Empire, John Frusciante, and film composer Brian Tyler (in the 2009 film *Fast and Furious*). — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Johnny-Hammond/Soul-Talk-1970/
- The track features Bernard "Pretty" Purdie on drums, one of the most-recorded drummers in jazz, funk and R&B history. — Source: https://www.jazzdisco.org/johnny-hammond-smith/catalog/

#### Album
- *Black Feeling!* was recorded in one session on December 22, 1969, at Rudy Van Gelder's studio in Englewood Cliffs, New Jersey, the most storied jazz recording room of the 1960s. Producer Bob Porter ran the date. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Feeling!
- The lineup added trumpeter Virgil Jones, second tenor saxophonist Leo Johnson (on one track), and bassist Jimmy Lewis to the core *Soul Talk* unit of Smith, Rusty Bryant, Wally Richardson and Bernard Purdie. — Source: https://www.jazzdisco.org/johnny-hammond-smith/catalog/
- Dusty Groove describes the record as Smith "shaking off the heavier sound of his past, and stepping right into the best modes of the Prestige Records jazz funk years," crediting Bob Porter's production for the "tightly snapping rhythms at the bottom." — Source: https://www.dustygroove.com/item/26448/Johnny-Hammond-Smith:Black-Feeling
- AllMusic gave the album three out of five stars, calling out the "much fuller combo" compared with Smith's earlier soul-jazz dates. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Feeling!

#### Artist
- Born John Robert Smith on December 16, 1933, in Louisville, Kentucky. The "Hammond" nickname stuck after he switched from piano to the Hammond B-3 organ in Cleveland, and conveniently distinguished him from jazz guitarist Johnny Smith and organ giant Jimmy Smith. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_%22Hammond%22_Smith
- He died June 4, 1997, in Victorville, California, of cancer. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_%22Hammond%22_Smith
- Smith spent a decade on Prestige throughout the 1960s, then signed to Kudu Records in 1971, becoming the first artist on Creed Taylor's new soul-jazz imprint. His Kudu debut *Breakout* featured Grover Washington Jr. as a sideman before Washington's solo career took off. — Source: https://fromthevaults-boppinbob.blogspot.com/2017/12/johnny-hammond-smith-born-16-december.html
- Early in his career he worked as accompanist to Nancy Wilson, and later wrote "Quiet Fire" for her 1988 album *Nancy Now!*. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_%22Hammond%22_Smith
- In retirement from full-time recording, Smith taught in the music department at Cal Poly Pomona starting in January 1987. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_%22Hammond%22_Smith

### Genre Suggestions

The current value is **Soul Jazz**. The book's own definition places soul jazz as "the early simmer" with smaller ensembles, and jazz-funk as "the full boil" with bigger bands and a more overtly funk-forward rhythm section.

1. **Jazz-Funk (recommended alternative).** *Black Feeling!* expanded Smith's working unit from a quintet to a sextet with trumpet and a second tenor. The tracks are long, the rhythm section locks into Bob Porter's "Prestige Records jazz funk years" template, and the album sits one calendar year before Smith's move to Kudu, which is canonical jazz-funk territory. Dusty Groove explicitly frames this as Smith stepping into "the Prestige Records jazz funk years." — Source: https://www.dustygroove.com/item/26448/Johnny-Hammond-Smith:Black-Feeling
2. **Soul Jazz (the current value, defensible).** The track is still organ-led, the harmony is blues-rooted, and AllMusic and most jazz reference sources file Smith's 1969-1970 Prestige work as soul-jazz. If the author wants to use this entry to mark the late-period soul-jazz on its way to becoming jazz-funk, the current tag holds. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_Talk_(Johnny_%22Hammond%22_Smith_album)
3. **Deep Funk (tertiary case).** The "Soul Talk-1970" cut was overlooked on first release (it was the closing track on a non-charting LP and immediately overshadowed by Smith's Kudu pivot), then found its audience two decades later through crate-digging UK breakbeat and drum 'n' bass producers. That arc fits the book's definition of deep funk almost exactly. The weakness: Prestige is not a regional or private-press label. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Johnny-Hammond/Soul-Talk-1970/

**Recommendation:** Keep Soul Jazz if the entry leans into the rerecording / Prestige-organ-tradition angle. Switch to Jazz-Funk if the entry leans into the Bob Porter / Purdie / Kudu-doorway angle. Both are defensible.

### Region Suggestions

1. **US - Northeast (filled in).** The track was recorded at Van Gelder Studio in Englewood Cliffs, New Jersey on Dec 22, 1969. Prestige Records operated out of Bergenfield, NJ at this time. The musicians on the date were the New York studio first call: Bernard Purdie, Virgil Jones, Rusty Bryant, Wally Richardson, Jimmy Lewis. This is a regional scene tag. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Feeling!
2. **US - New York (alternative).** The personnel are largely the NYC jazz studio core, and Smith himself based his professional career out of New York from the 1950s onward. The book's "On Regions" note allows the regional choice to reflect "where the scene lived." — Source: https://nkaa.uky.edu/nkaa/items/show/1857
3. (No case for tagging by Smith's Louisville origin or his California retirement; neither shaped this specific recording.)

**Recommendation:** Keep **US - Northeast**, which captures both the NJ studio and the NYC session-musician scene without committing to one state.
