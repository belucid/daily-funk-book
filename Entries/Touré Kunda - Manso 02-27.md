Touré Kunda’s "Manso" does not fit the Fela Kuti, Nigerian template for Afrobeat. It's mbalax, which is Senegalese popular music, but hooked to a disco bassline pitted against some aggressive polyrhythms. To make the genre classification problem even worse, the whole thing was recorded in Paris.

Brothers Ismaïla and Sixu Tidiane Touré were born in Casamance, Senegal. Their older brother, a musician, steered them toward the music business. "Touré Kunda" means "the elephant family" in Soninké, and the brothers adopted it as their stage name in 1977. They moved to Paris in 1979 to join the small but important West African music scene. From there, the bridge to the American market was much easier to cross.

Many Afrobeat bands leaned vocally into the American side of the fusion, often singing in English or in a pidgin designed to reach the widest possible audience. Touré Kunda went the other way. Across their records, they used several languages, reflecting the linguistic mix of southwestern Senegal: Soninké, Mandingo, Wolof, Diola, Fula, and Portuguese Creole. The brothers also picked up French and English during their careers, which makes me less proud of my C- in high school German.

Ismaïla Touré died in Paris on February 27, 2023, at the age of 73.

### Open Research Questions

No `open:` items were present in the source file. Two notable unresolved threads I uncovered while researching:

- **Meaning of "Manso" in context.** The word "Mansa" means hereditary king/ruler in Mandinka/Maninka, with cognates in Soninké ("manga"), Bambara ("masa"), and Susu ("menge") — see https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansa_(title). "Manso" is plausibly a related form (the Touré brothers grew up in a Soninké-speaking household in Casamance and the group sings in Soninké, Mandingo, Wolof, Diola, Fula, and Portuguese creole — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tour%C3%A9_Kunda). I could not find a published gloss confirming this reading of the song title. Approach taken: searched Mandinka and Soninké dictionaries, song-lyric sites (sonichits.com), and French-language coverage of the band (Music In Africa, Cap sur le Monde, Pan-African Music). None gloss the title. Suggested next step for the author: ask Sixu Tidiane Touré directly via Touré Kunda's contact channels, or consult a Soninké speaker.
- **Spelling of the album title.** Original 1980 Celluloid pressings render the title as "É'mma Africa" (Discogs https://www.discogs.com/release/1016356-Tour%C3%A9-Kunda-%C3%89mma-Africa). ### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"Afrobeat" as umbrella, not formula.** "Manso" doesn't fit the Fela-pioneered Lagos template at all: it's mbalax DNA stitched to a Paris-disco bottom, with reggae upstrokes peeking through. The theme is the book's working definition of Afrobeat as "funk rooted in Africa," and how 1980 Paris was the strangest, most fertile place to test it. (See https://www.musicinafrica.net/fr/magazine/5-chansons-emblematiques-du-groupe-toure-kunda for the era's signature stew of "pop mélangé à des sonorités africaines.")
- **The Casamance polyglot.** Most "African band" narratives flatten language; Touré Kunda fans the deck. Six languages across their catalog, all reflecting Casamance's borderland mix at the southwestern corner of Senegal. "Manso" becomes a doorway into how the group's bilingualism is the music, not just the lyrics. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tour%C3%A9_Kunda)
- **Paris as African capital, 1980.** The É'mma Africa story is also the Celluloid story: Gilbert Castro's label and a small Paris scene effectively built the first commercial bridge for West African pop into France and the US. Frame "Manso" as the sound of a diaspora hub finding its voice three years before the world music boom officially "started." (https://toure-kunda.neocities.org/toure-kunda-000)
- **The "elephant family" introduces itself.** "Touré Kunda" means "elephant family" in Soninké, and the brothers had only just adopted that name (1977, at Africa Fête in Paris) when they recorded É'mma Africa. The entry could lean into this as the moment the band finalized its identity. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tour%C3%A9_Kunda; https://www.capsurlemonde.org/casamance/toure-kunda.html)

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "Manso" appears as track 8 on É'mma Africa, sandwiched between the Latin-tinted "Baounane" and the closer "Touty Yolle." The pairing of "Manso" and "Touty Yolle" was singled out 42 years later by remixer Deni Shain as "the two songs that stood out" when he was digging Toure Kunda LPs in Guadeloupe, leading to the 2022 Atangana Records reissue. — Source: https://atanganarecords.bandcamp.com/album/toure-kunda-manso
- Atangana Records pressed the 2022 12" reissue as a numbered limited edition of 90 on white vinyl plus a black-vinyl edition — already collector-fodder status for a deep cut. — Source: https://atanganarecords.bandcamp.com/album/toure-kunda-manso
- "Manso" itself was never released as a single in 1980; it lived exclusively as an album track until the 2022 Atangana reissue gave it a standalone life. The 1980 7" lifted from the album was "É'mma / Africa," not "Manso." — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/3601822-Tour%C3%A9-Kunda-Emma-Africa

#### Album

- É'mma Africa was Touré Kunda's first record under that band name and the start of their seven-year run with the pioneering Celluloid label, run by Gilbert Castro in France and Jean Karakos in the US. — Source: https://toure-kunda.neocities.org/toure-kunda-000
- Album credits list A. Georges Crespin as producer (also the group's manager) and Pascal Mattioco as arranger — both Paris-scene fixtures rather than Senegalese collaborators, which helps explain the European-pop polish on the rhythm tracks. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/1016356-Tour%C3%A9-Kunda-%C3%89mma-Africa
- The album spans six languages: Soninké, Wolof, Fula, Mandingo, Diola, and Portuguese creole — reflecting Casamance's place as a linguistic crossroads at Senegal's southwestern edge. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tour%C3%A9_Kunda
- Total runtime is exactly 36 minutes and 16 seconds across nine tracks — Source: https://www.qobuz.com/us-en/album/emma-africa-toure-kunda/zibtq4gmkp4da

#### Artist

- "Touré Kunda" means "the elephant family" in Soninké. The brothers adopted the name as their stage identity in 1977 after a performance at the Africa Fête festival in Paris. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tour%C3%A9_Kunda
- Ismaïla and Sixu Tidiane Touré were born just 22 days apart in 1950 in Ziguinchor, the capital of Senegal's Casamance region. They are not twins but were close enough in age and lifelong collaboration that French press routinely referred to them as such. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tour%C3%A9_Kunda
- Their elder brother and mentor Amadou Touré joined the group in April 1979 and died in January 1983 — a loss that shadowed the next phase of the band's career. — Source: https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tour%C3%A9_Kunda_(groupe)
- They performed for Nelson Mandela in 1990 (some sources say 1992) at his Paris welcome after his release from prison. — Source: https://pan-african-music.com/ismaila-toure-laine-des-toure-kunda-nous-a-quittes/
- Touré Kunda eventually contributed to Carlos Santana's Grammy-winning 1999 album Supernatural and reunited with Santana plus Manu Dibango on their 2018 40th-anniversary record Lambi Golo. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tour%C3%A9_Kunda
- Ismaïla Touré died February 27, 2023 in Paris at 73 after a long illness. — Source: https://pan-african-music.com/ismaila-toure-laine-des-toure-kunda-nous-a-quittes/

### Genre Suggestions

Currently: **Afrobeat**.

Per the book's working definition, Afrobeat is used as a "broad label for funk rooted in Africa" rather than strictly Fela-tradition Lagos Afrobeat. "Manso" fits that umbrella but is genuinely a hybrid.

1. **Afrobeat (keep).** This is the book's catchall for African funk and is the most reader-legible choice. Allows the entry to slot into the Afrobeat lane alongside Manu Dibango "Soul Makossa" (already at 05-10) without splitting hairs.
2. **Latin Funk.** The bassline and rhythm-section feel on "Manso" lean Caribbean — Atangana Records labels the original as "afro disco groove," and the album's reviews repeatedly cite Latin and Caribbean intonations alongside the African and reggae elements (https://www.musicinafrica.net/fr/magazine/5-chansons-emblematiques-du-groupe-toure-kunda). The book's Latin Funk definition is explicitly "funk plus Latin rhythms… Afro-Cuban, Puerto Rican, Brazilian, and all pan-Latin flavors," which gives this room.
3. **(Out of scope) Disco/Boogie.** Multiple modern sources describe "Manso" as "afro disco" (https://atanganarecords.bandcamp.com/album/toure-kunda-manso). The book explicitly excludes disco/boogie, so this is mentioned only to note that some listeners will hear the track that way. The reader-mission can lean into "is this disco?" without changing the tag.

**Recommendation: Keep as Afrobeat.** The book's umbrella definition was written exactly for cases like this, and the alternative (Latin Funk) doesn't pick up the African roots that are the dominant identity of the track.
