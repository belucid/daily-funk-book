Pearl Farano was born Pearl Reaves on June 7, 1929, in Raleigh, North Carolina. She moved to Rahway, New Jersey, in 1949 and married drummer Paul Farano. After she won five consecutive weeks of amateur night, she earned a permanent spot singing at his Palace Blue Room club. The couple later moved to Boston, where they were living in 1982 when today’s song, “Who’s Your Boss,” was recorded.

“Who’s Your Boss” was Farano’s final commercial release before she left the stage to preach full-time at Mount Olive Temple of Christ in Dorchester. The single’s A-side, “Use Me Lord,” is a straight gospel number. “Who’s Your Boss” is the funky B-side, the kind of track gospel acts often recorded as filler, a throwaway until crate-diggers rediscover it. The title poses a workplace question, but the answer is clear: Jesus is your boss. It's a gospel sermon from a soon-to-be preacher set to a funk groove.

A few questions surfaced during research that the author may want to be aware of:

- **Was the single ever credited under both A- and B-side artists?** Discogs lists the original 1982 issue under "Pearl Farano" only (A: "Use Me Lord" / B: "Who's Your Boss"), but the 2021 reissue and the Cultures of Soul compilation credit "Pearl Farano and The High Lites/Lights of Joy." The entry's spelling "High Lites of Joy" matches the original record. Background knowledge, but no source found for which spelling Pearl herself preferred outside the label scan.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The last record of a 30-year career."** "Who's Your Boss" was Pearl Reaves/Farano's final commercial release before she left the stage entirely to preach full-time at Mount Olive Temple of Christ in Dorchester. Frames the track as the closing chapter of a working musician's life — R&B sideman in 1949, lead-singing guitarist in 1955 ("I'm Not Ashamed (Ugly Woman)"), East Coast lounge act through the 60s, gospel-funk shouter by 1982, preacher by the mid-80s.
2. **"Whose boss?"** The title plays on a workplace question and answers it theologically: Jesus is your boss. The track is a sermon set to a funk groove. This is the gospel-funk move in microcosm — borrow the secular vocabulary (funk pocket, R&B band, call-and-response) and redirect it toward praise.
3. **"Pearl Reaves invented Lonnie Youngblood."** A side angle: in 1959 the saxophonist who would later play with Jimi Hendrix joined Reaves' band. She took one look at him and said "Oh, he's just a baby!" — and he was Lonnie Thomas no more. The throughline: Reaves' bands kept turning out future legends while she stayed, the working artist, in magnificent obscurity.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. Pearl was a guitar player herself — listen to Edmund Bullock's guitar against Jerome Henderson's drums. Where does the band lock, and where does it scramble to keep up with Pearl's vocal?
2. The track runs under three minutes. Count the seconds before the first "boss" lands. How much groove are you given before the sermon starts?
3. Listen to the background vocalists ("extra voices: Alleyne Brown, Deborah Henderson, Carolyn Bullock"). At what moments do they answer Pearl, and at what moments do they get out of her way? In gospel call-and-response, the silence is structural — pick the most charged silence in the track.

### Artist

- Pearl Farano was Pearl Reaves (June 7, 1929 – March 6, 2000). She was born in Raleigh, NC, moved to Rahway, NJ in 1949, married Paul Farano (the drummer who hired her at his Palace Blue Room club after she won five weeks of amateur night), and relocated to Boston with him in the 1960s. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_Reaves
- Her biggest hit was 1955's "I'm Not Ashamed (Ugly Woman)" on Harlem Records, backed by the Concords — a vocal group whose lead singer, Milton Love, later fronted the Solitaires. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_Reaves and https://www.uncamarvy.com/Concords/concords.html
- Saxophonist **Lonnie Youngblood**, who later recorded with Jimi Hendrix in 1966, got his stage name from Reaves. His first professional gig (1959) was backing her band; the first time she saw him she exclaimed "Oh, he's just a baby!" and he stopped being Lonnie Thomas from that day forward. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lonnie_Youngblood and https://earofnewt.com/2025/07/05/saxophonist-lonnie-youngblood-recalls-recording-with-jimi-hendrix-before-he-became-jimi-hendrix/
- She sang lead and played guitar — extremely rare for a Black woman in 1950s R&B. The Paul Farano Trio toured the East Coast through the 50s and 60s. Source: https://www.popmatters.com/pearl-reaves-and-her-retro-rb-the-magical-obscurity-of-the-working-artist-2495376596.html
- "Who's Your Boss" was her last commercial recording. In the 1980s she gave up performing entirely and became a preacher at Mount Olive Temple of Christ in Boston's Dorchester neighborhood, where she served until her death in 2000. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_Reaves
- Pearl and Paul ran their own label, Pearlsfar Records, out of Dorchester, MA. Earlier Pearlsfar releases include the 1966 "How Can I Tell" / "Change Me" 45. Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/11753621-Pearl-Reaves-How-Can-I-Tell- and https://www.popmatters.com/pearl-reaves-and-her-retro-rb-the-magical-obscurity-of-the-working-artist-2495376596.html

### Genre Suggestions

1. **Gospel Funk (current value — keep).** Definitionally exact. Per `On Genres.md`: "gospel music that absorbed the funk vocabulary of its era, or funk musicians turning their groove toward praise. In practice, it shows up as a gospel act getting seriously funky on a track, or as a funk act using a track to make a joyful noise for the Lord." Pearl Farano fits both halves: a longtime funk/R&B veteran turning toward praise, on a record literally called "Use Me Lord" / "Who's Your Boss." Also: the track was rescued and named-as-genre by collectors (Greg Belson, Cultures of Soul) — exactly the "after-the-fact label, coined by collectors" pattern the book uses to define Gospel Funk. Source: https://culturesofsoul.bandcamp.com/track/pearl-farano-and-the-high-lites-of-joy-who-s-your-boss
2. **Deep Funk (alternative).** Equally defensible: per `On Genres.md`, Deep Funk is "less a sound than a commercial fate" — tracks "overlooked on release and only found their audience later through crate-digging DJs," especially private/regional pressings. "Who's Your Boss" was a 1982 private-label 45 from Dorchester, MA, completely ignored until Greg Belson dug it up 39 years later. That is the textbook Deep Funk story. The reason to keep Gospel Funk over Deep Funk is that the sacred content is so explicit — but a strong case exists either way. Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/8893882-Pearl-Farano-Whos-Your-Boss
3. **Classic Funk (weakest alternative).** The book draws the classic era as 1969–1977; "Who's Your Boss" falls outside that window (1982), so this is not a good fit. Mentioning only to rule it out.
