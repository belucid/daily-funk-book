### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"Open up the door": access, not handouts.** Brown said in a 1996 interview that he wrote it because "there are a lot of things closed to blacks, Hispanics, women" (quoted by Derrick Z. Jackson, https://black-international-cinema.com/BIC07/HTML/articles/article_15.htm). The parenthetical in the title still asks for the door to be opened, so the song is about getting access and then earning what's inside. That tension is a strong hook.
2. **The sequel to "Say It Loud."** This came a few months after "Say It Loud – I'm Black and I'm Proud." Wikipedia calls it one of two social-commentary songs Brown recorded in 1969, pleading for equal opportunity and self-reliance (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Brown). Jackson, who was 13 when "Say It Loud" came out, says this song meant more to him. The angle is the second, harder statement after the anthem.
3. **The song that lived on stage.** The single version did not appear on an LP until the 1996 CD *Foundations of Funk*. In the meantime the song survived as a live number: a live take recorded in Augusta on October 1, 1969 went on *Sex Machine* (1970), and the full homecoming show came out in 2019 as *Live at Home with His Bad Self*. The angle is a hit single without an album home.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. Brown sings "Don't give me integration, give me true communication." Listen to how the band responds right after a political line like that. Do the horns comment on the message or just keep the dance going?
2. Compare the 3:05 single (Part 1) with the 9:43 unedited take on *Foundations of Funk*. What did the edit cut out, and does the song feel more like a sermon or more like a groove at full length?
3. Play the studio single and then the live Augusta version from *Sex Machine* or *Live at Home with His Bad Self*. Notice what changes in the tempo and the shouting when Brown sings these lines to his hometown crowd.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- Recorded February 23, 1969, at Lafevere Studios in Atlanta. Written and produced by Brown. Released in March 1969 as a two-part single on King 6224 (Part 1 3:05, Part 2 2:50). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Don%27t_Want_Nobody_to_Give_Me_Nothing_(Open_Up_the_Door,_I%27ll_Get_It_Myself)
- Chart peaks: #3 R&B, #20 Hot 100, #51 Canada. It came out between "Soul Pride" (#33 R&B) and "The Popcorn." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Brown_discography
- It was on Billboard's R&B chart at #12 for the week of April 19, 1969, up from #34, in its second week. That puts the R&B debut around April 12, 1969 (inferred). — Source: https://www.billboard.com/charts/r-b-hip-hop-songs/1969-04-19 (seen via search result; the page itself is paywalled)
- In a 1996 interview Brown explained the song: "there are a lot of things closed to blacks, Hispanics, women." Columnist Derrick Z. Jackson reads the line "Don't give me integration" as rejecting *only* integration, not integration itself. — Source: https://black-international-cinema.com/BIC07/HTML/articles/article_15.htm
- The full unedited take runs 9:43 and first came out on the 1996 compilation *Foundations of Funk – A Brand New Bag: 1964–1969*, mixed by Joseph M. Palmaccio. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/2339434-James-Brown-Foundations-Of-Funk-A-Brand-New-Bag-1964-1969
- Sampled by Ice-T ("Power") and by Ed O.G. & Da Bulldogs ("I Got To Have It"). — Source: Wikipedia song article above. WhoSampled returned HTTP 403 and could not be checked.
- Swarthmore's "Black Liberation 1969" playlist project includes the song. Alumnus Clinton Etheridge '69 remembered being taken with it in his senior spring. — Source: https://blacklib1969.swarthmore.edu/items/show/1253
- Writer Jim Connelly prefers it to "Say It Loud," mostly because he can't stand children's choirs, and describes its "solid pre-disco bassline." — Source: https://medialoper.com/certain-songs-723-james-brown-i-dont-want-nobody-to-give-me-nothing-open-up-the-door-ill-get-it-myself/

#### Album

- There was no parent studio album. Wikipedia's discography lists it as a "Non-album track," and it is missing from the 1969 LPs *It's a Mother* (https://www.discogs.com/master/52539-James-Brown-Its-A-Mother) and *It's a New Day So Let a Man Come In* (https://www.discogs.com/master/98797-James-Brown-Its-A-New-Day-So-Let-A-Man-Come-In).
- A live version (4:31) opens side three of *Sex Machine* (King 1115, September 1970). It was recorded at the Bell Auditorium in Augusta on October 1, 1969, with reverb and applause added later. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sex_Machine_(album)
- The complete October 1, 1969 Augusta homecoming show was shelved because the "Sex Machine" single took off. It was finally released on October 25, 2019 as *Live at Home with His Bad Self* (Republic/UMe) and includes this song. — Source: https://news.umgcatalog.com/?p=1491 ; https://theseconddisc.com/2019/09/17/get-on-up-james-browns-1969-homecoming-show-remixed-and-expanded-for-october-release/

#### Artist

- Brown started supporting Nixon after he was invited to perform at Nixon's inaugural ball in January 1969, the month before this session. His 1972 endorsement of Nixon led to pickets reading "James Brown: Nixon's Clown." The politics of self-reliance in this song arguably prefigure that turn. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Brown
- Through James Brown Enterprises he "helped to provide jobs for blacks in business in the communities." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Brown

- **album**: left empty. The Wikipedia discography marks it "Non-album tracks." The song article says the single version first appeared on an album with *Foundations of Funk* (1996), and a live version was on *Sex Machine* (1970). Neither 1969 LP includes it (Discogs, linked above). I found no source claiming it was on an original album.
- **label**: King Records, single 45-6224 (Discogs release 1687743; Wikipedia "King 6224"). Consistent across sources.
- **year**: 1969. Wikipedia says March 1969. Discogs master year is 1969. Amazon metadata says Mar 1969. No conflicting sources.
- **track length**: Part 1 is 3:05 (Discogs, Wikipedia). Alternates that may confuse fact-checkers: Part 2 2:50; Parts 1 & 2 combined on *Star Time* about 5:59–6:00; Apple/Deezer list the Pt.1 single version at 3:04–3:05; *Cold Sweat & Other Soul Classics* lists "Pt. 1" at 3:31 (Deezer) or 7:02 (Apple), which are different edits; full unedited take 9:43 (*Foundations of Funk*); *Sex Machine* live version 4:31. If the author picks the combined A/B performance, use about `5m 55s`.
- **region**: set to `US - Southeast` (see below).
- **Title punctuation**: Discogs and Wikipedia print a comma ("Open Up the Door, I'll Get It Myself"). The entry's `track` field has no comma. Many digital releases also omit it. I did not change the field because it is not editable.

### Genre Suggestions

The genre definitions come from `Matter/On Subgenres.md`. There is no `On Genres & Regions.md` file.

1. **Classic Funk** (strong recommendation). It is a 1969 King single that charted #3 R&B and #20 Pop, which fits the book's 1969–1977 classic-era window. It was made by the band that defined the vocabulary (Stubblefield, Nolen, Parker, Wesley). Discogs styles: Soul, Funk.
2. **Deep Funk** (no). It was a major hit, which is the opposite of the delayed recognition that defines the category. It is listed here only to rule it out.
3. No other subgenre fits. A soul/message-song framing belongs in the writing, not the genre field.

### Region Suggestions

1. **US - Southeast** (written to the field). It was recorded in Atlanta, GA. Brown was born in Barnwell, SC, raised in Augusta, GA, and the live versions were cut in Augusta. Two of the three possible anchors (recording location and artist's home) point to the Southeast.
2. **US - Ohio**. King Records was based in Cincinnati, and the existing entry `James Brown - Ain't It Funky Now (Parts 1 and 2).md` uses `US - Ohio`. **Flag:** James Brown entries should probably use the same region throughout. If the author prefers the label-city convention, change this to `US - Ohio`.
