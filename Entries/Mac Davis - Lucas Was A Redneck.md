### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"Country funk by accident, on purpose"** — Mac Davis was never trying to make a funk record. He was a Columbia-Records crossover star, a TV variety-show host, and a songwriter who fed Elvis hits. But by cutting at FAME in Muscle Shoals with Rick Hall, with Travis Wammack on guitar and a tight pocket band, the album ended up with at least one track funky enough that crate-diggers grabbed it forty years later for the Light in the Attic *Country Funk 1969–1975* compilation. The theme is the difference between making funk and making something that *becomes* funk in retrospect.
- **"The hidden track on the polite album"** — Stop and Smell the Roses was a soft-AC hit machine — the title cut went to No. 9 on the pop chart and No. 1 on Easy Listening. Tucked at side B, track 3, "Lucas Was A Redneck" is the grit on an otherwise smooth record. The entry frames the strange one-deep-cut habit of polite mainstream LPs in the early '70s.
- **"Lubbock to Muscle Shoals"** — A West Texas kid who watched Buddy Holly and Elvis as a boy ends up at FAME in Alabama in 1974, making a track that sounds like neither Lubbock nor Muscle Shoals, but somewhere in the middle. Theme: the geography of country funk runs through producers and rooms, not just artists.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- This song was a deep cut on a sentimental ballad LP. After you hear it, queue the title track "Stop and Smell the Roses" right after. What does Rick Hall let Mac do here that the producer wouldn't let him do on the singles? Listen for which instrument is doing the work that makes "Lucas" funky and the rest of the album not.
- Forty years after release, this was picked by Light in the Attic crate-diggers as track 4 on *Country Funk 1969–1975*. Listen with their ears: what tells you in the first twenty seconds that someone went looking for this on purpose?
- This is a story-song about a fictional small-town drunk redneck, sung by a Lubbock-born ex-Elvis-songwriter at the height of his crossover-pop career. Listen for the moments where Mac drops the persona and gets close to the character. Is he laughing at Lucas, or with him?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "Lucas Was A Redneck" was first cut earlier — a 1971 Canadian-pressing Columbia 7" exists, catalog 4-45355, A-side "Lucas Was A Redneck (Born To Be A Bum)" b/w "Fall In Love With Your Wife", running only 2:15, produced by Jerry Fuller (not Rick Hall). The 1974 album version on *Stop and Smell the Roses* (2:47, produced by Rick Hall at FAME) is the rerecorded, longer, funkier take that the world remembers. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/15046720-Mac-Davis-Lucas-Was-A-Redneck-Born-To-Be-A-Bum
- The track sits at position B3 on the album, between "A Poor Man's Gold" and "Kiss It And Make It Better" — buried in the second half of a Side B otherwise full of ballads. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/master/187599-Mac-Davis-Stop-And-Smell-The-Roses
- Light in the Attic Records picked the song as track 4 of *Country Funk 1969–1975* (released July 24, 2012), positioning it alongside Dale Hawkins, Bobby Darin, Link Wray, Bobbie Gentry, and Tony Joe White as a foundational country-funk text. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_Funk_1969%E2%80%931975
- The song's narrator describes Lucas as a Tupelo, Mississippi redneck — Tupelo also being Elvis Presley's birthplace, and Mac Davis being one of Presley's most important late-career songwriters. — Source: https://krcgtv.com/features/beyond-the-trivia/beyond-the-trivia-mac-davis-in-the-ghetto

#### Album

- *Stop and Smell the Roses* was recorded at FAME Studios in Muscle Shoals, Alabama — the same room where Aretha Franklin, Wilson Pickett, and the Allman Brothers cut classic sides — with Rick Hall as primary producer. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stop_and_Smell_the_Roses_(Mac_Davis_album)
- Released August 1974 on Columbia (KC 32582). The album hit No. 2 on Billboard's Country Albums chart and No. 13 on the Billboard 200; in Australia it reached No. 60 on the Kent Music Report. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stop_and_Smell_the_Roses_(Mac_Davis_album)
- The title cut, co-written by Mac Davis with bandleader Doc Severinsen (of *The Tonight Show*), was a No. 9 pop hit and a No. 1 Easy Listening hit — meaning the LP carrying "Lucas Was A Redneck" was bought largely by middle-of-the-road pop and country fans, not funk listeners. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stop_and_Smell_the_Roses_(song)
- The session band included Travis Wammack and Ken Bell on guitars, Jerry Bridges on bass, Roger Clark on drums, Leo LeBlanc on steel guitar, plus Victor Feldman on vibraphone and Tommy Morgan on harmonica — a Muscle Shoals/LA hybrid lineup with strings arranged by Jimmie Haskell and backing by the Ron Hicklin Singers. — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/1760324-Mac-Davis-Stop-And-Smell-The-Roses

#### Artist

- Morris Mac Davis was born January 21, 1942 in Lubbock, Texas, and died September 29, 2020 in Nashville, age 78, following heart surgery. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mac_Davis
- Before going solo, Davis wrote some of Elvis Presley's most important late-career hits — "In the Ghetto," "Memories," "Don't Cry Daddy," and "A Little Less Conversation." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mac_Davis
- Davis's first solo No. 1 was "Baby Don't Get Hooked on Me" (1972), recorded at FAME Studios with Rick Hall producing — establishing the Mac/Rick/FAME pipeline that two years later produced *Stop and Smell the Roses* and "Lucas Was A Redneck." — Source: https://www.billboard.com/pro/forever-no-1-mac-davis-baby-dont-get-hooked-on-me/
- *The Mac Davis Show* premiered on NBC in 1974 — the same year as this album — and ran through 1976, winning an Emmy. He was simultaneously a pop singer, a country singer, a primetime TV variety host, and (by 1979) Nick Nolte's co-star in *North Dallas Forty*. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mac_Davis
- As a kid in Lubbock, Davis saw Buddy Holly drive past on the street, then at thirteen (June 3, 1955) saw Elvis Presley perform at the local Pontiac dealership — two formative encounters that connect West Texas, Memphis, and his eventual Elvis songwriting career. — Source: https://www.texasmonthly.com/arts-entertainment/remembering-lubbock-born-country-musician-mac-davis/

### Genre Suggestions

- **Country Funk** (current value) — Strong fit. Light in the Attic literally canonized this track on *Country Funk 1969–1975* (the genre-defining compilation, per the project's own "On Genres" essay). No stronger external endorsement is available. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_Funk_1969%E2%80%931975
- **Classic Funk** — Weak alternative. The track is from 1974, inside the 1969–1977 classic-funk window, and Davis is on a major label (Columbia) with broad distribution. But the primary identity is country, not funk; per the project's definition of Classic Funk ("the track's primary identity is funk, not funk fused into another genre"), this fails. Keep Country Funk.
- **Deep Funk** — Could be argued: the song was effectively rediscovered by crate-diggers in 2012 and made its name through that delayed recognition (the defining trait of deep funk per "On Genres"). But Mac Davis was a major-label star and the parent album was a top-15 Billboard 200 record, which is the opposite of the "regional or private pressings" and "poorly selling record with limited distribution" pattern that "On Genres" describes. Country Funk is the better fit.

### Region Suggestions

- **US - Texas** (suggested, and set) — Mac Davis is one of Texas's signature country-pop crossover artists, born and raised in Lubbock; the Texas Heritage Songwriters' Association inducted him. He's claimed by Texas culture as a native son. Source: https://texassongwriters.com/inductee/mac-davis/
- **US - Southeast** — Reasonable alternative: the track was cut at FAME Studios in Muscle Shoals, Alabama, with Rick Hall producing and the FAME house band. The "country funk" feel comes directly from that Southeastern studio sound. If the book wants to credit the room over the artist, Southeast is defensible. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stop_and_Smell_the_Roses_(Mac_Davis_album)
- **US - Midwest** — No case. Davis's career was rooted in Texas and Nashville/LA; nothing about this song or album connects to the Midwest.

I'd recommend keeping **US - Texas** because the song is sung in a Texan's voice about a fictional Mississippi character, written by a Texan, and Davis is consistently positioned as a Texas artist in obituaries and inductions.
