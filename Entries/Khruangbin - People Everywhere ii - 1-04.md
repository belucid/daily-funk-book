*The Universe Smiles Upon You ii* is a deliberate echo of Khruangbin's 2015 debut album, *The Universe Smiles Upon You*. The band returned a decade later to record it from January 4 to 6, 2025, in the same Central Texas barn where they have made most of their music. As a chill, funk-forward jam band, these tracks are never played the same way twice, but re-recording them was a way to celebrate the band’s evolution and to hear how a decade of playing together affects the same set of songs. The release landed exactly ten years after the original, and the recording sessions were staged across the same dates ten years apart.

Khruangbin recorded this live, with the trio in a circle on the dirt floor of a corrugated tin barn. There is very little isolation between them, and no condensers on the microphones. They sometimes joke that the barn is the fourth member of the band. Can you hear the room? If so, what does it add?

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The same song, ten years later"** — The heart of this track is its existence as a deliberate echo. Khruangbin returned to the same dirt-floor barn in Burton, TX, in January 2025, on the exact anniversary of their original January 2015 sessions, to recut their debut. The theme opens up questions about what a decade of playing together does to a groove: what tightens, what loosens, what breathes differently. The original "People Everywhere (Still Alive)" was 2m 39s. The new version runs nearly 8 minutes. That's not padding, that's confidence.

2. **"A barn with a dirt floor"** — Every Khruangbin album has been recorded in a corrugated tin barn with no insulation and dirt floors, on Mark Speer's family property in Burton, TX (population ~300). Producer Steve Christensen uses only dynamic mics, no condensers, and records the trio in a circle, live, with bleed as a feature. The barn is the fourth member. This theme grounds the entry in the physicality of the sound.

3. **"The gospel band that became Thai funk"** — Mark Speer and DJ Johnson met in 2004 playing in a gospel band at St. John's Methodist Church in Houston. Laura Lee Ochoa was a math teacher who didn't pick up a bass until 2009. Their path to becoming one of the most globally-inflected funk bands of the 2020s is unlikely and charming, and this track captures the destination of that journey.

### Genre Suggestions

The current genre is **Psychedelic Funk**. Here are considerations:

1. **Psychedelic Funk** (current) — A reasonable fit. Khruangbin's sound is rooted in psychedelic textures: reverb-drenched guitar, hypnotic repetition, and dreamy atmospheres. The book's definition emphasizes "surreal colors and sounds" and "hallucinatory storytelling," and while Khruangbin is more subtle than Parliament-Funkadelic, the psychedelic dimension is undeniable. AllMusic tags them as "Neo-Psychedelia" and "Deep Funk Revival." This is defensible.

2. **Nu-Funk** — The book defines Nu-Funk as "a modern funk revival scene that emerged in the mid-1990s, shaped by DJs and bands capturing the feel of rare funk 45s while making new records in that spirit." Khruangbin fits this description closely: they are a modern band making music deeply informed by vintage funk and soul-jazz 45s (especially Thai funk 45s). The 2025 release date reinforces this. AllMusic's "Deep Funk Revival" tag maps more directly to Nu-Funk than to Psychedelic Funk.

3. **Acid Jazz** — The book defines Acid Jazz as "a late-1980s and 1990s offshoot that revives 1970s jazz-funk for sampling and club culture. It keeps dancers moving with tight grooves, hooks, and atmosphere, not extended improvisation." Khruangbin shares the groove-first, atmosphere-heavy approach, and their music circulates heavily in similar DJ and crate-digging circles. However, the band emerged later than the classic acid jazz era, making this a looser fit.

**Recommendation:** Nu-Funk may be the most precise genre for this entry, given the book's own definition. Psychedelic Funk is also defensible if you want to emphasize the sonic palette over the historical context.
