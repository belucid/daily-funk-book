*Survival of the Fittest* was the Headhunters’ debut album as their own band, after years as Herbie Hancock’s sidemen. Herbie co-produced the record, but does not appear as a player.

Today's song, "God Make Me Funky," started as a radio jingle for Everett and Jones BBQ in East Oakland. Bassist Paul Jackson and drummer Mike Clark wrote it in 1971. Listeners kept calling Oakland radio station KDIA, asking about the commercial's music, so the band extended the commercial into a full-length track. The song's intro became one of the most sampled drum breaks in hip-hop history.

Some of the sample’s popularity comes from technical constraints in early sampling machines. They had limited recording time, and the clean drum and conga opening fit perfectly within those tight limits. Clark is on drums here, and he was born on October 3, 1946, in Sacramento.

If you have any friends who don’t get the whole year-long funk mission you’re on, play them this. It’s ten minutes of the absolute nastiest groove they’ll ever hear.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

**1. "Born in a BBQ joint"**
The song started life as a radio jingle for Everett and Jones BBQ in East Oakland, written by Paul Jackson and Mike Clark around 1971. Mrs. Jones asked them to record something to advertise her food on radio station KDIA, and callers kept phoning in asking about the music. That a jingle for a barbecue restaurant became one of the most sampled drum breaks in hip-hop history is a terrific narrative hook.

**2. "The broken snare that built hip-hop"**
During recording at Wally Heider Studios, Mike Clark's snare wires broke, making his snare sound like a tom. Producer David Rubinson motioned for them to keep playing. They later layered a wastebasket with a microphone to add a snare-like sound. This accident stripped out Clark's ghost notes and created the clean, stark drum pattern that samplers loved. The accidental quality of the break, and its outsized afterlife, is a compelling angle.

**3. "Hancock's band without Hancock"**
Survival of the Fittest was the Headhunters' debut as an independent act, separate from Herbie Hancock. Hancock co-produced and appears nowhere as a player. The fact that the sidemen's own record produced one of funk's most enduring grooves is a statement about the unsung rhythm section.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

**1.** "The drum intro sounds stripped bare for a reason: the snare wires broke mid-take. Listen to how Mike Clark and Bill Summers lock congas and drums into a single pulse. Can you hear the moment the Pointer Sisters enter and shift the track from instrumental jam to call-and-response gospel funk?"

**2.** "Paul Jackson's bass line anticipates the downbeat by a sixteenth note, pulling you forward before you even land. Try to feel where the bass sits against the kick drum. Is it ahead, behind, or right on top?"

**3.** "This groove was originally a BBQ jingle. Listen to the first 30 seconds of drums and congas alone. What makes this break so irresistible to hip-hop producers? Compare it to the 'Funky Drummer' break: what does Clark do differently from Clyde Stubblefield?"

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- The song originated circa 1971 as a radio jingle for **Everett and Jones BBQ** in East Oakland. Paul Jackson and Mike Clark lived next door and Mrs. Jones asked them to write something for radio station KDIA. Callers kept phoning the station asking about the music. — Source: https://bandonthewall.org/2018/08/inception-to-mixdown-headhunters-discuss-god-make-me-funky/
- During the studio recording, **Mike Clark's snare wires broke**, making the snare sound like a tom. Producer David Rubinson signaled to keep playing. They later layered a microphone on a wastebasket to add snare texture. This accident stripped out Clark's ghost notes and inadvertently created the clean, stark drum pattern that made the break so attractive to hip-hop samplers. — Source: https://bandonthewall.org/2018/08/inception-to-mixdown-headhunters-discuss-god-make-me-funky/
- The track has been sampled in **over 200 hip-hop and electronic tracks**, placing its drum break alongside Clyde Stubblefield's "Funky Drummer" and the Amen Break as one of the most-sampled in music history. Notable samplers include **N.W.A** ("Sa Prize, Pt. 2"), **Eric B. & Rakim** ("To the Listeners"), **Biz Markie** ("Albee Square Mall"), **De La Soul** ("Take It Off," "Pease Porridge"), **J Dilla** ("Jay Dee 15"), **Redman** ("Da Funk"), **Toni Braxton** ("How Many Ways"), **DJ Jazzy Jeff & The Fresh Prince** ("As We Go"), and **Digable Planets** ("Dog It"). — Sources: https://www.whosampled.com/The-Headhunters/God-Make-Me-Funky/sampled/ and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survival_of_the_Fittest_(album)
- The track's sampling popularity is partly due to a technical constraint: early sampling machines like the E-mu SP-12 and SP-1200 had very limited recording time, and the clean, sparse drum-and-conga intro of "God Make Me Funky" fit perfectly within those limitations. — Source: https://bandonthewall.org/2018/08/inception-to-mixdown-headhunters-discuss-god-make-me-funky/
- The band agreed that **all compositions on Survival of the Fittest would be credited equally** to all five members (Maupin, Summers, Jackson, Clark, McKnight), regardless of who wrote what, to ensure fair compensation. — Source: https://bandonthewall.org/2018/08/inception-to-mixdown-headhunters-discuss-god-make-me-funky/
- **The Pointer Sisters** appear on the track's chorus, chanting "anything you want!" Their involvement came through shared management: both groups were managed by David Rubinson and recorded at the same facility. The Pointer Sisters' appearance was courtesy of ABC/Blue Thumb Records. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survival_of_the_Fittest_(album)
- Paul Jackson sings lead vocals on the track; the lyrics have a gospel-inflected, spiritual quality, emphasizing dependence on God. — Source: https://alanlechusza.substack.com/p/good-news-god-made-me-funky-headhunters
- **Jamiroquai** covered the track live at Tokyo Club Citta in October 1993, though they reportedly sidestepped the full title's religious reference. — Source: https://alanlechusza.substack.com/p/good-news-god-made-me-funky-headhunters
- The Headhunters performed the track on **The Midnight Special** TV show. — Source: https://www.talkbass.com/threads/the-headhunters-god-make-me-funky-midnight-special.1672116/

#### Album
- *Survival of the Fittest* (1975) was the Headhunters' **debut as an independent act**, separate from Herbie Hancock. Hancock co-produced (with David Rubinson) but does not play on the album. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survival_of_the_Fittest_(album)
- Recorded at **Wally Heider Studios** in San Francisco. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survival_of_the_Fittest_(album)
- The album has been described as "the ultimate space-funk album." — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/survival-of-the-fittest-mw0000711443
- Guitarist **DeWayne "Blackbyrd" McKnight** was a new addition for this album. He had toured with Hancock and played on the Man-Child and Flood albums. McKnight later became musical director of **Parliament-Funkadelic** (1980-2008) and briefly played guitar for the **Red Hot Chili Peppers** in 1988 before John Frusciante replaced him. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeWayne_McKnight, https://rhcp.fandom.com/wiki/DeWayne_McKnight

#### Artist
- Herbie Hancock formed the Headhunters in 1973 after growing dissatisfied with his Mwandishi sextet, wanting a band with a "stronger funk component." He chose the name while doing **Buddhist chanting**, appreciating that it "made a triple reference to the jungle, to intellectual concerns, and to sexual activity." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Headhunters
- Their debut album as Hancock's band, *Head Hunters* (1973), was the **first jazz album to sell over one million copies** and reached #13 on the Billboard 200. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Headhunters
- **Mike Clark** was a child prodigy, sitting in with bands in Texas and New Orleans from age 4. He traveled the country with his father, a railroad union drummer. By his early twenties, he was recognized as a founder of the **East Bay Sound** coming out of Oakland. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Clark_(drummer)
- **Paul Jackson** played with the Oakland Symphony Orchestra at age 14, studied at the San Francisco Conservatory of Music, and co-wrote "Chameleon" (the lead track of Head Hunters, which became a jazz standard). He later moved to Japan and started a concert series called **Jazz for Kids** to teach Japanese students about African-American musical history. He died March 18, 2021, near Tokyo. — Source: https://www.npr.org/2021/03/19/978930638/paul-jackson-headhunters-bassist-who-played-with-herbie-hancock-dies-at-73
- **Bennie Maupin** played on Miles Davis's *Bitches Brew* (1970) before joining the Headhunters. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bennie_Maupin
- **Bill Summers** studied ethnomusicology at UC Berkeley and later worked on film scores for *The Color Purple* and the TV miniseries *Roots* with Quincy Jones. In the 1990s, he played with **Los Hombres Calientes** alongside Irvin Mayfield and Jason Marsalis. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Summers_(musician)

### Genre Suggestions

**1. Jazz-Funk (recommended)**
This is the most natural fit. The Headhunters are the textbook example of jazz musicians turning to funk. The band emerged from Herbie Hancock's jazz-fusion context, the album is classified as jazz-funk on AllMusic and Rate Your Music, and the track features extended improvisation over a funk groove with jazz instrumentation (bass clarinet, saxophone). Per the book's definition: "jazz funk is funk played by jazz musicians whose earlier careers were in more traditional jazz styles."
Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/survival-of-the-fittest-mw0000711443

**2. Classic Funk**
The track has a raw, stripped-down funk groove that sits comfortably alongside classic funk staples. The vocal hook, the gospel-inflected call-and-response with the Pointer Sisters, and the straightforward groove are more "classic funk" than cerebral jazz fusion. It falls within the 1968-1977 classic era.

**3. Deep Funk**
While the Headhunters were not obscure, this specific track's fame came primarily through its afterlife as a sampled break rather than commercial success on release. The track's delayed recognition through crate-digging and hip-hop sampling aligns with the deep funk definition of "delayed recognition." However, the Headhunters were on a major label (Arista), which makes this a weaker fit.
