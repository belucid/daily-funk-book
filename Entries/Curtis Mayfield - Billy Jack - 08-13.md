In the 1972 *Super Fly* soundtrack, Curtis Mayfield sang about dealers and addicts from inside their world. By 1975, on “Billy Jack,” he was telling the end of the story: the dealer lies dead in the street.

“Billy Jack” is six minutes of slow-funk storytelling with no chorus, no hook, and no obvious radio-friendly elements. Situated halfway through the 1970s, it sounds defeated. The violence of the 1980s crack era had not yet arrived, but the 1960s drugs-as-liberation dream was dead. They got Mayfield to perform at the funeral.

On August 13, 1990, a stage lighting rig fell on Mayfield at Wingate Field in Brooklyn, leaving him paralyzed. He continued to write and record, sometimes singing while lying on his back so gravity could help his diaphragm. He died in 1999 at the age of 57.

### Open Research Questions

**OPEN: What's the BPM tempo of Billy Jack?**
Approximately **82 BPM**, in C♯/D♭ minor, 4/4 time. This puts the track firmly in slow-funk territory (for reference, James Brown's "The Payback" sits around 91 BPM, and Mayfield's own "Pusherman" is around 93). SongBPM.com's algorithm also lists a double-time read of 164 BPM, which is a common artifact of automated analysis when the half-time feel is ambiguous; the slow reading matches every critical description of the track ("slow funk saga," "especially well-paced"). One other database (Tunebat, inaccessible directly during research but echoed in aggregated results) reports 143 BPM, which is inconsistent with how the track actually feels and is likely another algorithmic artifact. Recommend 82 BPM as the trustworthy figure. Note: the SongBPM track entry lists a 6:43 duration, longer than the LP's 6:10 and Spotify's 6:07, suggesting it indexed a compilation or extended version. (Sources: https://songbpm.com/@curtis-mayfield/billy-jack, https://jonjeffblogs.wordpress.com/2015/01/18/oldnew-albums-curtis-mayfields-theres-no-place-like-america-today/)

### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"Super Fly, three years later."** In 1972's Super Fly soundtrack, Mayfield voiced dealers and addicts from inside their world. By 1975, on "Billy Jack," he's telling the story from the morgue: the pusherman is face-down in the street. One critic called the track "Curtis Mayfield's reappraisal of his Super Fly attitudes." This framing lets the entry speak to the arc of Mayfield's social vision without making the reader do homework. (Source: https://www.whosampled.com/sample/4495/Black-Eyed-Peas-BEP-Empire-Curtis-Mayfield-Billy-Jack/)

- **"The slowest funk is sometimes the heaviest."** "Billy Jack" runs a deliberate six minutes with no chorus hook, no radio structure, just wah-wah guitars, Lucky Scott's wet bass, and Rich Tufo's horn charts circling a dead man. The entry could argue for tempo-as-emotion: funk as elegy rather than party. (Source: https://jonjeffblogs.wordpress.com/2015/01/18/oldnew-albums-curtis-mayfields-theres-no-place-like-america-today/)

- **"The album cover is the thesis."** The LP sleeve recolors Margaret Bourke-White's 1937 Life magazine photograph of Black flood victims queued beneath a billboard reading "World's Highest Standard of Living." "Billy Jack" is Track 1, Side A, the first answer the record gives to that image. This is a way in if the author wants to write about funk as social criticism in visual and musical conversation. (Source: https://www.artic.edu/articles/467/worlds-highest-standard-of-living)

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- Count how long Mayfield waits before the horn section fully commits. Rich Tufo's arrangement hangs back for most of the first verse, letting Lucky Scott's bass and Henry Gibson's congas set the crime scene. What does the patience of the arrangement do to the weight of the story?

- Curtis Mayfield almost never raises his voice here. Listen for the one or two moments when his falsetto tightens, when it sounds less like narration and more like someone who knew Billy Jack. Where does grief show up in the vocal?

- "Pusherman" in 1972 put you inside the dealer's head. "Billy Jack" in 1975 is about his funeral. Play them back to back and name what changed in Mayfield's point of view.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "Billy Jack" opens side A of the album and runs six minutes-plus, with no conventional verse-chorus structure, anchored by Lucky Scott's wah-wah bass, Henry Gibson's congas, and Rich Tufo's horn chart. — Source: https://jonjeffblogs.wordpress.com/2015/01/18/oldnew-albums-curtis-mayfields-theres-no-place-like-america-today/
- The hook lyric frames the character across two worlds: "Up in the city they called him Boss Jack / But down home he was an alley cat / Didn't care nothin' 'bout being Black." — Source: https://www.letras.com/curtis-mayfield/380541/
- DJ Premier sampled "Billy Jack" for Black Eyed Peas' "BEP Empire" on their 2000 album Bridging the Gap, before they became a global pop act. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/sample/4495/Black-Eyed-Peas-BEP-Empire-Curtis-Mayfield-Billy-Jack/
- Birdman and Lil Wayne's "1st Key" on their 2006 Cash Money album Like Father, Like Son also samples "Billy Jack." — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/sample/319495/Birdman-Lil-Wayne-1st-Key-Curtis-Mayfield-Billy-Jack/
- Philip Bailey of Earth, Wind & Fire covered "Billy Jack" on his 2019 album Love Will Find a Way, co-produced by Robert Glasper, with Kendrick Scott on drums. Rolling Stone described Bailey's version as a reggae-tinged brooder mixing West African influence and psychedelic touches. — Source: https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-features/earth-wind-and-fire-philip-bailey-curtis-mayfield-billy-jack-824840/
- Whether Mayfield's song is named for the 1971 Tom Laughlin film is unclear. The film's Billy Jack is a half-Navajo Vietnam veteran who defends a multiracial "Freedom School"; Mayfield's Billy Jack is a Black Chicago street figure gunned down for pocket change. The character profiles share little beyond the name, and no Mayfield interview found in research links the song to the film. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billy_Jack

#### Album

- The LP cover adapts Margaret Bourke-White's 1937 photograph "At the Time of the Louisville Flood," which ran in the February 15, 1937 issue of Life magazine. It showed Black flood victims queued for relief beneath a billboard of a smiling white family and the slogan "World's Highest Standard of Living" / "There's No Way Like the American Way." Mayfield swapped in the album title. — Source: https://www.artic.edu/articles/467/worlds-highest-standard-of-living
- The album peaked at #120 on the Billboard 200 and #13 on the Top R&B/Hip-Hop Albums chart, a commercial step down from Super Fly but still solid in R&B. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There's_No_Place_Like_America_Today
- It's Mayfield's seventh solo studio album and was included in 1001 Albums You Must Hear Before You Die and NME's 2013 "500 Greatest Albums of All Time" at #373. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There's_No_Place_Like_America_Today
- Core band: Curtis Mayfield (vocals, guitar, keys, producer), Phil Upchurch (guitar), Gary Thompson (guitar), Joseph "Lucky" Scott (bass), Quinton Joseph (drums), Henry Gibson (congas, bongos), Richard Tufo (keys, arranger). Recorded at Curtom Studios, Chicago. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There's_No_Place_Like_America_Today
- Singles released from the album were "So in Love / Hard Times" and "Love to the People / Only You Babe." "Billy Jack" does not appear to have been issued as a US single. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There's_No_Place_Like_America_Today

#### Artist

- Curtis Mayfield was born June 3, 1942 at Cook County Hospital in Chicago and grew up in the Cabrini–Green housing projects. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtis_Mayfield
- He co-founded Curtom Records in March 1968 with his manager Eddie Thomas, in Chicago. The label's name is a portmanteau of "Curtis" and "Thomas." By 1976, Curtom was grossing between $9–10 million annually. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtom_Records
- Mayfield tuned his guitar to an open chord (F#-A#-C#-F#-A#-F#, often called a "black key" or natural tuning) rather than standard, which shaped his distinctive chord voicings. — Source: https://www.britannica.com/biography/Curtis-Mayfield
- On August 13, 1990, a stage lighting rig fell on Mayfield at Wingate Field in Flatbush, Brooklyn, leaving him paralyzed from the neck down. He continued to write and record, lying on his back so gravity could help his diaphragm. He died December 26, 1999, in Roswell, Georgia, at age 57. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtis_Mayfield

### Genre Suggestions

- **Classic Funk.** "Billy Jack" fits squarely inside the book's "classic funk" window (1968–1977) and inside the commercial pipeline of a major-era Black music label (Curtom). It is not jazz-forward, not psychedelic, not gospel, not latin; it's funk as the primary identity of the track, with a Chicago studio band built around the groove. This is the cleanest fit per the book's own definition. (Source: On Genres & Regions.md; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There's_No_Place_Like_America_Today)

- **Jazz-Funk (secondary).** Rich Tufo's horn arrangement, the extended six-plus-minute length, and the non-radio structure lean jazz-funk. But the band is not a jazz band, and there's no extended improvisation; Tufo's charts are written, not blown. Classic Funk remains the better fit. (Source: https://jonjeffblogs.wordpress.com/2015/01/18/oldnew-albums-curtis-mayfields-theres-no-place-like-america-today/)

- **Psychedelic Funk (remote third).** Mayfield's falsetto narration and the track's swirling, hallucinatory arrangement could invite this label, and he was Clinton-adjacent in influence. But Mayfield's storytelling is grounded street realism, not cosmic allegory. Don't recommend this one.

**Recommendation: Classic Funk.**
