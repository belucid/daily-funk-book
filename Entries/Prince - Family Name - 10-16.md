“My fellow Americans, if there is a just God, we’re going to pay 4 this” is Thomas Jefferson as heard through a Prince Rogers Nelson lens. The song’s message: many African Americans have surnames, such as Nelson, that are inherited from their enslavers rather than their ancestors.

*The Rainbow Children* was Prince’s first studio album after reclaiming the name Prince, so it's likely that names were on his mind. After breaking free from Warner Bros. Records, his first move was uncommercial and preachy. He released it digitally as MP3s to NPG Music Club members on October 16, 2001, an early milestone in digital distribution by a major artist.

The album went almost completely unpromoted. It peaked at #109 on the *Billboard* 200 and became an insiders' album for devoted Prince fans. “Family Name” is a funky sermon on an album many casual Prince fans haven't heard.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "Family Name" runs 8:17 on the studio album and is constructed as a multi-part suite: an extended synthesized-voice "Akashic Records / Genetic Information Division" intro, a mid-tempo funk body with a repeated "but you still got your family name" hook, a ferocious guitar section that erupts around the 5:27 mark, and a spoken coda that includes a quote attributed to Thomas Jefferson: "My fellow Americans, if there is a just God, we're gonna pay 4 this." — Source: https://www.500princesongs.com/233-family-name/ and https://www.pastpri.me/home/prince-the-rainbow-children
- The song's central argument is that the surnames carried by most Black Americans were assigned by enslavers and are not ancestral, and the title phrase is used ironically: the "family name" in question is in part a record of erasure. The track dramatizes this with a scene set in "London, England, sometime in the early 1600s." — Source: https://www.pastpri.me/home/prince-the-rainbow-children
- A live arrangement of "Family Name" opens Disc 2 of *One Nite Alone... Live!* (the main-act-part-2 disc of Prince's first official live album), recorded in Portland on April 30, 2002. The live take runs 7:17, a full minute shorter than the studio version, largely by trimming the synthesized intro. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Nite_Alone..._Live!

#### Album

- *The Rainbow Children* was previewed as a single-file MP3 to NPG Music Club subscribers on October 16, 2001, before its physical release on November 20, 2001. Lead single "The Work, Pt. 1" was distributed via Napster, making Prince one of the first major artists to use the file-sharing service as a promotional channel. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rainbow_Children
- Prince deliberately suppressed promotion for the album. It peaked at only #109 on the Billboard 200, but hit #4 on Billboard's Independent Albums chart and eventually sold around 560,000 copies worldwide — a commercial success by indie standards, a flop by Prince standards. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rainbow_Children
- The album is Prince's most explicitly jazz-influenced studio record, built around live drums (John Blackwell on nearly every track), Najee on saxophone and flute, and the Fender Rhodes used prominently for the first time since the 1970s. Larry Graham plays bass on only two tracks; Prince plays nearly everything else himself. — Source: https://guitarcloud.org/era/rainbow-children/
- The cover painting, of an all-female choir-like ensemble, was commissioned from St. Louis artist Cbabi Bayoc and reflects the all-female gospel vocal group Milenia (the White sisters: Niyoki, Tia, and Malikah) who sing throughout the record. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rainbow_Children
- The original vinyl pressing was extremely limited and became a high-dollar collector's item. Sony Legacy reissued *The Rainbow Children* on crystal-clear double vinyl with a rainbow slipmat on May 29, 2020, the first vinyl reissue since the original. — Source: https://superdeluxeedition.com/news/prince-the-rainbow-children-and-one-nite-alone-reissues-on-cd-and-vinyl/

#### Artist

- *The Rainbow Children* was Prince's first album released under the name "Prince" since 1993, when he changed his name to the unpronounceable love symbol in a dispute with Warner Bros. He had reverted to "Prince" a year earlier, in 2000, when his Warner contract expired. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rainbow_Children
- The album's spiritual content came directly out of Prince's conversion to Jehovah's Witness belief in 2001, after a roughly two-year Bible-study relationship with bassist Larry Graham (ex–Sly and the Family Stone). Graham had relocated near Paisley Park in the late 1990s to continue the study sessions. Prince later described it not as conversion but as "a realization… like Morpheus and Neo in *The Matrix*." — Source: https://www.billboard.com/music/features/prince-jehovahs-witness-life-7348538/
- Prince was born Prince Rogers Nelson on June 7, 1958, at Mount Sinai Hospital in Minneapolis. He died April 21, 2016, at Paisley Park. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_(musician)

### Genre Suggestions

Current value is **Psychedelic Funk**. My read is that this genre *can* be made to fit, but is not the strongest choice.

1. **Psychedelic Funk (current).** The book defines psychedelic funk as funk groove fused with "surreal lyrics, colors and sounds, often leaning into alter egos, invented worlds, and hallucinatory storytelling." "Family Name" does have an invented world — the Akashic Records / Genetic Information Division framing, the sci-fi computer voice, the chant of "Rainbow Children" as a coined identity. The track is a surreal conceptual suite. However, the book also notes P-Funk and the Clinton universe as the center of gravity, and *The Rainbow Children* is stylistically quite far from that. **Verdict: defensible but stretched.**

2. **Minneapolis Sound.** The book's definition explicitly centers on Prince and the 1980s funk-rock/new-wave/synth-pop fusion "often utilizing the early drum machines of the 1980s." "Family Name" is 2001, live-drum, Fender Rhodes, jazz-tinged — it's Prince *away* from the Minneapolis sound rather than inside it. **Verdict: doesn't fit well, despite being Prince.**

3. **Jazz-Funk.** The album is overwhelmingly described as jazz-funk / jazz fusion: John Blackwell's live drums, Najee's sax and flute, the prominent Fender Rhodes, Larry Graham's bass, long track lengths, improvisational passages. The book's jazz-funk definition leans on 1970s records, but acknowledges the style as "funk played by jazz musicians… longer track lengths, room for extended improvisation." "Family Name" at 8:17 with its guitar extended outro fits the musical traits even if the era doesn't. **Verdict: strongest musical fit, if the era cutoff can flex.**

**Suggested:** Keep **Psychedelic Funk** if the editorial goal is to anchor the track's surreal conceptual frame, or switch to **Jazz-Funk** if the goal is to describe the actual sound of the band. My lean: **Jazz-Funk** is the more accurate descriptor of what a listener hears, and the book's era framing is soft enough to accommodate a 2001 track. Secondary option: leave as **Psychedelic Funk**.
