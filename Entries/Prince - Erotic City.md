### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The greatest B-side of the 1980s."** Frame the entry around the paradox: a track Prince slapped on the flip of "Let's Go Crazy" that ended up outlasting half the A-sides of its year. The editorial hook is about B-side culture, hidden funk, and the way Prince treated throwaway slots as a laboratory rather than a trash can.
2. **"Funk or f---?"** Build around the single most famous lyrical ambiguity in Prince's catalogue. Sheila E.'s claim that she was singing "funk" against Prince's other word is not just a censorship workaround — it's a tiny act of performer diplomacy that let the track sneak onto pop radio for years.
3. **"What a Parliament show does to a man."** Use Prince's own quote about seeing George Clinton at the Beverly Theatre ("frightening... 14 people singing 'Knee Deep' in unison. That night I went to the studio and recorded 'Erotic City.'") as the editorial spine. A rare direct lineage moment from P-Funk to Minneapolis sound.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. Prince layered at least three versions of his own voice on this track — a slowed-down baritone, his normal register, and a sped-up helium harmony, all produced by recording to tape at half-speed and flipping it. See if you can pick them apart as they trade the lead.
2. Listen for the moment the song stops being a Prince record and becomes a Prince-and-Sheila record. She doesn't enter as a backing vocalist — she arrives as a co-conspirator. Where does the balance tip?
3. The chorus contains the most contested four-letter word in funk radio history. Decide for yourself: is she singing "funk" or the other thing? Does it matter to the groove either way?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- Prince recorded "Erotic City" at Sunset Sound Studio 3 in Hollywood on March 25, 1984, the morning after seeing Parliament-Funkadelic at the Beverly Theatre. He later told the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1997: "I went to see [George Clinton] at the Beverly Theatre and it was frightening… 14 people singing 'Knee Deep' in unison. That night I went to the studio and recorded 'Erotic City.'" — Source: https://princesongs.org/2025/08/04/erotic-city/ ; https://ultimateprince.com/prince-erotic-city/
- The track is Sheila Escovedo's debut as a pop vocalist. She arrived at Sunset Sound expecting a percussion session and Prince told her, "We don't need any. You're going to sing with me." The studio had been dressed with candles and set up "like a living room." — Source: https://princesongs.org/2025/08/04/erotic-city/
- The chorus's disputed word has become funk folklore. Sheila E. has maintained that she sings "funk" while Prince sings the other word, giving the song radio-friendly plausible deniability. Despite this, the FCC fined Las Vegas station KLUC-FM $2,000 in 1989 for "at least sixteen apparent uses" of profanity, and multiple stations in San Antonio and Phoenix drew fines from 2004 onward. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erotic_City
- Prince's multi-character vocal was engineered by recording at half-speed with guitar parts in his natural register, then playing the tape back to normal speed. The result is one sonorous baritone (slowed down), his normal voice (slightly sped up), and a helium-pitched harmony voice — all the same Prince. Engineer Susan Rogers described the track's emotional range as "every emotion is a variation on 'horny.'" — Source: https://princesongs.org/2025/08/04/erotic-city/
- The track's bones are a LinnDrum (with reversed snare samples), a throbbing bass line, and a simple Oberheim OB-8 keyboard riff sometimes compared to a Touch-Tone phone. Prince played every instrument. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erotic_City ; https://princesongs.org/2025/08/04/erotic-city/
- Live debut: First Avenue, Minneapolis, June 7, 1984 — Prince's 26th birthday show with The Revolution. Sheila E. had flown in from California. The live version ran 8 minutes 31 seconds. — Source: https://archive.org/details/11-when-doves-cry ; https://www.setlist.fm/setlist/prince/1984/first-avenue-minneapolis-mn-3d855ff.html
- Prince reused the track in Spike Lee's *Girl 6* (1996) and it also appeared in *PCU* (1994). George Clinton and Parliament-Funkadelic covered it in 1994, closing the loop on its origin story. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erotic_City
- Critics have since reappraised the B-side as canonical. Rolling Stone ranked it #135 on the "200 Greatest Dance Songs of All Time" (2022); Slant placed it at #33 on "100 Best Dance Songs" (2020). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erotic_City

#### Album

- "Erotic City" was never released on a studio album. It lived as a non-LP B-side for nine years before appearing on the 1993 compilation *The Hits/The B-Sides* on Paisley Park/Warner Bros. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erotic_City
- The extended mix ("Make Love Not War Erotic City Come Alive") was reissued as part of the *Purple Rain Deluxe (Expanded Edition)* in June 2017, which collected extensive Vault material 33 years after the original album. — Source: https://discography.prince.com/albums/purple-rain-deluxe

#### Artist

- "Erotic City" was cut in the same white-hot Sunset Sound run that produced Sheila E.'s *The Glamorous Life* album, The Time's *Ice Cream Castle*, and Apollonia 6. Three days after recording "Erotic City" (March 28, 1984), Prince compiled Sheila's debut solo album tracklist. — Source: https://en.apoplife.nl/the-glamorous-life-the-complete-sheila-e-prince-story/
- Prince's affiliation with Sheila E. grew directly from this session. She became a long-term collaborator, Purple Rain tour opener, and Prince's drummer across multiple tours and bands. On the *Purple Rain* tour she performed "Erotic City" in her opening set with Prince singing his part from offstage. — Source: https://princesongs.org/2025/08/04/erotic-city/
- For decades, "Erotic City" was uniquely hard to hear: Prince's long-running streaming boycott kept most of his catalogue off Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube, SoundCloud, Deezer, and Tidal until his estate began restoring the catalogue following his death in April 2016. — Source: https://www.digitalmusicnews.com/2016/04/21/why-prince-hated-spotify/ ; https://www.billboard.com/music/music-news/prince-streaming-spotify-apple-amazon-pandora-purple-rain-7686209/

### Genre Suggestions

1. **Minneapolis Sound (current, keep).** This is the textbook case: lean synth-funk built on a LinnDrum pocket, Oberheim synth riff, one-man-band instrumentation, high-gloss production. Everything the vault glossary describes as Minneapolis Sound is present here. — Source: `Matter/On Genres & Regions.md`
2. **Classic Funk.** If the editor wants to foreground the P-Funk-lineage angle or the commercial-pipeline case (this was a Warner Bros. single attached to the best-selling album of Prince's career), Classic Funk is defensible — though the 1984 release date sits slightly past the 1968–1977 "classic era" boundary the book sets for itself. Weaker fit by the book's own definition.
3. **Electro Funk.** The reversed-snare LinnDrum programming, synthetic bass, Oberheim hook, and sparse futuristic texture all flirt with electro funk vocabulary. Worth a mention as a secondary label even if the editor keeps Minneapolis Sound as primary.

Primary recommendation: **keep Minneapolis Sound.** It's the cleanest genre call.
