 *Machine Gun* was the Commodores’ debut album, released on July 22, 1974. Unlike the ballad-heavy LPs that later made them famous, with songs like “Easy” and “Three Times a Lady,” it's a wall-to-wall funk record. Today’s song, “I Feel Sanctified,” captures the band at their funkiest, before the Lionel Richie ballads took over.

The all-white funk band Wild Cherry covered “I Feel Sanctified” two years later, planning to release it as the A-side of a single. For the flip side, they included a quick throwaway tune called “Play That Funky Music.” The label flipped when they heard the B-side and released "Play That Funky Music" as a single instead. It became a monster hit, reaching #1 on the *Billboard* Hot 100.

Wild Cherry released their “I Feel Sanctified” cover in 1977, but it failed to chart. Nothing they did before or since "Play That Funky Music" had much commercial success, and they became funk's quintessential one-hit wonder.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"Before they got soft."** Machine Gun is the Commodores' debut, and unlike the ballad-heavy LPs that would follow ("Easy," "Three Times a Lady," "Still"), it is wall-to-wall funk with no slow songs. "I Feel Sanctified" captures the band at their hardest-grooving, before Lionel Richie's balladeer instincts took over. This frames the entry as a corrective: the Commodores were a serious funk band first.
2. **"Sacred language for secular love."** The lyric uses the vocabulary of gospel (sanctified, blessed) to describe a man who has been spiritually rewired by his girlfriend's love. The a cappella intro lands like a church call before the Salvation-Army-gone-funk drum kicks in. The hook: how funk borrowed gospel's emotional vocabulary without ever stepping into the church.
3. **"The B-side that almost was."** Wild Cherry's Rob Parissi originally planned to record this as the A-side of a single, with a throwaway tune called "Play That Funky Music" on the flip. The label flipped them. Frame the entry around that near-miss alternate history and what it tells us about how this track was perceived in 1976.

The B-side became a Number One pop hit; the cover of "I Feel Sanctified" was released as a single in 1976 and failed to chart. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_Cherry_(band) ; https://www.songfacts.com/facts/wild-cherry/play-that-funky-music

#### Album

- Machine Gun is the Commodores' debut LP, released July 22, 1974, on Motown. The Commodores recorded the new sessions in early 1974 at Motown Recording Studios in Hollywood. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_Gun_(Commodores_album) ; https://www.adampwhite.com/westgrandblog/2017/7/31/the-commodores-wingman-1
- Unlike every Commodores album that followed, Machine Gun has no ballads. AllMusic's Alex Henderson: "What you won't find on Machine Gun are a lot of sentimental love ballads." — Source: https://www.allmusic.com/album/machine-gun-mw0000739193
- Robert Christgau gave it a B+: "The first side is good straight hard funk, kicked off by a title instrumental that's the best thing on the record, sure sign of a good straight hard funk band." — Source: https://www.robertchristgau.com/get_album.php?id=6614
- The instrumental title track "Machine Gun" became a sports-arena staple and later appeared in Boogie Nights (1997). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_Gun_(Commodores_album)
- The drum break from "The Assembly Line" became a heavily used sample in hip-hop, drum and bass, and jungle. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_Gun_(Commodores_album)

#### Artist

- The Commodores formed at Tuskegee Institute (now Tuskegee University) in Alabama in 1968 from two student groups, the Mystics and the Jays. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodores ; https://encyclopediaofalabama.org/article/the-commodores/
- William King picked the band's name by flipping open a dictionary at random. They narrowly avoided being called "The Commodes." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodores
- Suzanne de Passe, the same Motown executive who had developed the Jackson 5, put the Commodores on tour as the J5's opening act in 1971. They signed to Motown's MoWest label in mid-1971 and to Motown proper in November 1972. — Sources: https://www.adampwhite.com/westgrandblog/2017/7/31/the-commodores-wingman-1 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodores
- The original lineup: Lionel Richie (vocals, sax, keys), Thomas McClary (lead guitar), Milan Williams (keys), William King (trumpet), Ronald LaPread (bass), Walter "Clyde" Orange (drums). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodores
- Keyboardist Milan Williams died July 9, 2006. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodores

### Genre Suggestions

1. **Classic Funk (primary).** This is the textbook case for the book's "Classic Funk" definition: a major-label, mainstream-pipeline funk record on Motown, charting on the R&B Top 20, released inside the 1969-1977 classic era, with funk as its primary identity rather than a fusion. The track is a Motown hit single from a Motown band's debut LP, full stop.
2. **Gospel Funk (secondary, conceptual fit).** The book defines gospel funk as either gospel acts getting funky or funk acts "making a joyful noise for the Lord." "I Feel Sanctified" is not literally gospel, but it borrows wholesale from gospel: the title's sanctification language, the a cappella three-part-harmony intro that lands like a hymn, and Record World's "Salvation Army drum" comparison. If the book wants a Trojan-horse gospel-funk pick (a secular funk track wearing church clothes), this is a strong candidate. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_Gun_(Commodores_album)

Recommendation: lead with Classic Funk. If the author wants to hold a clean classic-funk slot for one of the band's bigger hits and use this entry to highlight the gospel-borrowing angle, Gospel Funk is defensible.
