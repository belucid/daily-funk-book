### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"The instrumental on the B-side of every James Brown song."** Brown was a vocalist defined by screams, grunts, and call-and-response — yet "Ain't It Funky Now" is an instrumental that became one of his most-sampled records. The angle: how Brown's groove was so self-sufficient that the voice was optional, and how the band, not the front man, made this one a hit.
- **"The loop before the loop."** Recorded a year before "Funky Drummer," and built on a single repeating Jimmy Nolen guitar figure and a Clyde Stubblefield/Jabo Starks pocket, the track anticipates the way hip-hop producers in the 1980s would literally lift and loop a single bar. The angle: the record itself is already a loop; the samplers just made the loop explicit.
- **"Cincinnati, not Augusta."** James Brown is identified with Georgia (Macon, Augusta) and the South, but the actual sound — these records — was cut on Brashear Avenue in Cincinnati at Syd Nathan's King Studios. The angle: the underrated role of a Midwestern country-and-bluegrass label in shaping the geography of funk.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- Brown is officially playing organ on this one. Try to hear him stop playing. The organ drops out for long stretches while the rhythm section keeps going — note what happens to the groove when the bandleader steps back and lets Jimmy Nolen and the drums carry it.
- Count the bars between any meaningful chord change. The whole track lives on one vamp. What does the band do *inside* that single chord to keep nine and a half minutes from getting boring?
- Listen for the seam between Part 1 and Part 2 (the single was split mid-jam for the 45). Can you hear the edit? On the album version it's smoothed over — try to spot where the side-flip would have been on the original 7-inch.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- "Ain't It Funky Now" is a funk instrumental — unusual for a James Brown A-side, which were almost always vocal-led. Brown is credited on organ rather than vocals. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_It_Funky_Now
- The two-part single (King 45-6280) peaked at #3 R&B and #24 Pop on Billboard, spending three months on the charts after a November 1969 release. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_It_Funky_Now and http://redkelly.blogspot.com/2007/01/james-brown-aint-it-funky-now-part-2.html
- The track is built almost entirely on a single repeating guitar figure by Jimmy Nolen, anticipating the literal "loop" concept that hip-hop producers would later impose on Brown's catalogue. — Source: http://redkelly.blogspot.com/2007/01/james-brown-aint-it-funky-now-part-2.html
- The B-side (Part 2) differs from the A-side by lacking the organ and horn bridge sections present on Part 1 — a glimpse of the arrangement being assembled on the fly in the studio. — Source: http://redkelly.blogspot.com/2007/01/james-brown-aint-it-funky-now-part-2.html
- The song has been sampled in dozens of tracks, including Beastie Boys' "Hey Ladies" (1989) and DJ Cam Quartet's "Saint Germain" (2007). — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/James-Brown/Ain't-It-Funky-Now/
- A live version recorded at the Olympia in Paris in 1971 was eventually released on the *Love Power Peace* album (1992). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_It_Funky_Now
- Jimmy McGriff cut a jazz-organ cover of the song on his 1970 LP *Soul Sugar*, produced by Sonny Lester — a quick acknowledgement of the track's groove from the jazz organ community. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Jimmy-McGriff/Ain't-It-Funky-Now/
- Notably, the track was *not* included in the *Star Time* box set (the canonical 4-CD James Brown anthology) except as part of a live "Brother Rapp" medley — an odd omission for a top-5 R&B hit. — Source: http://redkelly.blogspot.com/2007/01/james-brown-aint-it-funky-now-part-2.html

#### Album

- *Ain't It Funky* (King KS-1092) was Brown's 27th studio album, released in January 1970, and is largely a compilation of sessions cut between May 1967 and October 14, 1969. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_It_Funky
- The album reached #43 on the Billboard 200 — modest by pop standards, but it sits at a pivot point in Brown's catalogue between the late-60s soul-funk and the harder 1970s JBs sound. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_It_Funky
- Recording locations included King Studios in Cincinnati, Criteria Studios in Miami, and RCA Studios in Los Angeles — Brown was effectively recording on the road. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_It_Funky
- Side B of the album includes an instrumental remake of the 1967 vocal hit "Cold Sweat" — Brown was cannibalising his own catalogue for grooves. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_It_Funky
- Guitarist Kenny Poole was brought in to overdub guitar parts on several tracks ("Cold Sweat," "Give It Up or Turnit a Loose," "Use Your Mother," "After You Done It"), effectively replacing vocal tracks with new guitar lines. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_It_Funky

#### Artist

- James Brown was born May 3, 1933 in Barnwell, South Carolina, in a one-room wooden shack. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Brown
- He died on December 25, 2006 in Atlanta, age 73, of heart failure complicated by pneumonia. — Source: https://www.britannica.com/biography/James-Brown-American-singer
- The 1969–1970 lineup that defined this era of his band included Maceo Parker (tenor sax), Fred Wesley (trombone), Jimmy Nolen (guitar), Alphonso "Country" Kellum (guitar), "Sweet" Charles Sherrell (bass), and the two drummers — Clyde Stubblefield and John "Jabo" Starks. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_It_Funky
- A "famous mutiny" by Maceo Parker and most of the band occurred in early 1970, just weeks after these sessions; Brown immediately replaced them with the young Collins brothers, forming the new JBs that would record "Sex Machine." — Source: http://redkelly.blogspot.com/2007/01/james-brown-aint-it-funky-now-part-2.html

### Genre Suggestions

- **Classic Funk (strongest).** The 1969–1977 classic-funk window per *On Genres.md* is a perfect fit. This is funk released into the mainstream commercial pipeline by a major(-for-its-day) label, hit the R&B top 5 and Pop top 25, and the track's primary identity is funk — no rock, jazz, or Latin fusion. James Brown is the archetypal classic-funk artist; this is one of his archetypal classic-funk records.
- **Deep Funk (weak alternative).** Probably not appropriate per the book's definition: this single hit #3 R&B at release, so it was not "overlooked on release and only found their audience later." However, the track's later life — heavily sampled by crate-digging hip-hop producers in the late 80s — sits adjacent to the deep-funk story. Worth mentioning, not recommending.
- **Soul Jazz (very weak).** The instrumental, organ-led arrangement and extended length share DNA with soul-jazz organ records of the period, and the Jimmy McGriff cover the next year reinforces the kinship. But the track lacks jazz improvisation in any meaningful sense — it's a one-chord vamp, not a soul-jazz vehicle for solos. Not recommended.

**Recommendation: Classic Funk.**
