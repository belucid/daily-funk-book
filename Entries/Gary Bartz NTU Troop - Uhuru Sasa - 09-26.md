---
“NTU” is a Bantu word for “unity in all things,” and the “Troop” in the band’s title included the audience. People were invited to bring their own percussion to concerts to participate in the music-making. The band was often deliberately free of chorded instruments (no keys, no guitar), giving Bartz maximum freedom to play any note on the sax at any time without clashing.

Gary Bartz, born on this day in 1940, infused his music with a democratic spirit. That didn’t mean he was running a casual drum circle. Bartz was steeped in jazz history and craft. His father ran a jazz club in Baltimore called the North End Lounge, and Bartz said he committed to jazz after hearing Charlie Parker as a kid. True to his mission, he played with Charles Mingus and Art Blakey before forming NTU Troop with vocalist Andy Bey. Miles Davis came calling soon after.

The title of today’s track, “Uhuru Sasa,” means “freedom now” in Swahili. Beyond the social and political message, it also represents the band’s deliberate attempt to strip away the harmonic constraints of Western music. With no chord progressions guiding the way, Bartz’s saxophone is free to go anywhere.

### Open Research Questions

Open: when did Gary form NTU Troop and how old was he then?

**Research resolution:** The timeline is slightly tangled in secondary sources. Many sources (KCCK, Forced Exposure, Musicology blog) state NTU Troop was "formed in 1970." However, the group's first album, *Home!*, was recorded live at a Left Bank Jazz Society concert in Baltimore on **March 30, 1969**, and is credited to "Gary Bartz NTU Troop." This means the group existed by at least early 1969. The confusion likely arises because *Home!* was released in 1970, and that's when the name became publicly visible. Bartz was born September 26, 1940, so he was **28 turning 29** when the group performed the Home! concert in March 1969. Wikipedia's Gary Bartz article does not give an explicit formation date but lists *Home!* (1969) as the first NTU Troop entry in the discography.

Best answer: NTU Troop was active by at least March 1969 (when Bartz was 28). The group was likely formed in late 1968 or early 1969.

1. **"Freedom now, no chords allowed"** — The title "Uhuru Sasa" means "freedom now" in Swahili, and the band's deliberate elimination of chordal instruments (no piano, no guitar) was itself an act of liberation. The musical structure mirrors the political demand: strip away the harmonic constraints of Western music, and the saxophone is free to go anywhere. Two kinds of freedom in one track.

2. **"The anti-war protest you can dance to"** — Andy Bey's vocal refrain, "Hell no, we won't fight your filthy battles," is one of the most direct anti-Vietnam War statements in jazz-funk. But it rides Harold White's "wickedly funky" drums. The tension between the message's urgency and the groove's pleasure is the entry's hook.

3. **"From Miles to Malcolm"** — Bartz went from playing the Isle of Wight with Miles Davis in front of 600,000 people (August 1970) to recording this album dedicated to Malcolm X just months later (November 1970 / January 1971). That pivot from the biggest gig in rock history to a small-group political funk record in a few months is a vivid character story.

### Genre Suggestions

The current genre is `Free Funk`.

1. **Free Funk** (current value) — A reasonable choice. The band's no-chords policy and improvisational approach align with the genre definition ("when adventurous avant-garde jazz players pushed into funk"). Bartz's connections to Mingus, Blakey, and Miles Davis's electric period place him in the lineage of adventurous jazz players who embraced funk grooves. The track sits in a space between funk groove and jazz freedom.

2. **Jazz-Funk** — Also a strong case. The track features jazz musicians (Bartz, Ron Carter) playing over a funk groove with room for extended improvisation, which matches the book's definition: "jazz funk keeps key jazz habits inside a funk groove: longer track lengths, room for extended improvisation." The 6:48 runtime, the soloing over a groove, and the pedigree of the players all point here. Wikipedia categorizes the album as "jazz-funk."

3. **Soul Jazz** — A weaker but defensible case. The book defines soul jazz as "one of the clearest bridges between hard bop jazz and funk" with "a more danceable groove." The NTU Troop's small-group format and the absence of chordal instruments recall the smaller ensembles of soul jazz. However, the political intensity and improvisational freedom push it beyond the "early simmer" that soul jazz represents in the book's framework.

The strongest case is probably between Free Funk and Jazz-Funk. Free Funk emphasizes the band's deliberate structural freedom (no chords, audience participation, African philosophical framework). Jazz-Funk emphasizes the musical result (jazz musicians grooving hard). The author's choice depends on which aspect they want to foreground.
