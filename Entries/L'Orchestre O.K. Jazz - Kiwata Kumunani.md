### Open Research Questions

No `open:` markers were present in the entry. One open item surfaced by research:

- **Composer credit ambiguity.** The Editions Populaires sleeve credits Franco as composer; the YouTube upload titled "Kiwita Kumunani (Trad. arr. Franco) - Franco & L'O.K. Jazz 1969" attributes the song as traditional, arranged by Franco. This is consistent with Franco's documented practice of arranging Kongo folkloric material into rumba arrangements. I could not find a definitive third-party musicological source resolving "original" vs. "traditional arrangement." Sources consulted: Discogs master/release credits, YouTube upload (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fXk0HXXH-vc), Wikipedia on Congolese rumba (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congolese_rumba). Recommend treating it as "trad., arr. Franco" if the author wants to lean into the folkloric angle.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"Funk by latitude, not by lineage."** Kiwita Kumunani has nothing to do with James Brown — and yet it appears on Analog Africa's *Congo Funk!* compilation in 2024, retroactively recruited into the funk canon by a Western label looking for "sound madness" on the Congo River. The piece could examine how compilations like *Congo Funk!* both honor and reshape what gets called funk. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_Funk!
2. **"The OK Jazz school."** OK Jazz wasn't just a band, it was, in Wikipedia's phrase, "a musical academy of sorts, a finishing school for talent" that produced Verckys Kiamuangana Mateta, Sam Mangwana, and dozens more (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congolese_rumba). Use Kiwita Kumunani as the entry point into the conservatory-without-walls that fed half of Congolese popular music.
3. **"Authenticité on a 45."** 1969 is squarely in Mobutu's Authenticité moment, which pushed Congolese artists toward indigenous roots. A Lingala-language, possibly folk-derived OK Jazz B-side from that exact year is a perfect lens on how state ideology and musical fashion converged. Source: https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/literature-and-arts/music-popular-and-jazz-biographies/franco

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. **"This was a B-side."** The A-side, "Nzorba," is the one collectors chase. Play both back to back (the A-side is on YouTube too) and decide for yourself why the flip was the throwaway. What does the sebene break tell you about where the band put its energy?
2. **"Find the rumba inside the funk."** Listen for the moment, roughly two minutes in, when the rhythm guitar drops out and a single-line lead guitar starts answering the vocal. That is the *sebene* — the Congolese rumba's instrumental bridge — and it is the thing that makes a Western ear file this under "groove." Notice how it never resolves into a James Brown one.
3. **"Two voices, one band."** Youlou Mabiala and Franco share the vocal. Try to track which singer is doing what — Franco is the lower, gruffer voice; Mabiala the lighter, higher one. The interplay is half the song.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- The track was originally released as the **B-side of "Nzorba"** on a 7" 45 RPM "splattered" (multi-color) vinyl pressing — visually striking enough that auction listings tag it "SPLASH WAX / MONSTER AFRO GROOVE" — Source: https://www.popsike.com/FRANCO-OK-JAZZ-Kiwita-Kumunani-7-SPLASH-WAX-MONSTER-AFRO-GROOVE-/251441930157.html
- Vocals are by **Youlou Mabiala and Franco** trading lines — Source: https://www.discogs.com/release/29134093-Orch-OK-Jazz-Nzorba-Kiwita-Kumunani (Discogs release credits via linked source recording)
- The YouTube reissue uploads the title as **"Kiwita Kumunani (Trad. arr. Franco)"**, meaning Franco is credited as arranger of a traditional song rather than composer — a hint that the song's melodic material may be folkloric Kongo material — Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fXk0HXXH-vc
- Curated onto **Analog Africa's *Congo Funk!*** compilation (2024) as the **earliest recording on the album** — every other track is from 1970-1982 — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_Funk!

#### Album

- There is **no original album**: the song existed only as a 1969 7" single on Editions Populaires (Ep.11, DR Congo) and was reissued in France on Fonior/Decca's Fiesta imprint (cat. 51.010) — Source: https://www.discogs.com/master/3432722-Orch-OK-Jazz-Nzorba-Kiwita-Kumunani
- It later appeared on the compilation **"Franco 1966/1969"** (Sonodisc) — Source: https://music.apple.com/us/song/kiwita-kumunani/1065646579
- And in 2024 on Analog Africa's **"Congo Funk! - Sound Madness From The Shores Of The Mighty Congo River (Kinshasa/Brazzaville 1969-1982)"**, curated from roughly 2,000 candidate songs — Source: https://analogafrica.bandcamp.com/album/congo-funk-sound-madness-from-the-shores-of-the-mighty-congo-river-kinshasa-brazzaville-1969-1982-analog-africa-no-38

#### Artist

- **OK Jazz was founded in June 1956** in Léopoldville (now Kinshasa), named after the **OK Bar** where its founding members played — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TPOK_Jazz
- **Franco (François Luambo Luanzo Makiadi)**, born 6 July 1938 in Sona-Bata, was only 18 when he helped found the group; he became its undisputed lead guitarist immediately, co-leader in 1967, and sole leader in 1970 after Vicky Longomba's departure — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco_Luambo
- **Rolling Stone (2023)** ranked Franco at **#71 on its list of the 250 Greatest Guitarists of All Time** — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco_Luambo
- The band was officially renamed **TPOK Jazz** ("Tout Puissant Orchestre Kinois de Jazz") in **1971** — meaning the 1969 Kiwita Kumunani single is from the late "OK Jazz" period, before the rename — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco_Luambo
- Mobutu later titled Franco **"Grand Maître"** — a title normally reserved for judges, professors and sorcerers — and awarded him the Grand Order of the Leopard medal — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco_Luambo
- Franco died **12 October 1989** at Mont-Godinne Hospital in Yvoir, Belgium, leaving 18 children; he received a state funeral in Zaire on 17 October 1989 — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco_Luambo

### Genre Suggestions

The current value is **Afrobeat**, which per the book's own definition ("Afrobeat is used as a broad label for any funk rooted in Africa") is defensible. But Kiwita Kumunani is more precisely **Congolese rumba** than Afrobeat, and the book has no Congolese rumba genre. Suggestions:

1. **Afrobeat (keep).** Per the book's broad definition, this is the only genre tag in the enumeration that fits African funk-adjacent music. Source: Matter/On Genres.md, Afrobeat definition. Pragmatic, consistent with how the book treats non-Nigerian African material.
2. **Deep Funk (alternative).** Kiwita Kumunani is a textbook deep-funk story: a 1969 B-side, overlooked on release, only found its Western audience in 2024 via Analog Africa's crate-dig compilation. "The defining trait of deep funk is delayed recognition" (Matter/On Genres.md). The track meets that criterion exactly — but the genre is normally reserved for American/UK rare-groove finds, so it may strain the category.
3. **No good fit (case for new tag).** If the book accumulates enough Congolese material (this entry, Lolo Soulfire, possibly others), a sub-tag like "Congolese rumba" or "Soukous" would describe it more honestly than Afrobeat. Worth flagging for the author.

Recommendation: keep **Afrobeat** unless the author wants to make the deep-funk pivot the writing theme.
