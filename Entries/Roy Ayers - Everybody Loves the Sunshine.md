### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"The most sampled summer in funk."** The title track has been sampled roughly 200 times (Tupac, Mary J. Blige, Dr. Dre, and many more) — a frame about how a single mellow groove became foundational architecture for hip-hop and neo-soul.
- **"Godfather of Neo Soul."** Ayers is widely tagged with this title; the entry could trace the line from this 1976 track forward to D'Angelo and Robert Glasper, both of whom covered it.
- **"Sunshine in the shade."** The song's warmth comes from an unusual source — Philip Woo's ARP synth and Ayers' vibes over a near-static, almost ambient groove. A theme about how restraint, not energy, creates the feeling of summer.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- Listen for the ARP synthesizer line (Philip Woo, ARP Odyssey) that opens and floats through the track. It does almost nothing harmonically — why does it feel like sunlight rather than just a riff?
- This track barely moves: no chorus build, no climactic solo. Count how many times the vocal hook repeats and ask what holds your attention when a song refuses to "go" anywhere.
- You have almost certainly heard this groove resampled in hip-hop or neo-soul. Try to hear it as 1976 listeners did, before it became a sample — does it sound like a beat waiting to be lifted, or a finished whole?

### Genre Suggestions

- **Jazz-Funk (recommended).** Discogs styles the album "Jazz-Funk / Funk"; Ayers is the textbook jazz-musician-turns-funk figure, and this is the most commercially visible jazz-funk of the 1970s. Fits the book's jazz-funk definition (jazz players, jazz habits inside a funk groove, 1970s). — Source: https://www.discogs.com/master/118092-Roy-Ayers-Ubiquity-Everybody-Loves-The-Sunshine
- **Classic Funk.** Falls within the 1969–1977 classic era with major-label (Polydor) distribution and chart presence; a defensible secondary tag, though the track's identity is more jazz-fusion than core funk.
- **Acid Jazz.** Not for the original 1976 track, but worth noting: Ayers was a foundational figure to the late-80s/90s acid jazz movement that revived exactly this sound. Better as a connective note than a tag.

Recommendation: **Jazz-Funk.**
