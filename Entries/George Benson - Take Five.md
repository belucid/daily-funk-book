“Take Five” was composed by Paul Desmond and recorded by the Dave Brubeck Quartet in 1959. It became a surprise hit when it was reissued in 1961, reaching #25 on the *Billboard* Hot 100 and becoming the first jazz single to sell over a million copies.

Over a decade later, Phil Upchurch suggested a “Take Five” cover and wrote the funky arrangement. George Benson was not interested until he heard what Upchurch had done. If you’ve got time, listen to Brubeck’s original, then compare it to Benson's version. What makes Upchurch’s arrangement feel funky?

The original “Take Five” convinced a wider audience that jazz can groove in 5/4 time. Benson and Upchurch proved funk could groove in 5/4 too, and they landed a minor hit of their own. *Bad Benson* reached #1 on the *Billboard* jazz chart. Later pressings of *Bad Benson* were retitled *Take Five* to emphasize the track’s presence on the album.

## Research Notes

### Open Research Questions

**Q: When was Brubeck's Take Five originally released?**

"Take Five" was composed by Paul Desmond and recorded by the Dave Brubeck Quartet on July 1, 1959 at CBS 30th Street Studio in New York City, produced by Teo Macero. It was released as a promotional single on September 21, 1959, and appeared on the album *Time Out*. The single was reissued on May 22, 1961, when it became a sleeper hit, reaching #25 on the Billboard Hot 100 and #6 on the UK Singles Chart. It became the first jazz single to surpass one million in sales. — Sources: [Wikipedia: Take Five](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Take_Five), [Dave Brubeck Jazz: Time Out & Take Five Facts](http://www.davebrubeckjazz.com/Take-Five-&-Time-Out/Time-Out-&-Take-Five-Facts)

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"Funk in five"** — The original "Take Five" was revolutionary for proving jazz could groove in 5/4 time. Benson and Phil Upchurch went further, proving funk could groove in 5/4 too. The entry could explore the audacity of taking the most famous odd-meter jazz tune and making it swing with a funk pocket, something that shouldn't work but does.

2. **"Phil Upchurch's dare"** — Upchurch suggested the "Take Five" cover and conceived the funky arrangement. Benson reportedly didn't want to do it until he heard Upchurch's take. The entry could frame the track around Upchurch's role as the hidden catalyst, and his recent passing in November 2025 makes this a timely tribute angle.

3. **"The last great CTI session"** — Bad Benson hit #1 on the Billboard jazz chart in 1974, the same year Billboard named CTI jazz label of the year. Within months, the label was collapsing in lawsuits. The entry could frame this track as a peak moment before the fall, a high-water mark for Creed Taylor's empire.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- The track is in 5/4 time, one of very few funk recordings in an odd meter. The original "Take Five" was composed by Paul Desmond after drummer Joe Morello challenged Desmond to write something in 5/4. Benson's version maintains the 5/4 feel but layers in a funk rhythm section. — Source: [Wikipedia: Take Five](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Take_Five)
- Phil Upchurch conceived the funky arrangement and suggested Benson cover "Take Five." Benson was reportedly reluctant until he heard Upchurch's arrangement. — Source: [Dusty Groove: George Benson Take Five](https://www.dustygroove.com/item/816007/George-Benson:Take-Five-portrait-cover)
- The track runs 7:08, more than double the length of Brubeck's 5:24 original, giving Benson extended room for soloing. — Source: [Wikipedia: Bad Benson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Benson)
- The track was recorded on May 29, 1974 at Van Gelder Studio in Englewood Cliffs, New Jersey. — Source: [Wikipedia: Bad Benson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Benson)

#### Album
- *Bad Benson* reached #1 on the Billboard jazz chart, making it Benson's biggest commercial success before *Breezin'* (1976). — Source: [Wikipedia: George Benson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Benson)
- The album was reissued in 1979 under the title *Take Five* (CTI 8014), renaming the entire album after its opening track. This means the same album exists under two different titles. — Source: [Doug Payne CTI Records page](https://www.dougpayne.com/ctiagb.htm)
- 1974 was the year Billboard named CTI "jazz label of the year," but the label was already heading toward financial collapse. By 1975, CTI faced severe financial difficulties, and Benson left for Warner Brothers, sparking litigation that lasted a decade. — Source: [CT Produced: George Benson](https://www.ctproduced.com/otd-george-benson-paid-his-jazz-dues/)
- Another track from the album, "The Changing World," became one of the most sampled jazz-funk tracks in hip-hop history. Common's "I Used to Love H.E.R." (1994) samples it. — Source: [WhoSampled: Bad Benson](https://www.whosampled.com/album/George-Benson/Bad-Benson/)
- The session was produced by Creed Taylor and engineered by Rudy Van Gelder at his famous studio. — Source: [Wikipedia: Bad Benson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Benson)

#### Artist
- Benson was born March 22, 1943 in the Hill District of Pittsburgh, PA. He started performing publicly at age 4, winning a singing contest in 1947 and performing on radio as "Little Georgie Benson." — Source: [Wikipedia: George Benson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Benson)
- His hands were too small for guitar as a child, so he started on ukulele at age 7. — Source: [Wikipedia: George Benson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Benson)
- At 19, Benson joined Jack McDuff's band, where he spent two and a half years learning harmony and improvisation. He credited this period as crucial to his development. — Source: [CT Produced: George Benson](https://www.ctproduced.com/otd-george-benson-paid-his-jazz-dues/)
- Phil Upchurch, who played rhythm guitar and bass on this album, died on November 23, 2025 at age 84 in Los Angeles. He was born July 19, 1941 in Chicago. Over his career he appeared on more than 1,000 recordings, including work with Curtis Mayfield (*Superfly*, *Claudine*, *Let's Do It Again*, *Sparkle*), Donny Hathaway, Chaka Khan ("I'm Every Woman"), and Michael Jackson ("Workin' Day and Night"). — Source: [Variety: Phil Upchurch obituary](https://variety.com/2025/music/news/phil-upchurch-dead-guitarist-composer-1236599436/)
- Steve Gadd, the drummer on the session, was in the middle of a prolific run at CTI Records in 1973-74, backing Milt Jackson, Chet Baker, Art Farmer, Jim Hall, and Hubert Laws. — Source: [Wikipedia: Steve Gadd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Gadd)
- Ron Carter, the bassist, is one of the most recorded bassists in jazz history. — Background knowledge, widely documented.
- Benson has won 10 Grammy Awards and was named an NEA Jazz Master in 2009. — Source: [Wikipedia: George Benson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Benson)

### Genre Suggestions

The current genre is **Jazz-Funk**, which is strong. Here are considerations:

1. **Jazz-Funk (recommended, keep current)** — This is the textbook case: a jazz guitarist on a jazz label, covering a jazz standard, with a jazz rhythm section, but the groove is unmistakably funk. The extended track length (7:08), room for improvisation, and the players' jazz pedigrees all fit the book's definition of jazz-funk. — Source: Genre definitions in On Genres & Regions.

2. **Soul Jazz** — The album leans on the organ-trio/small-combo tradition, and Benson's early career with Jack McDuff puts him squarely in the soul jazz lineage. However, the 1974 date and the CTI big-production sound (Don Sebesky arrangements, full horn and string sections) push it past the "early simmer" of soul jazz into the "full boil" of jazz-funk per the book's definitions.

3. **Classic Funk** — At a stretch: the track hit #1 on the Billboard jazz chart, not the R&B or pop charts, and the performers are jazz musicians. The funk is real, but the identity is jazz-funk, not classic funk.
