It’s time to warn you about a peculiar side effect of funk exposure. It’s called “stank face.” It’s an involuntary reaction to the groove, when your mouth and nose scrunch up like you just smelled something truly nasty. If you're unsure what I mean, stand in front of a mirror and play today’s track, Prince’s Grammy-winning “Musicology” from his 2004 album of the same name. As the strings and congas fade and Prince’s squeal ushers in the drums and bass, notice what your face does. That’s stank face. Don’t worry, it’s not permanent.

“Musicology” is Prince in full retro mode, an “old school joint” aimed at the “true funk soldiers.” He shouts out a few of his heroes to educate the younger crowd, including James Brown, Sly Stone, and Earth, Wind and Fire. And just past the four-minute mark, he drops in a quick sample of his own “Kiss.” Is he placing himself in the funk canon?

The album was released on April 20, 2004. To support it, Prince and his team ran a Musicology tour promotion that provided a copy of the CD with every concert ticket purchased. The tour was the highest-grossing of 2004, so it had the effect of boosting album sales tremendously. As with so many things, Prince’s innovation rewrote the rules, and the bundling strategy became an industry standard. After Prince’s success, bundling became so common that *Billboard* eventually tightened its rules around ticket and merch bundles.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The man who changed the chart rules"**: The Musicology album's ticket-bundling strategy was so effective it forced Billboard and Nielsen SoundScan to rewrite their charting methodology. This is a story about Prince bending the music industry to his will, not just artistically but commercially, and it opens an angle about how funk artists have always had to hustle outside the system.

2. **"Maceo, blow your horn"**: Prince hired James Brown's legendary saxophonist Maceo Parker for this album and tour, then shouted the same command Brown made famous: "Maceo, blow your horn!" This is a story about a direct, physical passing of the funk torch, not just a lyrical shout-out to heroes but putting them onstage beside you.

3. **"The funk professor"**: Prince explicitly frames this song as a lesson, a "musicology" class where he's both student and teacher of the funk tradition. The entry could explore Prince's role as a bridge between the 1970s originators he shouts out and a 2004 audience that may have never heard of Sly Stone.

#### Track
- "Musicology" self-samples Prince's own "Kiss" (1986) at the 4:13 mark, making it both a tribute to his funk heroes and a sly self-canonization. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/sample/182613/Prince-Musicology-Prince-The-Revolution-Kiss/

- The song won the Grammy for Best Traditional R&B Vocal Performance at the 47th Grammy Awards (February 13, 2005), Prince's first R&B Grammy win since 1987. — Source: https://www.grammy.com/news/prince-musicology-album-20th-anniversary-legacy-tour
- The song was recorded in November 2002 at Paisley Park, Chanhassen, Minnesota. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musicology_(song)

#### Album
- Prince's ticket-bundling strategy (a free CD with every concert ticket) was so successful it forced Billboard and Nielsen SoundScan to change their charting rules. Future bundled releases had to offer customers an opt-out. Musicology was grandfathered in. This strategy became industry-standard: by 2017, over a third of Billboard 200 chart-toppers used it. — Source: https://www.grammy.com/news/prince-musicology-album-20th-anniversary-legacy-tour
- Just two days after Musicology's release, Prince dropped two additional albums (The Chocolate Invasion and The Slaughterhouse) exclusively online. — Source: https://www.grammy.com/news/prince-musicology-album-20th-anniversary-legacy-tour
- The album was Prince's first top-10 since 1995's The Gold Experience, reaching #3 on the Billboard 200. It went double platinum (2 million copies) by January 2005. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musicology_(album)
- Some of the album's oldest material dates back to 1996-1997: "If Eye Was The Man In Ur Life" originated in 1996, and "A Million Days" was created during sessions for The Truth in May 1997. — Source: https://goldiesparade.co.uk/discography/albums/musicology/
- The album was recorded primarily at Metalworks Studios in Mississauga, Ontario (a suburb of Toronto), where Prince had a new speaker system installed in studio #6 to hear exactly how he envisioned the playback. Prince had relocated to Canada in 2002 after marrying his second wife Manuella Testolini. — Source: https://goldiesparade.co.uk/discography/albums/musicology/
- The 2019 reissue by Sony Legacy Recordings was the first-ever vinyl pressing of the album, released as a purple double LP. — Source: https://goldiesparade.co.uk/discography/albums/musicology/
- Clare Fischer, who wrote string arrangements for Prince from 1985 onward without the two ever meeting, contributed string arrangements to the album. Fischer was given total creative freedom. — Source: https://theiconicprince.wordpress.com/2017/03/16/clare-fischer-prince-a-winning-combination/

#### Artist
- Prince initially thought Maceo Parker, James Brown's legendary saxophonist, had died. Candy Dulfer corrected him at Paisley Park, saying "He's very much alive and doing great." Prince's response: "Get him over here." Parker went on to become Prince's closest musical collaborator for the next decade. — Source: https://ultimateprince.com/prince-maceo-parker/
- Prince adapted James Brown's famous stage command for Parker: instead of Brown's "Maceo, blow your horn!", Prince got to say the same line to the same man. — Source: https://ultimateprince.com/prince-maceo-parker/
- Parker called his time with Prince his number one musical achievement, above his decades with James Brown and Parliament-Funkadelic. — Source: https://ultimateprince.com/prince-maceo-parker/
- The Columbia Records deal was structured as a joint venture where NPG Records retained ownership and Columbia handled manufacturing and distribution only. Prince said: "I got a chance to structure an agreement the way I saw fit instead of the other way around." — Source: https://www.today.com/popculture/prince-got-everything-he-wanted-new-deal-wbna10467211
- Prince opened the 46th Grammy Awards (February 8, 2004) with Beyonce in a legendary medley of "Purple Rain," "Baby I'm a Star," "Crazy in Love," and "Let's Go Crazy" seen by 26 million viewers. The performance was not re-aired for over a decade due to licensing issues, making it one of the most mythologized TV performances of the era. — Source: https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-news/flashback-prince-and-beyonces-dazzling-grammy-performance-188130/
- The Musicology Live 2004ever tour grossed $87.4 million across 77 dates, becoming 2004's highest-grossing tour and selling over 1.47 million tickets. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musicology_(album)

### Genre Suggestions

1. **Minneapolis Sound** (current value): This fits well. Prince is the defining artist of the Minneapolis Sound, and Musicology was recorded partly at Paisley Park. However, the track itself is deliberately retro-facing, paying tribute to 1970s funk rather than showcasing the synth-forward, drum-machine-driven characteristics typically associated with Minneapolis Sound. The genre assignment works more because of who made it than how it sounds.

2. **Classic Funk**: The song is explicitly an homage to "true funk soldiers" like James Brown, Sly Stone, and Earth, Wind & Fire. It features congas, horns, live drums, and a groove rooted in early 1970s funk vocabulary. Musically, it sounds more like a classic funk track than a Minneapolis Sound track. However, the book's definition ties Classic Funk to roughly 1968-1977, and this is a 2004 recording.

3. **Nu-Funk**: As a 2004 track that deliberately revives the feel of rare funk 45s while making new music in that spirit, "Musicology" could qualify as nu-funk. The book defines nu-funk as "a modern funk revival scene" with artists "capturing the feel of rare funk 45s while making new records in that spirit." This fits the track's intent, though Prince is far bigger than the nu-funk scene.

**Recommendation**: Minneapolis Sound is the strongest choice given Prince's centrality to that genre and the book's framing, even if the track itself leans classic funk in its sound.
