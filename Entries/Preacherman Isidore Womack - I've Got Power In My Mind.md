### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **"The hustler-preacher with the trunk full of 45s."** Lean into the image documented by Funky Virginia: a man in Richmond, Virginia, walking into record stores to sell his own gospel-funk 45 face-to-face, "with lofty expectations" that the record industry never matched. The track was crate-dug back to life four decades later. This is deep funk's defining narrative in miniature.
- **"Gospel funk as testimony, not church music."** Womack's lyric ("I've got power in my mind / I've got loving in my heart… all love goes to my God") preaches without a pulpit, sermon structure, or choir. The writing theme could compare this kind of personal-testimony funk to the institutional gospel sound it sits next to on Greg Belson's compilation.
- **"What 'self-released' meant in 1978."** Before Bandcamp, before SoundCloud, the only way an obscure artist got a record into circulation was to press it themselves and walk it into shops. Frame the entry around the economics and labor of self-distribution that produced almost all the "deep" / "rare groove" finds collectors prize.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- Listen for the guitar in the first thirty seconds. The Jazzman / Cultures of Soul copy calls this "lightly funky soul" with "great guitar riffs" — decide for yourself whether the guitarist is leading the song or just propping up Womack's vocal.
- Womack moves between secular love-song language ("loving in my heart") and outright praise ("all love goes to my God") in the same verse. Where does the song cross from soul into gospel, and when?
- This was a man selling his own 45 door-to-door in Richmond. Listen for the seams — the places where the recording feels homemade — and ask whether they hurt the song or are the whole point of it.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- The track was recorded in 1978 at Alpha Audio on Broad Street in Richmond, Virginia, the same room Bruce Springsteen used for a WGOE radio broadcast in May 1973. — Source: https://jazzmanrecords.co.uk/preacherman-isidore-womack-ive-got-power-in-my-mind and http://brucebase.wikidot.com/gig:1973-05-31-alpha-sound-studios-richmond-va
- The original 1978 B-side was "Every Time I Think Of You" — a different song than the "I Don't Want To Be Alone" by Allen Gauff Jr. that appears as the B-side on the 2021 Cultures of Soul reissue. — Source: http://funkyvirginia.blogspot.com/2008/07/preacherman-isidore-womack-ive-got.html
- The lyric hook — "I've got power in my mind / I've got loving in my heart… all love goes to my God" — frames the song as a piece of personal testimony rather than congregational gospel. — Source: https://jazzmanrecords.co.uk/preacherman-isidore-womack-ive-got-power-in-my-mind

#### Album

- The track has no original album. It exists on a 1978 self-released 45 and on the 2021 compilation *Greg Belson's Divine Funk – Rare American Gospel Funk and Soul* (Cultures of Soul Records), where it closes the LP as track 12. — Source: https://culturesofsoul.bandcamp.com/album/greg-belsons-divine-funk-rare-american-gospel-soul-and-funk
- *Divine Funk* is curated by British gospel-music collector and DJ Greg Belson and follows his earlier compilation *Greg Belson's Divine Disco: American Gospel Disco 1974-1984*. The Womack track is sequenced as the closer to twelve rare gospel-funk obscurities. — Source: https://www.serendeepity.net/product/preacherman-isidore-womack-allen-gauf-jr-ive-got-power-in-my-mind-i-dont-want-to-be-al/
- The compilation runs only 40 minutes for 12 tracks — most of them are short, radio-length pieces meant to do their work fast. — Source: https://culturesofsoul.bandcamp.com/album/greg-belsons-divine-funk-rare-american-gospel-soul-and-funk

#### Artist

- Per the Funky Virginia blog (the only deep-dive biographical source located), Womack would walk into Richmond-area record shops in person to sell copies of his self-pressed 45, and he held "lofty expectations" about a career that never broke through. — Source: http://funkyvirginia.blogspot.com/2008/07/preacherman-isidore-womack-ive-got.html
- Womack is described as testifying "in his own unique way," with "real talk for grown ups about struggle in life and love" — i.e. neither orthodox gospel nor straightforward secular soul, but a one-man hybrid. — Source: http://funkyvirginia.blogspot.com/2008/07/preacherman-isidore-womack-ive-got.html
- Last.fm has no biographical information on Womack at all — its page currently asks the community for any background — which underlines just how obscure he remained until Greg Belson re-surfaced the record in 2021. — Source: https://www.last.fm/music/Preacherman%20Isidore%20Womack

### Genre Suggestions

- **Gospel Funk (current, retain).** This is the textbook example. Womack is a self-styled "Preacherman" delivering personal religious testimony over a funk groove on a self-released 45 that crate-diggers rediscovered decades later. He fits *On Genres'* description of gospel funk almost word-for-word: "a gospel act getting seriously funky on a track."
- **Deep Funk (secondary case).** *On Genres* defines deep funk as "less a sound than a commercial fate" — overlooked at release, rediscovered by crate-digging DJs. Womack's 1978 self-released 45, rescued by Greg Belson in 2021, is essentially the textbook example of that fate. The author could reasonably tag this as both gospel funk and deep funk, but gospel funk is the more specific and informative label.
- **Classic Funk (weak case, not recommended).** The 1978 date falls just inside *On Genres'* "classic era" (1969–1977) on the late edge, but Classic Funk is defined as the music that was in the "commercial pipeline of major labels, well-distributed singles and albums, radio play, concert tours, and chart presence." Womack's record had none of that — it was hand-sold out of his trunk. Mention only to dismiss.
