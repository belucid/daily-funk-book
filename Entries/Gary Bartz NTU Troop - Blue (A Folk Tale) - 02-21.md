OK, things are about to get a bit weird. Bear with me. The juice is worth the squeeze.

At eighteen minutes, today's track "Blue (A Folk Tale)" takes up the entire first side of *Harlem Bush Music - Uhuru*. Not all of it is funky, and that is the point. If you keep an open mind and stay with it, you will hit a stretch that might be one of the best free funk passages ever put on tape. As a bonus, there is country blues, free jazz, and modern blues living inside the same long arc.

Bartz dedicated the album to civil rights activist Malcolm X, assassinated on February 21, 1965. Bartz said he used to see Malcolm regularly in Harlem, especially at the Shabazz Restaurant, where Malcolm would stop after working at the *Muhammad Speaks* newspaper office. He also saw him at Louis Michaux's bookstore, where Malcolm would discuss Black history and politics.

### Open Research Questions

open: more details on the ties between Malcom X and Gary Bartz and this album.

**Resolved.** The connection between Gary Bartz and Malcolm X was personal, not just ideological. Here are the key findings:

**Personal encounters in Harlem (early 1960s):** Bartz regularly encountered Malcolm X in Harlem. He and fellow musicians would go to the Shabazz Restaurant at 116th Street and Lenox Avenue, knowing what time Malcolm would arrive after working at the Muhammad Speaks newspaper office. Bartz recalls: "He would come in every day about that time from the Muhammad Speaks office...he would just give a warm greeting." Bartz also saw Malcolm at Louis Michaux's bookstore near the Theresa Hotel, where Malcolm discussed Black history and politics. Malcolm valued young people's perspectives and would sometimes ask them, "What do you think about this?" — Source: https://tedpanken.wordpress.com/2011/09/26/its-gary-bartzs-71st-birthday-three-wkcr-interviews-from-the-90s/

**Spiritual impact:** Bartz identifies Malcolm X and John Coltrane as the two people who most profoundly affected him through their presence alone: "Two people in my life have affected me like that, Malcolm X and John Coltrane. There was nothing I could say that could make me sound like I was really saying something to them." Elsewhere he has described them as having "a Christ-like aura" or as "Buddha figures" for "the way they made me feel, that I was in the presence of a spiritual being." — Source: https://tedpanken.wordpress.com/2011/09/26/its-gary-bartzs-71st-birthday-three-wkcr-interviews-from-the-90s/ ; https://hokesjukebox.com/2025/06/25/jazz-master-gary-bartz-looks-back-and-ahead/

**The dedications:** The two-volume Harlem Bush Music is dedicated to both figures: Taifa to Malcolm X, and Uhuru to John Coltrane. The liner notes read: "The music of both volumes of Harlem Bush Music (Taifa and Uhuru) is humbly dedicated, respectively, to the memories of Malcolm X and John Coltrane." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlem_Bush_Music

**Malcolm X's words on the album:** On "The Warrior's Song" (from Taifa), Bartz overdubs himself reading an excerpt from a Malcolm X speech about demanding political change rather than passively waiting for it. — Source: https://jazzdesk.wordpress.com/2021/08/29/a-gary-bartz-snapshot-of-1971/ ; http://jazztruth.blogspot.com/2010/08/gary-bartz-and-ntu-troop-look-back.html

**Max Roach as a bridge:** Bartz credits working with Max Roach, whom he describes as "very socially conscious and was a friend of Malcolm's," as a key catalyst in developing his own political consciousness and the NTU Troop concept. — Source: https://tedpanken.wordpress.com/2011/09/26/its-gary-bartzs-71st-birthday-three-wkcr-interviews-from-the-90s/

**The NTU Troop as social commentary:** Bartz has described the group explicitly: "the Ntu Troop was a social commentary group." The combination of Malcolm X's political urgency and Coltrane's spiritual searching is the philosophical DNA of the entire Harlem Bush Music project. — Source: https://tedpanken.wordpress.com/2011/09/26/its-gary-bartzs-71st-birthday-three-wkcr-interviews-from-the-90s/

**Relevance to this date (02-21):** Malcolm X was assassinated on February 21, 1965. Placing this track on 02-21 directly honors the dedication.

**Track title note (resolved):** The track title has been corrected to "Blue (A Folk Tale)" per all sources.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The whole side of the record"**: Blue (A Folk Tale) occupies the entire first side of the LP, an 18-minute suite that moves through country blues piano, free sax, grinding funk, and urban blues. The theme is about ambition, about a jazz musician in 1971 saying "I'm going to put the entire Black musical experience on one side of vinyl." This connects to Bartz's philosophy that genre walls are artificial, and that NTU means "unity in all things."

2. **"Hearing Charlie Parker at six"**: Bartz heard a Charlie Parker record at age six and said, "This is what I want to do with my life." His father ran a jazz club in Baltimore where the teenage Gary sat in with Sonny Stitt. This entry could frame the track as the product of a life marinated in music from birth, a musician who played with Mingus, Blakey, and Miles Davis before he was 30, and then used all of it to build something new.

3. **"Dedicated to Malcolm and Trane"**: Uhuru is dedicated to Malcolm X and John Coltrane. The track weaves political consciousness and spiritual searching into a blues framework. The theme is about how the blues can carry revolutionary content when the right musician plays it.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- "Blue (A Folk Tale)" takes up the entire first side of the LP. It begins with only bass and Bartz's vocals over piano "in a style quite reminiscent of Thelonious Monk," then builds through free saxophone, funk, and urban blues. Bartz overdubs his own voice to create layered vocal arrangements. — Source: http://blog.musoscribe.com/index.php/2017/10/14/album-review-gary-bartz-ntu-troop-harlem-bush-music-uhuru/
- The track bounces between the vocals of Bartz and Andy Bey and the group's most energetic bursts, functioning as the album's theatrical opening statement. — Source: https://floodmagazine.com/45873/gary-bartz-ntu-troop-harlem-bush-music-uhuru/
- A listener on Twitter compared Kendrick Lamar's "To Pimp a Butterfly" (2015) directly to "Harlem Bush Music," specifically comparing "Complexion" to "Blue (A Folk Tale)" for their shared ambition of encompassing the breadth of Black musical experience. — Source: https://twitter.com/franklinmorton/status/585200067411337216

#### Album
- Uhuru is dedicated to Malcolm X; its companion volume Taifa is dedicated to John Coltrane. Together the two-volume Harlem Bush Music project aimed to "encompass the vastness of the black experience in the early 1970s." — Source: https://floodmagazine.com/45873/gary-bartz-ntu-troop-harlem-bush-music-uhuru/
- The album was recorded at Decca Sound Studios in New York in November 1970 and January 1971, produced by Orrin Keepnews, who had co-founded Milestone Records in 1966. Bartz was the first young artist Keepnews signed to the label. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orrin_Keepnews ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlem_Bush_Music
- Ron Carter (not Juni Booth, who played bass on the Taifa sessions) plays bass on Uhuru. Carter was one of the most recorded bassists in jazz history. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlem_Bush_Music
- "Vietcong" features Andy Bey singing "Hell no, we won't fight your filthy battles," directly connecting the anti-war and civil rights movements. — Source: https://floodmagazine.com/45873/gary-bartz-ntu-troop-harlem-bush-music-uhuru/
- "Celestial Blues" from the same album has been sampled by Black Sheep ("To Whom It May Concern"), Bolts ("Universe"), RPM ("Food of My De-Rhythm"), and DJ Day feat. Aloe Blacc ("Closer"). Andy Bey later recorded his own cover of it. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Gary-Bartz/Celestial-Blues/

#### Artist
- Gary Bartz heard a Charlie Parker record at age six and declared, "This is what I want to do with my life." His parents bought him an alto sax at age eleven. — Source: https://www.arts.gov/honors/jazz/gary-bartz
- His father Floyd ran a jazz club in Baltimore. When Gary was 14, saxophonist Sonny Stitt, tipped off by Bartz's proud father, called the teenager up on stage to sit in, his first public performance. — Source: https://www.arts.gov/honors/jazz/gary-bartz
- Bartz went to Juilliard in 1958 but was drawn more by NYC's jazz scene than by formal study. He sometimes slept on the subway while pursuing gigs. — Source: https://www.kqed.org/arts/13935159/8-over-80-gary-bartz
- He played in Charles Mingus's Jazz Workshop (1962-1964), with Max Roach and Abbey Lincoln, and with Art Blakey's Jazz Messengers (1965) before joining Miles Davis's band in mid-1970. He performed at the Isle of Wight Festival with Davis (August 1970) and the Cellar Door sessions (December 1970), both recorded for Davis's Live-Evil album. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Bartz ; https://www.arts.gov/honors/jazz/gary-bartz
- NTU is a root word from the Bantu language meaning "unity in all things, time and space, living and dead, seen and unseen." The "Troop" was not just the band but the audience too: Bartz encouraged listeners to bring percussion and participate. He wanted a group that could "play anywhere, even in the middle of the jungle." — Source: https://www.capitalbop.com/gary-bartz-interview-library-of-congress/ ; https://floodmagazine.com/45873/gary-bartz-ntu-troop-harlem-bush-music-uhuru/
- The NTU Troop was deliberately chord-less (no piano or guitar in the regular lineup), relying on implied harmonies and pedal-point techniques. This gave Bartz's alto a dark, reedy freedom to blend blues, bebop, folk melodies, and Coltrane-influenced vocabulary naturally. — Source: http://jazztruth.blogspot.com/2010/08/gary-bartz-and-ntu-troop-look-back.html
- Bartz rejects the word "improvisation," preferring "informal composition": "We're not improvising. We're composing, all the time. You don't study something for 50 years just to go make stuff up." — Source: https://www.kqed.org/arts/13935159/8-over-80-gary-bartz
- A Tribe Called Quest sampled Bartz's "Gentle Smiles (Saxy)" for their 1991 track "Butter." — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/sample/2686/A-Tribe-Called-Quest-Butter-Gary-Bartz-Gentle-Smiles-(Saxy)/
- In 2021, Bartz collaborated with Adrian Younge and Ali Shaheed Muhammad on Jazz Is Dead 6, introducing his music to a new generation of hip-hop and funk listeners. — Source: https://www.jazzisdead.com/garybartz
- Bartz struggled with heroin addiction during his New York years but overcame it and devoted himself fully to music. He describes himself as a "born-again musician." — Source: https://www.kqed.org/arts/13935159/8-over-80-gary-bartz
- He has been Professor of Jazz Saxophone at Oberlin Conservatory since 2001 and was named a 2024 NEA Jazz Master. — Source: https://www.arts.gov/honors/jazz/gary-bartz
- He listens to Frank Sinatra every day. — Source: https://www.kqed.org/arts/13935159/8-over-80-gary-bartz

### Genre Suggestions

The current genre is **Free Funk**. Here are suggestions with rationale:

1. **Free Funk** (current, strong fit): The book defines free funk as what happens "when adventurous avant-garde jazz players like Ornette Coleman and James 'Blood' Ulmer pushed into funk." Bartz came from Mingus, Blakey, and Miles Davis's most adventurous period, and this track's extended free alto solo section over a funk rhythm section fits the definition precisely. The funk section of the track is genuinely nasty, but it arrives through free jazz territory.

2. **Jazz-Funk**: The book defines jazz-funk as "funk played by jazz musicians whose earlier careers were in more traditional jazz styles," with "longer track lengths, room for extended improvisation, and a rhythm section built to support soloists." At 18 minutes with extended saxophone improvisation, featuring Ron Carter on bass, this checks every box. The album is filed as "Jazz-Funk" on both Wikipedia and Rate Your Music. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlem_Bush_Music ; https://rateyourmusic.com/release/album/gary-bartz-ntu-troop/harlem-bush-music-uhuru/

3. **Soul Jazz**: The book defines soul jazz as "one of the clearest bridges between hard bop jazz and funk," with "jazz improvisation and blues harmonies" shifted toward "a more danceable groove." The track's blues foundation and the chord-less group format (reminiscent of organ trio freedom) place it in soul jazz territory, especially its opening and closing blues passages. However, the track's length and adventurousness push it beyond the "early simmer" the book associates with soul jazz.

**Recommendation**: Free Funk remains the strongest fit given the track's structure and the book's definitions. Jazz-Funk is the most common external classification. The author should weigh whether the free sections or the funk sections define the track's identity for this book.
