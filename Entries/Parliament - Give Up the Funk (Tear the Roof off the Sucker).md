### Writing Theme Suggestions

- **The first funk record to go platinum, on a label everyone thought of as a disco shop.** Casablanca Records is remembered for Donna Summer and the Village People; "Give Up the Funk" was Parliament's first million-selling single, and Mothership Connection their first gold and then platinum album. The entry can frame the track as the moment funk forced its way into the room where disco was supposed to live.
- **A chant before a chorus.** The song is built on a call-and-response chant rather than a verse-chorus structure. There is no narrative; the chant *is* the song. A good angle for talking about why so much of P-Funk reduces lyrics to communal incantation, and how the chant pulled audiences across the line from listeners to participants.
- **Three writers, three jobs.** George Clinton (concept and showmanship), Bootsy Collins (bass and pocket), and Jerome "Bigfoot" Brailey (drums) each get a writing credit. The entry can use the songwriting split to show how P-Funk credits worked when the groove itself was the composition, and how Brailey's name on the credits foreshadows the bitter "Mr. Wiggles" feud he later wrote against Clinton.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

- Count how many distinct vocal lines are layered into the chant by the song's halfway point. P-Funk doesn't write a chorus; it builds a crowd. When does the studio stop sounding like a band and start sounding like a stadium?
- Listen for Ray Davis's bass-voice intro: "We want the funk, give up the funk." That low spoken-sung announcement is the song's actual hook before anything melodic happens. How much of the track's power is set by those first seven seconds?
- The single edit (Casablanca NB 856, 3:39) trims the album cut down by more than two minutes. Listen to both back-to-back. What did the radio edit lose, and what, if anything, did the tighter version gain?

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track

- Released as the second single from Mothership Connection in April 1976 (Casablanca NB 856, b/w "P. Funk (Wants to Get Funked Up)"), it peaked at #5 on the Billboard Hot Soul Singles chart and #15 on the Billboard Hot 100. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Give_Up_the_Funk_(Tear_the_Roof_off_the_Sucker)
- Certified Gold by the RIAA on October 19, 1976, becoming Parliament's first million-selling single. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Give_Up_the_Funk_(Tear_the_Roof_off_the_Sucker)
- Written by George Clinton, Bootsy Collins, and drummer Jerome "Bigfoot" Brailey; produced by Clinton. Ray Davis delivers the spoken bass-voice intro. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Give_Up_the_Funk_(Tear_the_Roof_off_the_Sucker)
- The single edit runs 3:39 and "begins without the 'tear the roof off the sucker' intro." The album cut is 5:46. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Give_Up_the_Funk_(Tear_the_Roof_off_the_Sucker)
- The official A-side title is "Tear the Roof Off the Sucker (Give Up the Funk)" — the words flip between the label copy and the album's table of contents. — Source: http://www.45cat.com/record/nb856
- Heavily covered and sampled: Grandmaster Flash ("Tear the Roof Off"), Gerardo ("We Want the Funk", 1991), Positive Force ("We Got the Funk"), MC Hammer, plus a Snoop Dogg / Bootsy Collins / Fred Wesley cover in 2002. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Parliament/Give-Up-the-Funk-(Tear-the-Roof-Off-the-Sucker)/sampled/
- Vice President Kamala Harris used the track in her 2024 campaign rally rotation. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Give_Up_the_Funk_(Tear_the_Roof_off_the_Sucker)

#### Album

- Mothership Connection is Parliament's fourth studio album, released December 15, 1975 on Casablanca Records (NBLP 7022), and was Parliament's first gold and first platinum LP. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mothership_Connection
- Recorded March–October 1975 at United Sound Systems in Detroit and Hollywood Sound in Hollywood, produced by George Clinton, engineered by Jim Vitti (Detroit) and Ralph "Oops" Jim Callon (Hollywood). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mothership_Connection
- The album peaked at #13 on the Billboard 200 and #4 on the Billboard R&B Albums chart. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mothership_Connection
- It was the first Parliament album to feature horn players Fred Wesley and Maceo Parker, both freshly arrived from James Brown's J.B.'s. The session also includes the Brecker Brothers (Michael and Randy). — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mothership_Connection
- Selected by the Library of Congress for the National Recording Registry in 2011 for having "had an enormous influence on jazz, rock and dance music." — Source: https://www.loc.gov/item/2007576772/mothership-connection-album/
- Ranked #276 on Rolling Stone's 2012 "500 Greatest Albums of All Time," #363 on the 2020 list, and named VH1's 55th-greatest album. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mothership_Connection
- Concept: "a pimp sitting in a spaceship shaped like a Cadillac" - an afrofuturist arrival narrative that gave rise to the Mothership stage prop, a touring set piece whose rebuilt 1990s version now lives in the Smithsonian. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mothership_Connection

#### Artist

- George Edward Clinton was born July 22, 1941, in Kannapolis, North Carolina. He formed The Parliaments as a Plainfield, NJ doo-wop group in 1955, rehearsing in the back room of a barbershop where he straightened hair. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Clinton_(funk_musician)
- Parliament-Funkadelic was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1997 with 16 members honored together. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Clinton_(funk_musician)
- Bootsy Collins (William Earl Collins) was born October 26, 1951, in Cincinnati, Ohio. He rose to fame in James Brown's band in the late 1960s before joining the Parliament/Funkadelic collective in 1972. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bootsy_Collins
- Drummer Jerome Eugene "Bigfoot" Brailey was born August 20, 1950, in Richmond, Virginia. He co-wrote "Give Up the Funk" and is ranked #48 among the 50 greatest drummers in the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome_Brailey

### Genre Suggestions

- **Psychedelic Funk (current, strongly supported).** The book's own definition explicitly names George Clinton's Parliament-Funkadelic universe as the center of this genre, and Mothership Connection is its founding text: alter egos (Star Child, Lollipop Man), an extraterrestrial concept, and the "Mothership" mythology. Keep this.
- **Classic Funk (secondary case).** 1975 sits inside the 1969–1977 classic-funk window; the track was a top-15 pop hit on a major label, with the song's "primary identity" being funk rather than a fusion with rock or jazz. A defensible second choice, but Psychedelic Funk is more specific.
- **Funk Rock (weak).** The Mothership Connection Wikipedia article lists "funk rock" among its broader genre tags. The song itself has rock energy in the breakdown but the rock framing isn't load-bearing. Not recommended.
- **Recommendation:** keep Psychedelic Funk.

### Region Suggestions

- **US - Michigan (current, set).** The album was recorded primarily at United Sound Systems in Detroit; the Parliament/Funkadelic operation was Detroit-based throughout its Casablanca peak. This matches the choice made for `Parliament - Dr. Funkenstein`. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mothership_Connection
- **US - Northeast.** The Parliaments formed in Plainfield, New Jersey, in 1955, and George Clinton's career through the early 1960s was a Plainfield/New Jersey story. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Clinton_(funk_musician)
- **US - California.** Mothership Connection was also recorded in part at Hollywood Sound in Hollywood, CA; Casablanca Records itself was a Los Angeles-based label. A real but secondary case. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mothership_Connection
- **Recommendation:** keep `US - Michigan` for consistency with the other Parliament entry and because Detroit/United Sound was the dominant recording base for this album.
