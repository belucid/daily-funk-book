Today’s song, “Cool Ade,” is a collaboration across generations. Jazz veterans Johnny Otis (producer, piano, vibes) and Preston Love (saxophone) bring the gravitas, while Otis’ teenage son Shuggie Otis makes his recorded debut on guitar and bass at just 14. Shuggie also earned writing credits on the album.

Preston Love’s career stretched from lead alto sax with Count Basie in the 1940s, through leading Motown sessions on the West Coast in the 1960s, to cutting this funky instrumental with a teenage guitar prodigy. The track appears on an album built around food-themed titles, a celebration of Black Midwestern food culture.

This song is barely two minutes long. Notice how it builds a complete groove, peaks, and gets out without a wasted second. What would a longer version add, or would it just dilute the punch?

### Open Research Questions

**Q: What instrument(s) did Preston Love play on this track? Who did the vocals?**

Preston Love is credited with alto saxophone and flute on the album (AllMusic credits: https://www.allmusic.com/album/omaha-bar-b-q-mw0000015093). Ace Records describes his playing as a "blues-drenched soul alto," which refers to his saxophone tone, not his voice (https://www.acerecords.co.uk/omaha-bar-b-q). Note that Dusty Groove erroneously describes him as playing "tenor saxophone," but all other credible sources (AllMusic, Discogs, Ace Records) consistently credit him with alto.

As for the vocals on Cool Ade: Dusty Groove's review specifically singles out this track as having "a hip call-response vocal that's mighty nice," making it one of the few non-instrumental cuts on the album (https://www.dustygroove.com/item/723747/). However, no source identifies the vocalist(s) by name. The credited personnel are Preston Love (alto sax, flute), Shuggie Otis (guitar, electric bass), Johnny Otis (piano, vibraphone), and Clifford Solomon (tenor sax). The vocals are likely performed by band members (possibly Love himself, or Johnny Otis, or uncredited session vocalists), but this could not be confirmed from available sources. The Ace Records page mentions the band that played on this album "comprise pretty much the band (augmented by horns) that played the 1970 Monterey Pop Festival," suggesting there may be additional uncredited players beyond the four named in AllMusic's credits.

**Q: If Shuggie was born in 1953 and was 14, that means this would have been recorded in 1968 at the latest. The release date is 1970 (and note the recording was 1969). 1969, 14, and Shuggie's birth date don't add up.**

You're right that the math doesn't work for 1969. Here's the breakdown:

Shuggie was born November 30, 1953 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuggie_Otis). His age by year:
- In 1968 (before Nov 30): **14 years old**
- In 1968 (after Nov 30): 15 years old
- In 1969 (before Nov 30): 15 years old
- In 1969 (after Nov 30): 16 years old

The key finding: **AllMusic lists the recording date as 1968, not 1969** (https://www.allmusic.com/album/omaha-bar-b-q-mw0000015093). If the album was recorded in 1968 before Shuggie's November birthday, the "14 years old" claim from Ace Records is accurate. This also aligns with the timeline: Johnny Otis's previous album *Cold Shot!* (also featuring Shuggie on guitar) was released in 1968 on Kent (https://www.discogs.com/release/12711932), so the Omaha Bar-B-Q sessions likely followed shortly after, still in 1968.

Supporting evidence: Al Kooper's *Kooper Session* (1969) is described as featuring "the then-fifteen-year-old Otis" (https://jazzrocksoul.com/artists/shuggie-otis/), which is consistent with a November 1953 birth and a 1969 recording. If Shuggie was 15 in 1969, he was 14 in 1968.

**Resolution:** The album was most likely recorded in 1968, released in 1969 or 1970. The "14 years old" claim is accurate for a 1968 recording session. The entry body text says "recorded debut" for Shuggie, but this isn't strictly accurate: he had already appeared on *Cold Shot!* (1968). The author may want to say "one of his earliest recordings" instead. Also, the entry text says "two generations" but the writing theme says "three generations," and three is more accurate (Love born 1921, Johnny Otis born 1921 but a generation ahead culturally as producer/bandleader, Shuggie born 1953).

3. **"Two minutes of proof"** — At 2:02, Cool Ade is a case study in economy. No fat, no filler. The track makes its argument for funk as a tight, self-contained thing, closer to a 45 single than an album jam. This is the opposite of jazz-funk's long-form improvisation, and that tension (given the jazz pedigree of the players) is interesting.

#### Album

#### Artist
- Preston Love joined the Count Basie Orchestra in 1943 after Basie's lead alto player Earle Warren fell ill at Omaha's Dreamland Ballroom. Warren himself recommended Love as his substitute, and Love's sound was so close to Warren's that Basie hired him on the spot. — Source: https://davidbristow.com/articles/swingin-with-preston-love/
- Love played on Count Basie's only #1 hit, "Open the Door Richard" (1947). — Source: https://richielove.com/preston-love-sr-tribute/
- Love became Motown's first West Coast house bandleader/contractor, playing and touring with The Four Tops, The Temptations, Tammi Terrell, Marvin Gaye, Gladys Knight, The Jackson 5, and others. — Source: https://richielove.com/preston-love-sr-tribute/
- He appeared in Clint Eastwood's film *Play Misty for Me* (1971). — Source: https://richielove.com/preston-love-sr-tribute/
- Love co-launched Spin Records in 1952 and recorded for Federal and King labels. — Source: https://richielove.com/preston-love-sr-tribute/
- He published his autobiography *A Thousand Honey Creeks Later: My Life in Music from Basie to Motown, and Beyond* in 1997 (Wesleyan University Press), with a foreword by Johnny Otis. The title references Honey Creek, Iowa, where a 15-year-old Love made his musical debut. — Source: https://www.amazon.com/Thousand-Honey-Creeks-Later-Motown_and/dp/081956320X
- His sons Richie and Norman Love are both accomplished saxophonists, and his daughter Portia Love is a vocalist. — Source: https://richielove.com/preston-love-sr-tribute/
- Love was a civil rights activist in Los Angeles, organizing protests and demonstrations. — Source: https://omahafreedomfestival.com/the-blog/from-omaha-to-hollywood-the-incredible-journey-of-musician-preston-love-sr/
- In the 1930s-40s, Omaha was the central booking hub for jazz in the upper plains region. Love recalled it as "the booking center for the biggest dance territory in the world." — Source: https://davidbristow.com/articles/swingin-with-preston-love/
- Tenor saxophonist Clifford Solomon, who plays on this album, later became Ray Charles's musical director from 1974 to 1987. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifford_Solomon

### Genre Suggestions

The `genre` field is currently set to `Classic Funk`.

1. **Classic Funk** — The strongest fit. This is a short, punchy, groove-driven track built on a tight rhythm section with saxophone leads. It sits squarely in the late-1960s funk tradition: horn-driven, rhythm-first, no extended solos. The Dusty Groove description calls the album tracks "funky 45 mode" material. The album's 1968 recording date puts it right at the start of the classic funk era (1968-1977 as defined in the book). — Source: https://www.dustygroove.com/item/723747/Preston-Love:Preston-Love-s-Omaha-Bar-B-Q-180-gram-pressing

2. **Deep Funk** — A strong secondary case. The track is obscure, collectible (original 7" singles are rare), and built for the dancefloor in a raw, unpolished way. The album's collector market status, the single release on a small subsidiary label, and the delayed recognition through crate-digging all align with the deep funk definition ("less a sound than a commercial fate... tracks that were overlooked on release and only found their audience later"). — Source: https://www.acerecords.co.uk/omaha-bar-b-q

3. **Soul Jazz** — A weaker but defensible case. Love's background is big band jazz, and the presence of vibraphone and the album's bluesy, organ-less small-group feel has some affinity with the soul jazz tradition. However, the rhythm is more firmly funk than soul jazz's earlier swing-influenced pocket.
