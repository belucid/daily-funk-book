Billy Preston has one of the best claims to be the true fifth Beatle. He played with them at their last live performance together, the rooftop concert at Apple on January 30, 1969. He also played organ on late-era sessions for *Abbey Road* and *Let It Be*, and he's the only outside musician to be named on a Beatles single: “Get Back” and “Don’t Let Me Down” are by “The Beatles with Billy Preston.”

Preston first crossed paths with the Beatles in Hamburg while he was touring with Little Richard. Years later, George Harrison produced Preston’s album *That’s the Way God Planned It*, and it became the first non-Beatles release on Apple. From there, Harrison brought him into the Beatles’ London orbit. Preston was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 2021, with Ringo Starr handling the announcement.

Today’s song is Preston’s cover of Bob Dylan’s “It’s Alright (I’m Only Bleeding)” from 1965’s *Bringing It All Back Home*. Dylan’s original was basically a one-take live studio performance. Preston's track is more arranged and cinematic, with horn and string arrangements by Motown's Paul Riser.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

1. **"The man who played with everybody"**: Preston backed Little Richard at 16, played organ on the Beatles' rooftop concert, toured with the Rolling Stones for years, and co-wrote "You Are So Beautiful." This track, a Dylan cover reimagined as orchestral funk, is another example of Preston absorbing someone else's material and making it completely his own. The theme is transformation: what happens when a once-in-a-generation sideman steps to the front?

2. **"Gospel darkness"**: The Jive Time Records review describes this cover as "orchestral-funk darkness evoking David Axelrod" and calls it "one of the most inventive Dylan covers extant." Preston takes Dylan's bitter acoustic protest poem and runs it through gospel conviction and horn-and-string arrangements. The theme is the collision of gospel feeling with Dylan's cynicism: what does belief sound like when the lyrics are about disillusionment?

3. **"A church kid plays everything"**: Preston started playing organ in church at age three, was backing Mahalia Jackson by ten, and appeared on TV with Nat King Cole. This album, *Everybody Likes Some Kind of Music*, is his most genre-scattered record: soul, gospel, country, classical, funk, psychedelia. The track could anchor a theme about a musician whose church roots gave him the confidence to try anything.

### Listening Mission Suggestions

1. "Dylan's original is a solo acoustic rant, seven minutes of bitter poetry. Preston cuts it nearly in half and buries the words under horns and strings. What survives the translation, the anger or the melody?"

2. "Preston was a gospel musician first. Listen for the moment where his vocal delivery shifts from singing Dylan's words to preaching them. Does the song change meaning when it sounds like a sermon?"

3. "The arrangement evokes David Axelrod's orchestral funk. Focus on Paul Riser's horn and string charts: are they decorating the song, or replacing Dylan's guitar as the emotional engine?"

### Genre Suggestions

1. **Classic Funk** (current value): A strong fit. The track features a funk groove with orchestral arrangements, and it comes from Preston's 1972-1974 peak when he was one of the biggest funk-adjacent hitmakers in America. Preston's clavinet and keyboard style are foundational to the classic funk sound. The album charted #3 R&B. This is funk released through the mainstream pipeline on a major label during the core 1968-1977 window. — Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billy_Preston / https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everybody_Likes_Some_Kind_of_Music

2. **Psychedelic Funk**: The Jive Time Records review notes the track has "orchestral-funk darkness evoking David Axelrod," and the album's broader aesthetic includes psych-soul elements (the track "Listen to the Wind" is described as evoking Rotary Connection). If the arrangement leans heavily into surreal or atmospheric territory, this could be a secondary consideration. — Source: https://jivetimerecords.com/2022/01/billy-preston-everybody-likes-some-kind-of-music-am-1973/

3. **Jazz-Funk**: Preston's keyboard work on this album draws from jazz and soul jazz traditions, and the horn/string arrangements by Paul Riser (best known for his Motown work) give the track a sophistication that edges toward jazz-funk. However, Preston is not primarily a jazz musician, so this is the weakest of the three options. — Source: https://www.sessiondays.com/2021/05/1973-billy-preston-everybody-likes-some-kind-of-music/
