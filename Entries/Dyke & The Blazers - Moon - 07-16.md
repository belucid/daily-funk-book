The Apollo 11 moon landing on July 16, 1969 may have been "one giant leap for mankind," but many Black Americans felt excluded from the accomplishment. They asked if the nation had the right priorities in pursuing the moon over problems here at home.

Gil Scott-Heron’s spoken word poem set to music, "Whitey on the Moon" (1970), is the most famous Black musical response to the moon landing, and is a worthy listen if you've never heard it. Today's "Moon" is another musical response that emphasizes exclusion, and is considered one of Arlester "Dyke" Christian’s best tracks.

"Moon" was not an obvious single, and Dyke's early death (see March 13) meant there was never a new album, so this was not released during Dyke’s lifetime. It first appeared on deep funk compilations nearly 40 years after it was recorded.

### Writing Theme Suggestions

The current writing theme is: **"Black America’s perception of the moon landing"**

This is strong. Here are some alternatives or refinements:

1. **"Funk before the flag was planted"** — Frame the entry around timing. "Moon" was likely recorded in the same months as the Apollo 11 landing. While most of America watched the TV, Dyke was laying down a six-minute groove that channeled a completely different reaction to the same event. It belongs to a cluster of socially conscious Dyke tracks ("Black Boy," "Runaway People," "My Sisters’ And My Brothers’ Day Is Comin’") that show a side of the band their hit singles didn’t. The entry becomes about what labels chose not to release and why.

3. **"Whitey on the Moon, Dyke on the groove"** — Pair this track directly with Gil Scott-Heron’s spoken word poem "Whitey on the Moon" (1970), which became the most famous Black artistic response to the moon landing. Both emerged from the same cultural moment. The contrast: Scott-Heron used words, Dyke used the groove itself.

### Interest and Surprise

#### Track
- "Moon" was never released as a single during Dyke’s lifetime. It first appeared on the 2007 Ace Records compilation *We Got More Soul: The Ultimate Broadway Funk*, compiled by Alec Palao, nearly 40 years after it was recorded. — Source: https://www.acerecords.co.uk/we-got-more-soul
- Liner notes by Alec Palao describe "Moon" as one of the "superlative numbers" where "Dyke’s socially conscious, street-hip side comes through loud and clear." — Source: https://www.acerecords.co.uk/we-got-more-soul
- The 2021 Craft Recordings reissue presents "Moon" as a "previously unreleased new stereo mix," suggesting the 2007 Ace version may have been a different mix. — Source: https://craftrecordings.com/products/dyke-the-blazers-i-got-a-message-hollywood-1968-1970-lp
- At 6:13, "Moon" is one of Dyke’s longest recordings. Their hit singles were typically 2-3 minutes.

#### Album
- *I Got A Message: Hollywood 1968-1970* contains 16 previously unreleased recordings out of 21 total tracks, remastered by Dave Cooley at Elysian Masters. — Source: https://craftrecordings.com/products/dyke-the-blazers-i-got-a-message-hollywood-1968-1970-lp
- The Hollywood sessions were recorded at Original Sound Studios (7120 West Sunset Boulevard, Los Angeles), a studio opened by DJ Art Laboe in 1959. — Source: https://rockandrollroadmap.com/places/studios-and-labels/los-angeles-area-studios-and-labels/original-sound/
- During the Hollywood period, Dyke recorded with members of Charles Wright & the Watts 103rd Street Rhythm Band as backing musicians. — Source: https://jasthomas.com/dyke-the-blazers/

#### Artist
- Arlester "Dyke" Christian was shot and killed on March 13, 1971 in Phoenix at age 27, joining the "27 Club." A man named Clarence Daniels was arrested but the killing was ruled self-defense. An autopsy showed no alcohol or drugs in Christian’s system. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyke_and_the_Blazers
- At the time of his death, Dyke was about to launch a UK tour and had lined up an album project with Barry White. — Source: https://www.phoenixnewtimes.com/music/dyke-and-the-blazers-phoenix-arlester-christian-11565102/
- Two future members of Earth, Wind & Fire played with Dyke & The Blazers: guitarist Al McKay and guitarist Roland Bautista. Drummer James Gadson later became Bill Withers’ go-to drummer. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyke_and_the_Blazers
- "Funky Broadway" (1967) is often cited as the first record to use the word "funky" in its title. Some radio stations banned it, considering the word offensive. Wilson Pickett’s cover became a bigger hit. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyke_and_the_Blazers
- Rick James called Dyke & The Blazers’ music "revolutionary." — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyke_and_the_Blazers
- The band formed when members of Carl LaRue’s Buffalo group were invited to Phoenix by DJ Eddie O’Jay to back The O’Jays. After LaRue left, they merged with a local group called The Three Blazers. — Source: https://jasthomas.com/dyke-the-blazers/, https://www.phoenixnewtimes.com/music/dyke-and-the-blazers-phoenix-arlester-christian-11565102/
- "Let a Woman Be a Woman, Let a Man Be a Man" was sampled by Tupac Shakur and DJ Shadow, among others. — Source: https://www.whosampled.com/Dyke-&-the-Blazers/Let-a-Woman-Be-a-Woman-Let-a-Man-Be-a-Man/

**Context: Black America and the Moon Landing**
- On July 15, 1969, the day before the Apollo 11 launch, about 500 mostly African American protesters led by Ralph Abernathy (head of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference) arrived at the gates of Kennedy Space Center with two mules and a wooden wagon to dramatize the contrast between the space program and poverty. — Source: https://www.history.com/articles/apollo-11-moon-landing-launch-protests
- Gil Scott-Heron’s spoken word poem "Whitey on the Moon" was released in 1970 on his debut album *Small Talk at 125th and Lenox*. It became the most famous Black artistic response to the Apollo program, contrasting medical debt and poverty with space spending. Scott-Heron said he was inspired by Eldridge Cleaver’s argument that the space program was meant to distract from domestic problems. — Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitey_on_the_Moon
- A 1969 poll found that the majority of Black Americans opposed the space program’s funding priorities. The New York Amsterdam News editorialized against the spending. — Background knowledge, but no source found.

### Genre Suggestions

The current genre is: **Deep Funk**

1. **Deep Funk** (keep) — This is the strongest fit. "Moon" was never released as a single, sat unreleased for nearly 40 years, and only surfaced through a crate-digging-oriented compilation on Ace Records. This is the textbook definition of deep funk per the book’s genre framework: "tracks that were overlooked on release and only found their audience later through crate-digging, DJs, and dance floors."
   Source: Genre definition in *On Genres & Regions.md*

2. **Classic Funk** — Dyke & The Blazers are undeniably a classic funk act (1965-1971, major label distribution, chart hits, concert tours). The band fits the classic era perfectly. However, "Moon" specifically was never part of that commercial pipeline, which makes Deep Funk the better label for this particular track.

3. **P-Funk / Psychedelic Funk** — At 6+ minutes, if "Moon" has any psychedelic or trippy qualities in its groove (hard to confirm without detailed listening), this could be a secondary consideration. But without evidence of psychedelic elements, this is speculative.
